Rumi and the Masnavi: Stories of Love, Loss and the Search Inside — full script

Cold open

Settle in, and let the day go quiet. We begin in a city, on a cold afternoon, at a place where two streets meet.

The city is Konya, in the high country of Anatolia, and the year is twelve forty four. Late in the autumn, a man is riding home through the market on a mule. His name is Jalaluddin, and he is about thirty seven years old. He is a teacher of religious law, and he has a title that people use with respect.

Around him walk his students. Some carry his books. Some hurry to keep close, hoping to ask a question before he reaches his door. This is how his days have gone for years. He speaks, and people write down what he says.

The old accounts say it happened at a crossing where the market streets met, near the sellers of sugar. The date later tradition gives is the end of November.

A stranger steps out of the crowd. He is plainly dressed, the way a traveller is, and nobody knows him. He takes hold of the mule's bridle. The students stiffen, because no one stops the teacher in the street like this.

Then the stranger asks a question. One famous version, told by a biographer who wrote decades after the event, has him ask which man was greater, the Prophet Muhammad or the mystic Bayazid of Bistam.

It sounds like a schoolroom puzzle. A scholar might answer it in a breath. But the question was not really about rank, and the teacher felt this at once. The stranger was asking what knowledge is for. Is it a thing you hold, or a thing that holds you?

In the old telling, the teacher, who had answers for everything, had none. Everything he had memorised went still inside him, like a room after the last guest has left.

Another story, from the same biographer, shows the stranger again. He finds the teacher sitting by a pool with his books around him. He asks what the books are. The teacher says that it is something he would not understand. The books go into the water. The teacher cries out in dismay. The stranger lifts them out, and they are dry.

You should hold such stories lightly. They were written down long afterwards by devoted followers, and they are told the way a family tells stories about a grandfather. A careful listener keeps two things apart. One is what we can say happened. The other is what people needed to say about it.

Still, something real lies underneath. A respected teacher met a stranger, and his life turned on that meeting. Out of the years that followed came one of the great poems of the world, six books of rhymed couplets, about twenty five thousand of them, full of stories that open into other stories.

We will take that poem slowly, a tale at a time, as you drift. But first we have to stand at that crossing a little longer, with the mule stopped, the students silent, and the cold light falling across the sugar sellers' stalls.

Because it matters who these two men were. Who was the teacher, and where had he come from? And who was the stranger, with his hand on the bridle?

From Balkh to Konya

On a Friday morning in the first years of the thirteenth century, the mosque at Balkh is filling. The city lies in the north of what is now Afghanistan, on a plain with the mountains behind it. A preacher named Baha al Din Walad climbs the steps of the pulpit, and the crowd settles to listen. Near the back sits a small boy, his son, who has been brought along and told to be quiet. The boy is called Muhammad Jalaluddin. The world will know him by another name.

The traditional date of his birth is the thirtieth of September, twelve oh seven, and the traditional place is Balkh. Some modern scholars think he was born in a smaller town called Wakhsh, in what is now Tajikistan, and that the family moved to Balkh while he was an infant. The old sources do not settle it, and we will not pretend that they do.

His name tells a story of its own. Jalaluddin means the glory of the faith. The name Rumi means "of Rome," and it came later, from the land where he spent his life. Muslims of that age called Anatolia, the western part of modern Turkey, the land of Rum, because it had so long been Roman and then Byzantine ground. So the man from the far east of the Persian world is remembered by a name from the far west of it.

His father was no ordinary preacher. Baha al Din was a jurist and a theologian, and his admirers gave him the title Sultan of the Scholars. He also had an inward side. He kept a collection of sermons and spiritual reflections, which his family later called the Maarif, and the book shows a man who spoke of God with an almost startling closeness. Rumi would read it for the rest of his life, and its echoes can be heard in his poems.

Now the story turns to leaving. Why did the family go? The best known answer comes from Aflaki, a follower of Rumi's descendants who wrote about a century later. He says Baha al Din fell out with the ruler of the region, the Khwarazmshah, and with a famous theologian at his court named Fakhr al Din Razi. It makes a good tale, and it may even be partly true. But Aflaki was writing long after the events, to honour a family, and modern scholars treat the quarrel with caution.

The other reason is the one we all know. Out of the east, a new power was coming. Genghis Khan's Mongol armies crossed into the Khwarazmian lands in twelve nineteen and twelve twenty, and the great cities of the region fell one after another. Balkh suffered in those years. The usual account says that the family left before the worst came, sometime between twelve ten and twelve twenty, though the exact year is not known. What is certain is that they did not leave for a few weeks. They left for good, and they would never see Balkh again.

They went west, slowly. One of the best loved stories of the road is set in Nishapur, where the elderly poet Attar is said to have met the family. Aflaki tells us that Attar looked at the boy walking behind his father and gave him a copy of his poem, the Book of Secrets. He is supposed to have said that this child would one day set fire to the burning hearts of the world. It is a lovely scene. It is also a legend, told long after the fact, and some historians doubt the two ever met. Hold it lightly, the way you would hold a bird.

Then came the long journey itself. The family is said to have passed through Baghdad and on to Mecca for the pilgrimage, and then north through Damascus into Anatolia. They stopped in small towns, Malatya, Erzincan, Akshehir, and finally Larende, which we now call Karaman. They were refugees, but not poor ones, because a famous preacher and his household were welcome in most places.

In Larende, the boy grew into a man. Around twelve twenty four, when he was about seventeen, Rumi married a young woman named Gawhar Khatun, the daughter of a man from Samarkand. Their first son, Sultan Walad, was born there in twelve twenty six. This son matters a great deal to our story, because he would later write the first biography of his father in verse, and he would found the order that carried his name forward.

Around twelve twenty eight, a summons came from Konya. The Seljuk sultan, Alaeddin Kayqubad, invited Baha al Din to settle in his capital. So the family arrived at last in the city where the rest of this story takes place. This is the point in our story where we have the boy from Balkh, now a young husband and father, finally standing in Konya.

Konya in those years was a splendid place. It was the capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, and the sultan had filled it with mosques, schools and gardens. Scholars, merchants, craftsmen and refugees from the Mongol lands crowded its streets, speaking Persian, Arabic, Turkish and Greek. It was a city that had learned to be many things at once, and Rumi would later write in the language of the court, Persian, with Arabic and the occasional Turkish and Greek word dropped in.

Baha al Din did not live long to enjoy it. He died in twelve thirty one, and his son, who was twenty four, took over his teaching post. Think of that. A young man, grieving, sits in his father's place in front of his father's students, and begins to explain the law of God to a room that remembers a greater voice.

Help soon arrived from the old country. A man named Burhan al Din Muhaqqiq, from Termez, who had been a pupil of Baha al Din, reached Konya about twelve thirty two. He found his old master dead and the master's son in his place. He saw that the young man knew a great deal about God, and had not yet been made to feel the thing he knew. According to the tradition, he set Rumi to a strict inner training, with long spells of fasting and solitary retreat, forty days at a time. Rumi also went to Aleppo and Damascus for a time, to study in the great schools there. Burhan al Din died in the early twelve forties, and Rumi carried on without a guide.

By then he was well known. He taught in a religious school, preached on Fridays and gave legal opinions to those who asked for them. Students filled his lectures. Respectable people greeted him in the street. And he had not yet written a line of poetry that anyone remembers. The man who would one day be called the greatest mystical poet of the Persian language was, at this stage of his life, a distinguished and rather serious teacher of law.

Meanwhile the world kept shifting under his feet. In twelve forty three, the Mongols defeated the Seljuk army at Köse Dağ, and from then on the sultans of Konya ruled by the permission of the Mongol khans. The city that had been a refuge now lived in the shadow of the same power that had driven the family from Balkh. Rumi had run from the Mongols as a child. Now he lived under them as a master of a school.

So here is the man we have been following. He is somewhere near the age of thirty five. He has a wife, sons, students, a good name and a settled life. He seems to have everything that a scholar should want. And yet something in him is still waiting, and does not yet know what for.

Then, one winter day in twelve forty four, a stranger arrived in Konya, dressed in rough black felt, with no pupils and no reputation, and asked to see him. Everything the teacher knew was about to go quiet. And that brings us to the question the next part must answer. Who was the stranger?

Shams of Tabriz

In the autumn of twelve forty four, a man on a mule rode through the streets of Konya, and the people who passed him barely looked twice. He was a tall, thin stranger in a coarse felt robe, and he had come a long way. His name was Shams al-Din, and he was from Tabriz, in the north west of what is now Iran.

He had spent years on the road. The stories say he travelled from town to town, lodging in the cells of caravanserais, taking odd work, and speaking to almost no one. He was known as Shams the Bird, because he never stayed anywhere long.

By this time Rumi was about thirty seven years old, and he was no wanderer. He taught law and religion in Konya, and he had pupils, a pulpit and a good name. In the story you have been following, he is the respectable scholar at the centre of a settled life.

Now we come to the meeting itself, and here the sources do not agree. Aflaki, who wrote the most famous collection of stories about Rumi and his circle, tells several versions. In one, Rumi was riding through the town, surrounded by his students, when the stranger stepped out and took hold of his reins.

The stranger asked him a question. In Aflaki's telling, it was about the great saint Bayazid and the Prophet Muhammad, and which of the two was the greater. Bayazid had said, "Glory to me, how great is my majesty," while the Prophet had said, "We have not known thee as thou shouldst be known."

Rumi is said to have answered that the Prophet was greater, because Bayazid had been satisfied with one mouthful, while the Prophet's thirst was never quenched. And the story says that Shams cried out, and Rumi fell to the ground.

Hold that story loosely. Aflaki wrote about seventy years after the event, collecting what the community remembered, and he loved a miracle. Other versions put the meeting in a street of the sugar sellers, or in a schoolroom, or beside a well. The details move. What does not move is the shape of it: a famous teacher met a stranger, and his life changed.

What do we know about Shams himself? Not very much. We have a book of his sayings, put together from notes and memories, called the Maqalat, and in it he speaks in a voice that is blunt, restless and sometimes rude. He had no patience for scholars who collected learning the way a merchant collects goods.

He is also said to have asked his teachers, over and over, for a companion who could bear his company. The tale goes that he prayed to be shown someone able to endure him, and the answer came, go to Rum, the old name for Anatolia.

So he came, and he found the teacher. What happened next was plain enough from outside. Rumi stopped teaching. He and Shams shut themselves away in a room, and they stayed there for weeks, perhaps for months. Aflaki says that no one was allowed in, except for one man who brought them food.

We do not know what they said to each other. The sources say only that they talked, and that the talk went on day and night. Whatever it was, Rumi later wrote that it was not an exchange of lessons. It was closer to a long recognition, as though the stranger had been holding a mirror and the scholar saw himself in it for the first time.

Meanwhile Konya was watching, and it was not pleased. The students had lost their teacher. Rumi's classes stood empty, the pulpit was unused, and the man who had once taught them law now spent his days with a barefoot drifter.

Think of how this would look from the benches. A respected man of thirty seven abandons his position, and the cause is a stranger nobody can place. The students called Shams a sorcerer and a fraud, and some of them are said to have spoken openly against him.

The sources also record that Rumi began to change in other ways. He took up music and poetry, and listened to the reed and the drum. Before Shams, he had been a preacher and jurist, and there is no sign he had written verse. After Shams, he began to compose, and the poems came in a flood.

The pressure grew all the same. Aflaki tells us that Shams was not unaware of the anger around him. After about a year or so, he left, quietly, without a word, and went to Damascus.

The effect on Rumi was immediate and severe. The sources describe him as stricken, unable to teach, wandering the streets, and refusing comfort. He wrote poems of longing that are some of the saddest ever written in Persian, and he sent word to everyone he could that Shams must be found.

His son, Sultan Walad, was sent to Damascus. He was by now a young man, about twenty, and he carried a letter in verse from his father. Sultan Walad found Shams and begged him to return, and he is said to have walked beside him on foot, all the way back to Konya, as a sign of respect.

So Shams came back, and the old joy returned with him. But the old trouble returned too. The same people who had resented him before were still there, and there was a new bitterness among them, now that they had seen how deeply Rumi had suffered.

We are now at the saddest part of the story, and it is also the least certain. After some time, Shams vanished for the second time. This time he did not go to Damascus, and no letter came, and no one could say where he had gone. The date is usually given as about twelve forty seven.

Several explanations were later offered. The most dramatic, repeated by Aflaki and by later writers, is that Shams was murdered by a group of jealous men, perhaps including one of Rumi's own sons, and that his body was thrown into a well. The tale even gives the name of the son, Ala al-Din.

But we should be careful. Aflaki does not clearly say that Shams was murdered. He reports the rumour, among other things, and treats it with a certain reserve. Sultan Walad, who was there and wrote about the events himself, was more guarded still. Historians today call the death of Shams unproven, and the well a story that grew in the telling.

What is certain is the effect on Rumi. He searched again, even travelling to Damascus himself, and the story goes that somewhere on that road he understood something, and the search turned inward. He is said to have said that he himself was Shams, and that there was no need to look any further.

Think of the man at the end of this part. He is still a scholar by training, and still a father and a teacher in a Turkish city. But he has lost the one person who woke him, and he does not know where that person is. The grief is real, and it is huge, and it has nowhere to go.

So here is the question the rest of the story has to answer. A man has been broken open by love, and then left alone with the opening. What did the loss become?

The Reed Flute

It is night in Konya, some time in the early twelve sixties, and a man named Husam al-Din Chalabi is sitting with a pen, a sheaf of paper and a lamp. He has been close to Rumi for years now, since the days after Shams was gone. Tonight he has come with a request, and it is a modest one. He would like a book.

The tradition says Husam al-Din asked for a long poem in rhyming couplets, in the manner of the older poets Sana'i and Attar, so that the friends who gathered around Rumi would have something to read besides the great lyric poems. The story goes that Rumi smiled, reached into his turban, and drew out a folded paper. On it were the first eighteen lines of what would become the Masnavi.

You should know that this story comes from Aflaki, who wrote decades after Rumi died, and who loved his subject too much to be a cold witness. It may be exactly true, or it may be a tidy shape that memory gave to a beginning. What does not change is the poem itself. Those eighteen lines are still there, at the head of the first book, and they have been read aloud, sung and memorised for more than seven centuries.

The Masnavi grows from them to six books and roughly twenty-five thousand couplets. Everything that follows, the fables, the jokes, the arguments with God, hangs from this small opening. So tonight we stay with it, slowly, one line at a time, the way you might walk once around a house before you go inside.

In Persian, the first words are two: bishnav az nay. Listen to the reed. It is a command, but a soft one, the kind you give to someone who is already half turned toward you. And the reed in question is the nay, the end-blown flute of the Middle East, a simple hollow cane with finger holes, and no keys, no strings, nothing but breath and the space inside.

Reynold Nicholson, the Cambridge scholar whose edition and translation of the whole Masnavi appeared between nineteen twenty-five and nineteen forty, begins his English like this: "Listen to the reed how it tells a tale, complaining of separations." Nicholson was a careful, literal translator, and his English is a little stiff, but the sense is plain. The reed tells a story, and the story is a complaint, and the complaint is about being parted.

Parted from what? From the reed bed. Picture it. A cane grows in a marsh, among thousands of its own kind, rooted in wet earth, swaying together in the wind. Then someone cuts it. Someone hollows it out, and burns holes along its length. It is no longer a plant. It is an instrument, and what it plays is the sound of the place it was torn from.

The second line says this directly. Since it was cut from the reed bed, the reed says, its lament has made men and women weep. See that nobody asked the reed to cry. The crying is simply what happens when breath passes through something that has been emptied.

The third line tells us what the reed wants. It wants a heart torn open by separation, so that it can explain the pain of longing to someone who already carries the same wound. It does not want sympathy from the comfortable. It wants a listener who has lost something.

Then comes the line that many readers take to be the centre of the whole passage. Nicholson gives it as "Every one who is left far from his source wishes back the time when he was united with it." Read it twice. It does not say every lover, or every exile, or every pilgrim. It says every one. The reed's sorrow is turned, in a single sentence, into the condition of anyone who has ever felt that they come from somewhere they cannot quite get back to.

You are listening to the story of the reed's flute in the middle of the night, and where we are in the larger story is this. Rumi has lost his father's world in Balkh, and then his teacher and friend Shams in Konya, and he is now, in his fifties, dictating the poem that will carry what he made of those losses. The reed is where he begins.

The next lines turn to the reed's loneliness among people. It has sung in every kind of company, the reed says, among the unhappy and among the glad. Yet each person became its friend according to their own opinion, and no one sought out the secret hidden inside it. That is a hard thing to say about an audience, and Rumi says it gently. People hear in the reed what they already believe.

And yet the secret is not far from the cry, the reed says. It is only that ears and eyes lack the light to see it. The following line makes a comparison that is worth hearing slowly: the body is not veiled from the soul, nor the soul from the body, and still no one is allowed to see the soul. The two are as close as two things can be, and one of them remains invisible. The sound of the flute is a little like that. You hear the breath, you never see it.

Then the poem grows hot. Nicholson gives it as "This noise of the reed is fire, it is not wind." The reed is not making music with air alone. Whatever is in it is burning. And whoever lacks this fire, the next words say, may he be nothing. It is a fierce line in a quiet poem, and it is meant to be. Rumi is saying that longing is not an ornament to the spiritual life. It is the engine of it.

Several lines now widen the picture. The fire of love is in the reed, and the fervour of love is in the wine, he says. The reed is the companion of anyone who has been parted from a friend, and its tune tears our veils apart. Then comes a question that has outlived its century: who has ever seen a poison and an antidote like the reed, or a sympathiser and a longing lover like it? The sound hurts, and the sound heals. It opens the wound and it is the only comfort offered.

A word on the next line, because it adds a name. The reed, he says, tells of the way that is full of blood, and recounts the stories of the love of Majnun. Majnun is the lover in the old Arabian tale of Layla, the young man whose desire drove him out of his tribe and into the wilderness, where he wandered among the animals, reciting poems to a woman he could not reach. Every Persian listener knew him. His name means, quite simply, mad. By placing him in the second half of the passage, Rumi tells you that this is a poem about love that unmakes a person, and not about gentle feeling.

After this the mood changes. The reed says that only the senseless are told this sense, and that the tongue has no customer save the ear. Here, "senseless" is a paradox, as it so often is in Sufi writing. It means those who have let go of ordinary cleverness. And the line about the tongue is a plain observation about all speech: it is worth only what the listener will buy. The poet needs you, as much as you need the poem.

The final lines fall softly. Our days have grown untimely in our woe, he says, and they travel hand in hand with burning griefs. But if the days go, let them go, it is no matter, so long as You remain, for none is as pure as You. The word You, in Nicholson's English, has a capital letter, because he reads it as God. The poem has turned from the reed to the one the reed is crying toward, and it does so without announcement.

Then a pair of images. Whoever is not a fish, the reed says, becomes sated with the water, and whoever has no daily bread finds the day long. A fish cannot be bored of water because it has never been anywhere else. Those who are at home in their source do not understand the restlessness of those who have left it. And the very last line makes the same point about teaching: none that is raw can understand the state of the ripe, so the words must be brief, and then, in Nicholson's rendering, a single word of leave-taking, farewell.

It is a remarkable way to begin a book of this size. There is no argument yet, no tale, no teaching in the usual sense, only one voice complaining across a distance, and then going quiet.

Commentators, from the early centuries onward, have read the reed as the human soul, and the reed bed as its divine origin, and the cutting as the soul's entry into the world. Nicholson himself understood it this way in his notes. Many readers, in Rumi's own time and since, have also heard Shams in it, the friend whose absence taught the poet to sing. That reading is natural, but it is a reading, and the poem never names him.

What the reed gives you, if you lie still and let it, is a way of hearing the next ninety thousand words. Each story that follows is a cry from some place of loss, and each is looking for an ear. But how does Rumi hold so many stories at once, one inside another, without the thread breaking? How are the stories built?

Stories Inside Stories

Some years after the loss of Shams, a man is sitting in a room in Konya. It is evening, and a lamp is burning low. A man sits on a cushion with his back against the wall, and around him are a few friends and disciples. Among them is a younger man named Husam al-Din Chelebi, who has a pen and a stack of paper ready. The older man is Rumi, and he begins to speak in verse, and the younger man begins to write.

That is how the Masnavi came to be, as far as the tradition tells it. It is a long poem in Persian, in six books, and it was not planned out like a building. It was spoken, night after night, to people sitting close by. And to understand how it works, it helps to keep that room in mind. This is a voice talking to listeners, and a good talker knows how to hold a room.

You are in the fourth part of the story, and here we are looking at the machinery of the poem: how a tale begins, how it opens into another tale, and how the lesson arrives. First, the form. The Masnavi is written in rhyming couplets, and the word masnavi itself names that form, a poem in which each couplet carries its own rhyme. That gives the poem a wonderful freedom. Because each pair of lines rhymes within itself, the poet never has to hunt for a rhyme that has run out. He can go on as long as he likes, and Rumi did, for about twenty-five thousand couplets.

Now the habit that surprises new readers. Rumi starts a story, and then, in the middle of it, he stops. Something in the tale reminds him of another tale, or of a saying, or of a point he wants to make. So he leaves the first story hanging, and he begins the second. Sometimes the second holds a third inside it. The listener is carried down through the layers, like a person descending a staircase in a house with many floors.

Scholars sometimes describe this as nesting. A story sits inside a story, the way one bowl sits inside another. And the remarkable thing is that Rumi nearly always comes back. He climbs the stairs again, closes each tale in turn, and returns to the first story exactly where he left it. The listener, who may have forgotten where the poem began, finds it waiting.

Why would a teacher work this way? Part of the answer is in the room we began with. A man speaking in the evening to friends is watching their faces. He sees a look drift, or a question forming, and he answers it with a story. The poem is a long conversation, and conversations wander.

There is an old story told about how Husam first asked for the work. He is said to have gone to Rumi with the idea that the master should compose a book in the manner of the earlier Sufi poets, who had written long didactic poems. Rumi is said to have taken from his turban a sheet of paper on which the opening eighteen lines were already written, the lines of the reed flute. The report comes from a later source, and it is the kind of story that grows lovely in the retelling, so it is wise to hold it lightly. What can be said more safely is that Husam was the one who wrote down the verses, and that Rumi named him as the inspiration of the book.

So what does a typical stretch of the poem look like? Take the pattern as the poem itself describes it. Rumi tells a tale about ordinary people: a merchant, a king, a cook, a donkey, a madman. The tale is vivid and often funny. Then he turns to the listener and draws out what the tale means. He does not leave the meaning to chance. In one famous passage he says, in Nicholson's English, that the stories are like the husk and the lesson is like the kernel. The tale is how you carry the meaning, and the meaning is what you came for.

Here is something worth noticing, because it is part of why the book has lasted. The tales are not all solemn. Rumi uses jokes, and some of them are quite earthy. There are stories about bodily humour, about greed, about foolish husbands and sharp-tongued wives, told with a grin. Later readers, and some translators, have been shy about this. But the plain fact is that the poem moves between the very high and the very low, often in the same page. A story about a quarrel over grapes sits close to a passage about the soul's return to God.

Why mix them? One answer is that Rumi believed the sacred was not kept in a separate house. The ordinary world, with all its mess and comedy, was where the teaching had to happen. A listener who laughs is a listener who is awake and leaning in. And then the turn comes, and the laugh opens into something deeper.

Consider the sources of the stories. Rumi did not invent most of them. He drew on a wide stream of tales that had been told for centuries across the Persian and Arabic worlds. He took from the Qur'an and its stories of the prophets. He took from the sayings of Muhammad. He took from the lives of earlier Sufis, from Persian poets who came before him, and from fables of animal wisdom that travelled from India through Persia into Arabic. Then he reworked what he took. The same old tale, in his hands, would bend toward a new point.

This is a good moment to name how he handles the Qur'an in particular, because it is everywhere in the poem and it shapes the nesting. Often a story begins from a Qur'anic scene, and Rumi lets it open into a long reflection that goes well beyond the original. Nicholson, who spent years on the English translation and a commentary, noted how thickly the verses of the Qur'an and the traditions are woven through the Masnavi. A reader who knows them hears a second voice under the first.

Now think about the audience again, because it explains the looseness. The people in that Konya room were not strangers to this world. Many were craftsmen, merchants, and townspeople who had followed Rumi as a teacher. They did not need every term explained, and they did not need a tidy argument. They needed stories they could remember, carry home, and turn over in the night. Rumi, who had been a trained jurist and preacher before he became a poet, knew exactly how a story sticks.

There is also a point about pace. Because the poem was dictated, and because Husam wrote it down as it came, it has the rhythm of a spoken thing. It repeats. It circles back. It will say a thing, then say it again from a new side. A reader in a hurry may find this slow. But a listener, lying quietly with eyes closed, may find it is exactly right. The poem does not rush you toward a conclusion. It lets you sit with each image until it has done its work.

The tradition adds that Rumi would sometimes dictate in a kind of absorbed state, even while walking or turning. We should be careful here, since these pictures come mostly from the biographer Aflaki, writing decades after Rumi died, and Aflaki loves a marvel. Aflaki is a rich source, and a late one, and he tells wonders as readily as facts. So take the image of the walking poet as a portrait of how his followers remembered him, rather than as a record taken on the spot.

One more feature of the grammar is worth naming. Rumi often steps out of a story to speak directly to the reader, or to scold himself, or to say he has talked too long. He will break off with a line like a man clearing his throat. In the long middle of the poem he sometimes says that he is being pulled along by the tale, and that the real subject is just beyond what words can hold. This gives the poem its intimate tone. You do not feel lectured. You feel accompanied by someone who is still working it out as he goes.

So the pattern holds across the six books. A fable begins. A joke or a parable opens inside it. A saying from the Prophet or a verse from scripture is set down beside it. The teacher steps in, names the lesson, and sometimes admits that words cannot finish the job. Then the first story is picked up again, and the poem moves on.

The stories are the poem's heart, and some of them have travelled much farther than the book itself. Many people who have never opened the Masnavi know a few of its tales without knowing where they came from. A blind man and an elephant. A merchant and a parrot in a cage. So the question we turn to next is a simple one. Which are the best-known tales, and what is each one really saying?

The Elephant in the Dark

Evening in Konya, and a lamp has been lit. Husam al-Din Chelebi sits with a reed pen and a stack of paper on his knee, and a few paces away the poet is walking up and down, speaking. Nothing has been prepared. The verses come the way water comes from a spring, couplet after couplet, and the scribe's only task is to keep up.

Tonight the poet is speaking of an elephant. It is in the third of the six books, and it is one of the best loved stories in the whole Masnavi. You have heard how the poem builds its tales one inside another. This one stands almost alone. It is short, and it hardly needs the frame. It simply begins.

An elephant, the poem says, was being kept in a dark house. Some Indians had brought it there to be shown. Word had spread, and people came to see it, one after another, and each of them went into the darkness.

Now picture the room. There is no window, and no lamp. You can hear the animal breathing, and you can smell straw and dust and something warm and enormous. You cannot see it. The poem says that since seeing with the eye was impossible, each visitor felt the creature with the palm of his hand.

One man's hand fell on the trunk. He said that the elephant was like a water pipe. Another touched an ear, and to him it seemed like a fan. Another laid his hand on a leg and said that the animal was shaped like a pillar. And another stroked the back and said, truly, that the elephant was like a throne.

Notice how fair each of them is. Not one of them lies. The trunk really is like a pipe, and the back really is broad as a seat of honour. Every man reports honestly what his hand has met. The trouble is not in what they say, but in how much they think they have said.

The poem gives that in a single plain sentence. Whenever anyone heard a description of the elephant, he understood it only in terms of the part that he himself had touched. So the accounts went different ways, and the poem adds a small, quiet detail. One man gave the creature the name of one Persian letter, and another the name of a different one, the dal and the alif. If you know the script, you know why that is funny. One letter is a bent little curve, and the other is a single straight stroke. Two honest men, one animal, and two shapes that could hardly be less alike.

Then comes the line that holds the whole tale. If each of them had held a candle in his hand, the poem says, the difference would have gone out of their words. Hold on to that, because it is easy to lose. The cure for their quarrel is not that they should stop speaking, or that they should split the difference and agree that an elephant is half a fan and half a pillar. The cure is light.

This is the middle of the story, so let us stop here a moment. We are in the third book of the Masnavi, in a dark room in a story told in Konya, with a scribe writing and a poet speaking, and we are about to ask where the tale came from.

For Rumi did not invent it. He knew it, as his listeners probably did, and he took it up the way a good storyteller takes up a story already worn smooth by many hands.

The oldest version we can point to is Indian, and it is Buddhist. In a short text of the Pali collection called the Udana, the Buddha tells of a king who has a number of men, all blind from birth, brought together and shown an elephant. One touches the head, one an ear, one a tusk, one the trunk, and so on, and each is then asked what an elephant is like. They answer by comparing it with a pot, a winnowing basket, a ploughshare and other things of that kind. Then they fall to quarrelling, and in that text they come to blows, while the king looks on and laughs.

The Buddha does not tell it to be funny. He tells it about teachers and wanderers of his own day who clung each to his own view and called the others fools. The point there is a warning against dispute. People hold a part, and take it for the whole, and then fight over it.

How old is that? The Pali collection was, by tradition, first written down in Sri Lanka in the first century before the common era, though the teachings themselves are held to be older. So the tale had a long road to travel before it reached Konya. And how it travelled, which merchants or monks or storytellers carried it west, nobody can tell us. The road is not documented. We have only the two ends of it, and the story is recognisably the same at both.

There is, however, a nearer stepping stone, and it brings us back to the Persian poets whom Rumi loved. Sanai of Ghazna, who wrote in the first half of the twelfth century, tells a version in his long poem the Hadiqat al-haqiqa, the Walled Garden of Truth. Sanai died roughly a hundred years before Rumi's best years, though the exact year is argued about. In his telling, as I will describe it in the poem's own spirit, the people are blind, in a land where an elephant is a stranger, and each gives a different report of what it is.

Rumi had the deepest respect for Sanai, and for Attar too. Verses credited to him say that Attar was the spirit and Sanai the two eyes, and that he himself came after them both. I would hold that attribution lightly, but the feeling in it is certain from everything else he wrote. He knew these poets, and he was answering them.

And look at what he changed. In Sanai the men are blind, and blindness cannot be helped by anything the men do. In Rumi the people can see perfectly well. They are simply standing in the dark. That small change opens a door. Blindness is a verdict. Darkness is a circumstance, and a circumstance may change. A candle can be carried in.

Now, you may know this story in another form. Many English speakers meet it first in a comic poem by the American writer John Godfrey Saxe, published in eighteen seventy two, in which six blind men of Indostan feel the elephant and each decides it is a wall, a snake, a spear, a tree, a fan or a rope. Its last lines say that each of them was partly in the right, and all of them were in the wrong. That is a good joke, and a true thought. But it is not quite the thought of the Masnavi.

Because Rumi does not stop at the quarrel. Right after the tale he turns the meaning over, and says in effect that the eye of the senses is no more than the palm of the hand, and the palm has no power to reach the whole of the animal. Then he sets one image beside another. There is the foam on the sea, and there is the sea itself. Let the foam go, he says, and look with the eye of the sea. The foam is what moves and shows and catches the light. The sea is what is underneath it.

Some people today read the elephant as saying that every view is as good as every other, that all the groping hands are equally right, so let everyone rest. It is a gentle reading, and a common one. But it is not what the verses say. The verses say that the hands touch only parts, that quarrels come from taking the part for the whole, and that what ends them is not agreement among the blind but a light that someone carries.

Whose light? Back in that dark room, you will notice that the poem does not ask the visitors to feel harder, or to compare notes more cleverly. A hand, however skilled, stays a hand. If it is a candle that is needed, then the question becomes where such a candle is to be found, and who could possibly hold it.

Husam al-Din lifts the pen. In the lamplight the poet has stopped walking. The senses, he has just said, are only the palm. So what is it that sees the sea? What about the heart?

The Merchant and the Parrot

In a market town, some years before anyone now living, a merchant stands beside a cage and weighs a question he has not yet put to anyone. The cage holds a parrot, and the parrot is the pride of his house. This is the scene Rumi gives us in the first book of the Masnavi, and it is where this part begins.

The merchant is about to leave on a long trading journey to India. Before he goes, he does what a generous man does. He asks each of his servants and slaves what gift they would like him to bring home. Each of them names something, and he promises to remember. Then he turns to the parrot, which is a fine talker and a sweet singer, and he asks the same question.

The parrot has a request, but it is not for a gift. It asks the merchant, when he reaches the land where the parrots of its own kind live, to look for them. He is to tell them that a parrot of theirs is shut in a cage, longing for them, and to ask what advice they have for a friend in such a state. The merchant agrees. It seems a small thing to carry, a few words for a bird.

Keep the shape of that request in mind. The parrot does not ask to be freed. It asks only to send word, and to hear back. Rumi lets this simple, almost humble errand carry a great deal of weight, as we shall see.

The merchant arrives in India. Somewhere in the countryside he sees a number of parrots perched in the trees beside the road. He stops and calls out the message he was given, and he tells them of his parrot in the cage. Hardly has he finished speaking when one of the wild parrots trembles, falls from its branch, and lies still on the ground. It is dead.

The merchant is horrified. He is a decent man, and he is stricken with regret. He blames himself bitterly for having spoken. He tells himself that he has killed an innocent creature with his news, and that perhaps this was a relative of his own bird, or a friend, or a king among them. He wishes he had held his tongue.

This is a fair place to stop and say where we are. We are in the Masnavi, in its first book, in a tale Rumi tells to a company of listeners, and we are following a merchant who has just delivered a message he now wishes he had never carried. The story is not finished, and the sorrow in it is not the end.

The merchant completes his business. He buys the gifts his servants asked for, and he sets out for home. He is a man with one thing on his mind. When he arrives, each servant receives the present that was promised. The parrot, in its cage, asks the question the merchant has been dreading: what did the parrots of India say?

The merchant resists. He says he wishes he had never told them. He says he is sorry, and that he is ashamed of what the message brought about. The parrot presses him, and at last he tells the story as it happened: how he gave the message, and how one of the parrots there shook, fell down, and died.

At that, the parrot in the cage does the same. It trembles, drops from its perch, and lies lifeless on the floor of the cage.

Now the merchant's grief changes. It is no longer the grief of regret for a stranger. It is the grief of a man who has lost his own beloved companion, and he knows he is to blame. He weeps. He tears at his clothes. He reproaches himself in the plainest terms. Nicholson, in his English, renders the merchant's cry in the voice of a man who has lost everything he loved in one stroke. And so he does the one thing left to him. He opens the door of the cage, lifts the small body out, and lays it outside.

At once the parrot rises into the air. It flies up to the branch of a tree nearby. It was never dead. It had only played at death, and it had learned that play from the parrot in India.

Here is the heart of the tale, and here Rumi slows down. The merchant looks up at his bird in wonder and asks how this can be, and why it did what it did. The parrot answers him. The wild parrot, it explains, was not killed by the message. Its fall was the message. When the merchant said a captive bird sent its greetings, the free parrot understood what it was being told. It was being shown how to escape a cage. To get out, one must become as though dead.

Consider the cleverness of that. The caged parrot had asked for advice, and the advice came back in the only form that could pass. Not in words. In an act. The free bird showed by its own body what the captive needed to do, and the captive understood it perfectly.

What does the parrot mean by this? It means that the way out of the cage of the self is a kind of dying before death. The bird must stop clinging to its life of display and comfort, the sweet voice and the praise and the fine house, and become still and let go. Then the door can open. Rumi does not leave this to guesswork. He has the parrot speak it as counsel, and the counsel is plain: die before you die, and so be free.

That saying has a history outside the poem. Tradition ascribes to the Prophet Muhammad the words, die before you die. It is a hadith that scholars of that tradition have treated with caution, and it is quoted far more often in Sufi writing than it is relied upon as a firmly authenticated report. What can be said with confidence is that the idea ran through the Sufi world that Rumi lived in, and that the parrot tale is one of its liveliest homes.

The parrot also says something about the merchant himself, a gentle word, and a sad one. The merchant had been a good keeper, but a keeper all the same. His kindness had not set the bird free. It had only made the cage pleasant. Rumi does not scold him. He lets the merchant be what he is, a man who loved his bird and did not know how.

The tale does not end in triumph. When the parrot has said what it has to say, it bids the merchant farewell. The merchant, now old in his understanding, answers it kindly. He says, go in safety, you have shown me a road. And he decides that he will follow it. The bird flies off. The cage stands empty. The reader is left with the empty cage, and with a question that is not about parrots.

There is one more thing to say about how this story came to Rumi. The tale of a bird that feigns death to be set free is older than he is, and it appears in the wider world of Persian and Indian storytelling that fed his poetry. He did not invent every strand of it. He took what was already circulating among tellers and made it his own, adding the merchant's sorrow and the parrot's last counsel, so that an animal fable became a lesson in letting go.

And notice that the parrot is not a stranger. Rumi uses the parrot elsewhere in the poem as a figure for a talker who repeats what he has heard, a voice with no depth behind it. That is the cage in another sense. The parrot that only repeats is imprisoned by repetition. The parrot in this tale is the other kind, the one that listens, understands, and acts.

So there the story rests. A merchant, a cage, a message carried across the sea, a death that was not a death, and a bird on a branch in the open air. It asks the listener to think about what they are holding on to, and what it would mean to put it down.

And it leaves one practical question hanging in the air, quiet and unanswered, for the next part to take up. If a person is to die before dying, to go still, to let the cage open, then what did that look like? What did the practice look like, in the hands and the days of the people who tried it?

The Turning

In the year twelve hundred and seventy three, in the city of Konya, a long procession followed a coffin through the streets. People of several faiths walked behind it, and the sound of mourning went on for days. The man being carried had been a teacher, a preacher and a poet, and he had been a living presence in that city for nearly fifty years. Tonight the story turns to what he left behind, and in particular to the turning itself.

Before the turning, though, you should hold one fact steady. The ceremony most people picture when they hear the name Rumi, the white robes, the tall hats, the slow spinning to music, is not something he left behind in that form. Much of it took shape after him.

So we begin with what was his. The sama was a gathering for listening. It could mean poetry, music, movement, and a kind of surrender to all three. Sufis of that age argued about it, and some scholars thought it close to forbidden. Rumi belonged to the party that welcomed it.

Think of him in Konya, a respected jurist and teacher with a turban and a long robe, a man who had once sat above students on a high platform. Then came the years with Shams of Tabriz, which the story has already told. Afterward, the reports say, the grave scholar changed. He began to listen to the reed and the drum, and sometimes he rose and moved.

The oldest account of this is a long book of anecdotes, the Acts of the Adepts, written by Aflaki. Aflaki was a follower of the later Mevlevi circle, and he finished his work around the year thirteen hundred and fifty-three, some eighty years after Rumi died. It is full of wonders, and the honest listener should treat it as devotion, not as a court record.

One of its famous tales tells of a goldsmith named Salah al-Din Zarkub. Rumi, it says, was walking through the bazaar when he heard the rhythmic hammering of the goldbeaters, and the sound moved him so deeply that he began to turn right there in the street.

The story may be true in its bones or it may be a tale told to explain the rest. It is lovely either way. It says that the turning began as something unplanned, a body answering a sound. It was no rehearsed rite.

Rumi himself speaks of the sama in his poems, and there the matter is clearer. In his lyric poems, the great collection called the Divan, he writes of dancing and of music as a way toward union with the beloved. These verses are certainly his. They come from the living man, in his own hand of language, and they are full of movement.

The Masnavi is a different book. It is a long poem in rhyming couplets, and it was dictated over many years to his devoted companion Husam al-Din Chelebi. The Masnavi talks about music too, and it names the human voice and the reed as carriers of something beyond themselves. But it is a book of stories and teaching, and it does not lay out the rules of a ceremony.

Here we reach a quiet but important boundary. In his lifetime, Rumi had companions, students and admirers. He had a circle. What he did not have, by most accounts, was a formal order with fixed rules, titled officers and a standard uniform. That came later.

When Rumi died, the leadership of his circle passed first to Husam al-Din, who had written down the Masnavi, and after Husam al-Din died, to Rumi's son, Sultan Walad. Sultan Walad is the figure who gave the community its lasting shape.

Sultan Walad was a poet too, writing in Persian and also in Turkish and Greek, a sign of how mixed the Konya of his day was. He organised the followers, and from his time the group began to be known as the Mevleviyya, from the title Mevlana, meaning our master, which people used for Rumi.

Rumi's tomb became the centre. A tomb tower was built over his grave in Konya soon after his death, and a distinctive ribbed dome rose above it, glazed in green-blue tile. It still stands, and it has been the heart of the order ever since.

From there the order spread. Over the following centuries it moved through the lands of the Ottomans, and lodges were founded in many towns. Istanbul, Cairo, Damascus and Aleppo all had Mevlevi houses at some point. Poets, musicians and calligraphers gathered in them.

Slowly the sama became a formal service. Many accounts credit a later head of the order, Pir Adil Chelebi, with fixing its ritual form around the early fifteen hundreds, though the exact stages of its development are not well recorded. Be wary of any telling that gives you a single clean date.

So think now of the ceremony as it came to be, and you can lay it out like a quiet room. A group of dervishes enters wearing a dark cloak over a white robe. The cloak stands for the grave, the tall felt hat for a tombstone, the white skirt for a shroud. These meanings are the tradition's own explanation, and they come from later writers.

The shaikh stands at a place called the post. The dancers walk slowly round the hall in a procession, bowing to one another. Then they drop their cloaks, which means leaving the worldly self behind, and they begin to turn.

The right arm lifts with the palm to the sky. The left arm lowers with the palm to the earth. The tradition says that what is received from above passes through the dancer to the ground, so that the turner is only a channel. The dancer does not keep it.

Each turn follows the others around the hall, as the planets follow their courses, and the foot pushes round a point as the body spins on the left heel. It is a skill learned through long training. The traditional training period, as later sources describe it, was one thousand and one days, spent in the lodge doing humble tasks.

The musicians sit apart. They play the ney, the reed flute, together with a kettledrum and sometimes a bowed string instrument. The reed is no accident. You will remember that the Masnavi opens with the reed, cut from its reed bed and crying for its home. The ceremony carries that first image into sound.

Here is where the story moves from lovely to sobering. In the nineteen twenties the new Turkish republic closed the dervish lodges. In nineteen twenty five a law banned the Sufi orders and shut their lodges, and the Mevlevi house in Konya was converted into a museum.

For some decades the whirling was kept alive in quiet and in exile. In the nineteen fifties Turkey began to allow the ceremony again in a limited way, as a cultural event in December, around the anniversary of Rumi's death, which the community calls his wedding night, meaning his meeting with God.

That phrase, wedding night, is worth a pause. It does not show a morbid streak. It shows how the community learned to hear its master. The turning is no performance for the sake of beauty alone. It is a form of prayer, and it keeps alive a way of remembering a man who died long ago.

So you can see how the story sits. A man who loved music and movement, and who was shaped by loss, left poems and a circle of friends. His son shaped that circle into an order, and later generations gave it its robes and its rules. What we call the turning is partly his and partly theirs, a river made of many streams, and a careful listener learns to tell which is which.

And all of it, the poems, the dance, the order, reached the wider world through the hands of translators. Some of them were faithful scholars, some were poets, and some bent the words to their own taste. What did those later translators do?

Translation and Misquotation

In the early years of the twentieth century, a small study in Cambridge is lit by a single lamp. A scholar named Reynold Nicholson sits at a desk, bent over a Persian manuscript of the Masnavi, and beside him lies a stack of paper that is slowly growing. He is a quiet man, and he is about to spend a good part of his working life on one poem.

Nicholson was a professor of Persian at Cambridge. He published his edition of the Persian text of the Masnavi in eight volumes, between nineteen hundred and eight and nineteen hundred and twenty-five, and then turned to the English translation and commentary, which appeared in the years up to nineteen hundred and forty. The whole undertaking took him something like three decades.

You are listening to the story of how a thirteenth-century Persian poem reached English ears, and how it changed along the way. Earlier we watched the whirling ceremony and the order that grew up after Rumi's death. Now we follow the poem across languages, and we look honestly at what is his and what is not.

Nicholson did not begin from nothing. Others had come first. A Victorian translator named James Redhouse had published a part of the first book of the Masnavi in eighteen hundred and eighty-one. A few years later, in eighteen hundred and eighty-seven, Edward Henry Whinfield issued an English version that condensed the whole six books into a single volume. Whinfield worked in rhyming couplets and he cut a great deal, and his book was the one most English readers could actually find for a generation.

Nicholson did something different. His translation is plain, literal and complete, set in prose, with a long commentary beside it explaining the references, the Quranic allusions and the Sufi vocabulary. He wanted the reader to see what the Persian actually said, and where it was obscure, he said so. It is a scholar's book. It is not an easy one to read in bed, and almost no one has ever read it from end to end.

Consider a line from his rendering of the opening of the poem, the reed flute we met earlier. Nicholson gives it as: "Hearken to this Reed forlorn, breathing, ever since 'twas torn from its rushy bed, a strain of impassioned love and pain." Mark how formal it sounds. The words are old, a little stiff, the grammar of a Cambridge study. It is accurate, and it keeps the shape of the thing, but it does not sound like a voice at a gathering in Konya.

Now we come to the second great channel through which Rumi reached the modern world. In the nineteen seventies and eighties an American poet named Coleman Barks began to produce versions of Rumi's verses. Barks did not read Persian. What he did was take earlier scholarly translations, many of them by the Cambridge scholar Arthur John Arberry and by Nicholson himself, and recast them as free verse in plain American speech.

Barks has been open about this. He has described his versions as interpretations, made from the literal translations of others. They are, if you like, a second step on the road. A scholar carries the poem from Persian to a careful English. A poet then carries it from careful English to something that sounds like talking.

And that second step is the reason Rumi became widely loved in the English-speaking world. Barks's books sold in very large numbers, and for many readers he became the Rumi they know. Many people met this poet first on a bookshop shelf, in lines that moved freely and spoke warmly.

But the free versions also took liberties, and you should hear what kind. Barks's renderings tend to leave out the Islamic frame of the poems, the Quranic references and the praise of God that run through the Persian. Some critics, among them scholars of Persian literature, have argued that this removes the religious ground the poems grew from. Others reply that the versions bring many readers to the poet who would never otherwise have come.

Neither side is wrong about the facts. The Masnavi is a deeply Islamic book. It quotes the Quran constantly, and it was written by a trained religious teacher for a circle of disciples. A translation can honour that, or it can quietly set it aside, and each choice has a cost. Nicholson kept it and lost the ease of speech. Barks gained the ease and lost much of the frame.

There were other renderings too. In the nineteen eighties and after, translators such as William Chittick, writing from within the scholarly study of Sufism, produced careful selections that explained Rumi's thought with real depth. More recently Jawid Mojaddedi has made a fresh complete English translation of the Masnavi for Oxford, in verse, which began to appear in two thousand and four. So there is more than one way into the poem now, and a patient reader can compare them.

Here the story turns to a harder matter, and it is a matter of honesty. Because Rumi is so beloved, a great many verses have been attached to his name that he never wrote.

You may have met some of them. They appear on posters, on greeting cards, in the signatures of emails. Among the most repeated is a line of the form: out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing there is a field, I will meet you there. This is among the best known of all the so-called Rumi lines in English. And it has a real source, in the sense that Barks published a version of it. But it grows out of a Persian poem in which the Persian words are much more modest and quite different in feeling, and scholars have noted how far the English has travelled from them.

Another famous line says that you were born with wings, so why prefer to crawl through life. It is often credited to Rumi. Yet careful searching has not turned up a clear Persian original for it in his collected works. That does not prove he never said anything like it, but it means the attribution cannot be shown.

And then there are the lines that are simply not his at all. A popular quatrain begins with the words that you are not a drop in the ocean, you are the entire ocean in a drop. This is repeated everywhere as Rumi, and no one has produced a source for it in his Persian. It seems to be a modern creation, polished by many hands until it sounded ancient.

Let us be careful about how much we claim, because the honest state of the evidence is a quiet one. We know that Rumi's own work comes down in two great bodies: the Masnavi, in six books, and the large collection of lyric poems known as the Divan, associated with Shams of Tabriz. The Persian text of these has been edited and studied for a very long time. When someone gives you a line and says Rumi wrote it, there is a simple test. Can anyone point to the Persian, to a book and a place within it? If they can, you have something to hold. If they cannot, you have a saying that has taken his name, perhaps kindly, perhaps carelessly.

There is nothing shameful in this, and it has happened to many great writers. The sayings of a beloved teacher drift together over the years, and the best ones find a famous owner. It happened in his own century, when later followers gathered stories about him into long books of marvels. We met one of these, the Acts of the Adepts by Aflaki, written decades after Rumi died and full of wonders that cannot be checked.

So the poet reaches us through many hands. Persian copyists first, then Cambridge professors with their stiff, faithful prose, then American poets with their warm free lines, and then, last, the long unsigned crowd of quoters who have given him sentences he never spoke. Some of what arrives is exact. Some is a loose likeness. Some is a stranger's wish dressed in his name.

And a patient listener can hold all of this without distress. The poem is still there, under the layers, in its Persian, six books long. The reed still cries in it. The elephant is still in the dark room, and the parrot is still in its cage.

Which brings us to the last question, the one that sits quietly at the end of every long journey like this one. After the scholars and the poets and the posters, after the whirling and the grief and the stories nested inside stories, what does he leave us.

The Quiet Lesson

In the winter of twelve seventy three, in the city of Konya, a man of about sixty-six lay ill in his house, and the people who loved him kept a quiet watch. He had been a teacher, a preacher, a poet, a friend of strangers. Tonight he is mostly a body in a bed, and around him the household moves softly, so as not to disturb what is happening.

You have come a long way with him. You have followed his family out of Balkh ahead of the Mongols, and sat with him in a schoolroom in Konya, and watched a stranger from Tabriz walk into his life and out again. You have listened to the reed, and the elephant, and the parrot in its cage. Now the story is nearly told, and there is only one thing left to ask. After all the tales, what is the teaching?

Start with the shape of the book itself. The Masnavi is a long poem in six books, in rhyming couplets, and tradition says it began when a devoted pupil named Husam al-Din Chalabi asked Rumi to compose something for the community, in the manner of the older mystical poems. Rumi is said to have taken a slip of paper from his turban with the opening lines already written. That story comes to us from Aflaki, who wrote about a lifetime after Rumi's death, and it is told with love, so you may hold it gently.

The poem opens with the reed flute, and the reed is the key to everything. Nicholson renders the first line as an instruction: "Listen to the reed, how it tells a tale, complaining of separations." The reed was cut from the reed bed, and every note it sings is the sound of that cutting. Rumi says that anyone who is left far from his source seeks the day he will return. That is the whole book, folded small. The soul is a thing cut from somewhere, and it hums with longing for home.

Now, this could be a sad teaching. It is not. Rumi does not tell his listeners to despair of the distance. He tells them the longing itself is the road. In the same opening passage the reed says that the fire in its voice is love, and that it is the fire of love that is in the reed. The ache is not a sign that something has gone wrong. The ache is the compass needle, turning.

So the first part of the quiet lesson is this: turn toward what hurts, because it points somewhere.

The second part is about the inside. Again and again the Masnavi sends the listener inward. There is a tale where a mystic calls to God and is told by his own doubt that God never answers, and in a dream he is shown that the very act of calling was the answer. The Nicholson translation puts it in the voice of the divine reply: your cry of "Allah" was itself my "Here am I." The point is plain and tender. The reaching is already the arriving. You do not climb a ladder to a distant roof. You uncover a room you were standing in.

The third part you have already met in the dark room, with the elephant. Some people touch the trunk and call it a water pipe, some touch the ear and call it a fan, some touch the leg and call it a pillar. Rumi says that if each of them had held a candle, the disagreement would have gone. The lesson is not that nothing is true. The lesson is that a single hand, however honest, holds only a piece, and the one who sees the whole has a different kind of light. It asks for humility at the exact moment you feel most sure.

The fourth part is the parrot, who learns that the way out of the cage is to die to the cage, to act as though it were lifeless and so be set free. Many readers take this as the heart of his sense of release: that some freedom comes only when you stop gripping the thing you thought was your life. See that Rumi does not frame it as a threat. He frames it as a mercy that looks like a loss.

Put the four together and a quiet figure appears. A soul, cut from its source, singing with longing. A longing that is already a kind of answer. A truth too large for any one hand. A freedom that comes through letting go. None of it is loud. None of it requires you to be clever. It asks you to listen, to soften, to stay curious, and to love.

And love is the word Rumi will not let go of. In the Masnavi he calls love the physician of all our ills, the remedy of our pride and our self-regard. He writes that through love bitter things become sweet, and copper becomes gold. He means it as practice rather than ornament. In the stories, the person who loves is the person who changes, and the person who only argues about love stays exactly as he was.

Back to the house in Konya now, where the evening is deepening.

Rumi died on the seventeenth of December, twelve seventy three, which in the Islamic calendar fell in Jumada al-Akhira of the year six hundred and seventy two. Accounts say there had been an earthquake in Konya in the days before, and that when his family wept, he comforted them, saying that the earth was hungry and wanted a sweet morsel, and that it would be a good mouthful. Aflaki records this, and again it comes with the warmth of a later disciple, so take it as the way his community remembered his temper at the end: unafraid, a little amused, steady.

His funeral, the tradition says, was enormous. Muslims, Christians and Jews are all said to have walked behind the bier, each in their own way grieving a man who had spoken to them as friends. The scale of the crowd is reported by Aflaki and cannot be checked, but the claim that people of different faiths mourned him fits what Rumi's own writing and teaching suggest about how he met the world.

He was laid to rest beside his father, Baha al-Din Walad, in Konya. Over the years a tomb rose there, with its famous fluted dome, and later the lodge of the order that bore his name. It stands today and is visited by people of many languages. Those who come do not mostly come for an argument. They come the way you come to a quiet well, to stand a while.

One of the old verses attributed to the end of his life is often given as his last wish, and it is worth being honest about it. It is said that he asked his followers not to seek his tomb in the earth but to look for it in the hearts of the wise. Whether he said it in those words, no one can tell. But it is the sort of thing the poem itself would say, and it points the way the poem always points: away from the place, toward the person who listens.

Think now of the long lamp of his work, six books of tales within tales, left unfinished at his death. Tradition holds that the sixth book was the last he dictated, and that the poem simply stops, with no neat ending. A poem about longing that does not quite conclude. It may be an accident, and it may not. Either way, it leaves the listener where Rumi would want them: still on the road, still listening to the reed.

So what do you carry from all this into the night? Perhaps only this. Whatever hurts, point it toward home. Whatever you are certain of, hold with an open hand. Whatever you are gripping, ask whether it is a cage. And whoever is near you, love them a little more simply than you did this morning.

The candle in the dark room is the quiet kind. It does not shout the elephant into view. It only lets you see what was there all along, and lets you be less afraid of it.

Now the house in Konya is still. The watchers have gone to rest. The cold air outside settles over the roofs and the streets, over the courtyards, over the long road that leads away from the city in every direction.

The reed is silent for the night. The stories are folded away. You have nothing left to do, and nowhere left to go.

Let your breath be slow. Let your hands be heavy. Let the day, with everything in it, set itself down beside you, and be at rest.

Somewhere a flute is playing, very softly, very far away, and it is only the sound of coming home.
