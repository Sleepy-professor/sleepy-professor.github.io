Cold open

In a lamplit room in Rome, late in the evening, a man named Amelius sits close to his teacher. Amelius has asked before, and he is asking again. He wants a portrait of the older man, a painter or a sculptor to sit with him and keep his face for those who will come after. The teacher is Plotinus, and he says no.

It was not shyness, exactly, and it was not pride. His student Porphyry, who later wrote his life, begins the whole account with this very point. He says Plotinus seemed ashamed of being in a body.

Because of that, Porphyry tells us, Plotinus could not be drawn into talk about his parents, his homeland, or his people. Friends who asked got nothing. And when they pressed him to let a painter or a sculptor work from his face, he refused that too.

Amelius pressed him anyway, and the answer Porphyry records is worth hearing slowly. It was a question. Is it not enough, Plotinus asked, to carry about this image that nature has wrapped around us, without leaving behind a longer lasting image of the image, as though it were something worth looking at?

Hold that sentence for a moment. To him, the face was already a copy of something. A portrait would be a copy of a copy.

Amelius did not give up. He brought a friend, a painter named Carterius, to the lectures. Carterius looked closely, week after week, and drew from memory, and Amelius helped correct the likeness. So a portrait was made after all, without the man ever sitting for it.

Then there is the matter of his birthday. Plotinus would not say when it was. Porphyry explains that he did not want anyone to offer sacrifices or hold a feast in his honour. Yet he was no enemy of feasts. He kept the birthdays of Plato and of Socrates, with offerings and a meal, and his students gave speeches. Those were days for remembering what the mind had found, and not for celebrating a body arriving in the world.

This is the first thing to learn about him, and it will carry us through the night. Plotinus did not think a person was the face, the family, the city, or the date. He thought a person was something deeper and quieter, and that the work of a life was to turn toward it.

We are at the very beginning of a long story, in Rome, in the middle of the third century. You do not need to hold on to anything. Let the room fade, and let the lamp burn low. We will go back, and we will go slowly.

A man who would not tell his birthday, who would not sit still to be remembered, and who still left behind fifty-four treatises that people have been reading ever since. Who was this man?

A Student of Ammonius

Evening in Alexandria. A young man is walking slowly through the streets of a great harbour city, with the sea somewhere behind the buildings and the light going gold on the stone. He is nearly twenty eight years old, and he is unhappy. His name is Plotinus.

He has just come away from yet another teacher of philosophy, and he is carrying nothing but disappointment. Porphyry, the man who later wrote his life, tells us that at about that age Plotinus felt a sudden urge to study philosophy, and that he was pointed toward the most celebrated teachers in Alexandria. He came away from them downcast. He told his trouble to a friend, and the friend understood what he was really looking for, and led him to one more teacher, a man named Ammonius.

Plotinus went in and sat and listened. When he came out, he said to his friend that this was the man he had been seeking. That sentence is our first real glimpse of him. Everything else in this part, the years of study, the long road east, the arrival in Rome, grows out of that one afternoon.

But first, who was he, and where had he come from? Here the story begins with a small silence. Porphyry says that Plotinus would not speak of his race, his parents, or his native country. A later writer, Eunapius, says he was born at Lycopolis in Egypt, and that is the best answer we have, though it comes from a distance. Porphyry also tells us that Plotinus died in Campania in the second year of the emperor Claudius, at the age of sixty six. Counting back from that, he was born around the year two hundred and five. That is a calculation, and not a date he gave anyone.

So picture an Egyptian who lived inside the Roman Empire, spoke and wrote Greek, and carried a Latin name. It is a very third century combination. And Egypt never quite leaves his pages. In one of his later treatises, he reflects on how the Egyptian sages wrote. They did not use the letters of words and sentences, he says, but carved images, each one a single thing, so that the whole meaning showed at once. Hold that thought, because the idea of seeing a whole truth in a single glance will come back many times in this programme.

Now to Ammonius. It would be wonderful to tell you what he taught, and the honest answer is that we hardly know. No book of his survives. Porphyry reports that Ammonius's students agreed among themselves, in a kind of pact, not to publish his doctrines. Three of them are named: Herennius, another man called Origen, and Plotinus. A later tradition gives Ammonius the nickname Saccas, which is often explained as sack carrier, but that explanation comes from much later writers and is uncertain. And about the name Origen, there is an old puzzle. Eusebius, quoting Porphyry, mentions a Christian Origen who studied under Ammonius, and readers have long argued over whether this is the same man as the companion of Plotinus, or a second person of the same name.

So you should imagine a school without textbooks. A teacher speaks. Students listen, and think, and speak back. What they have afterward is memory, and a way of looking at things.

Plotinus stayed with Ammonius for eleven years. Eleven years is a long time to sit with one voice. Think of what that means for a man who started at twenty eight. He was not a boy picking up a trade. He was a grown man, and he gave his thirties to one question, and to one patient teacher who would not hand him an answer in writing. Whatever Ammonius gave him, Porphyry says, Plotinus made it his own. We will see in later parts how much of it was Plato, and how much was something nobody had quite said before.

Let us stop for a moment and see where we are. We are in Alexandria, in the middle of the third century, with a quiet student of nearly forty who has spent eleven years listening, and who is about to do something very surprising for a man of books.

Because Plotinus got restless. Porphyry says he felt a longing to learn about the philosophy practised among the Persians, and the philosophy of the Indians. He wanted to see the thing at its source, in the lands where he imagined wisdom might still be kept in living form. The chance came from a most unlikely direction. The emperor Gordian was preparing an expedition against the Persians, and Plotinus joined the army. He was thirty nine.

Try to hold that picture. A philosopher, who has never written a line, walking east with a Roman army, through dust and baggage carts and the noise of ten thousand men. He is hoping that at the end of the march there will be teachers who know something his own teacher did not.

It did not work out that way. Porphyry says Gordian was killed in Mesopotamia, and the campaign fell apart. Other accounts of that death differ. A Persian royal inscription describes a battle, while Roman writers tell of a plot inside the army, so the exact truth remains in dispute. What matters for our story is that Plotinus was suddenly a civilian in a broken army, in a hostile land, far from any lecture hall. Porphyry says he escaped only with difficulty, and made his way to Antioch.

Think about what that must have been like. The road east had been an act of hope, and it ended in flight. Whatever Persia or India might have taught him, he never found out. And yet, if you read the Enneads afterward, you do not find a man embittered by a wasted trip. You find a man turning, with great steadiness, back to the one place he was sure of, which was his own mind.

From Antioch, Plotinus went to Rome. He arrived in the first year of the emperor Philip, when he was forty. That is a small detail with a large meaning. At an age when many philosophers have already finished their careers, he was only beginning. The Rome he came to was an enormous city of marble and crowds, of senators and soldiers and traders from every province, and it is not the sort of place anyone imagines for a quiet teacher of inward things. But it was the centre of the world, and people there had the leisure and the curiosity to listen.

Here we meet the strangest fact of his whole life. Porphyry says that in Rome Plotinus held conferences with students, building on what he had heard from Ammonius, and that for ten years he wrote nothing at all. Ten years of speaking, and not one page. The pact with Ammonius was still holding him. Porphyry tells us it was Herennius who broke it first, then Origen, and only after them did Plotinus feel free. He began to write in the first year of the emperor Gallienus, when he was around forty nine.

Porphyry himself did not arrive until later. He says he came to Rome in the tenth year of Gallienus. That means everything we have heard so far, the unhappy walk through Alexandria, the eleven years, the road with the army, comes to us secondhand, most likely from what Plotinus told his students in later years. We should hold it with a light hand, and still be grateful for it, because it is the only portrait of these years that we have.

So the shape of the man is now clear. He was an Egyptian who would not name his birthplace, a late starter who listened for eleven years, a would-be traveller whose journey collapsed, and a teacher who spoke for a decade before he picked up a pen. He had been shaped, above all, by patience.

Now he is in Rome, in a room, among people who have come to hear him. The door is closed against the noise of the city. Someone asks a question. And the quiet man from Egypt begins to answer, and what he says in that room will take the rest of this programme to unfold.

The Teacher in Rome

The year is two hundred and forty-five or so, and in a house in Rome the street noise has begun to settle for the evening. A man in middle age sits in a room with a few students around him, and a question has been put to him, and he is turning it over slowly. His name is Plotinus. He has been in the city for about a year now, and what he is doing here, in a private house, is the beginning of a school.

You may remember where the story stands. Plotinus came out of Egypt, spent years with the teacher Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria, and joined the emperor's army on its way east, hoping to meet the sages of Persia and India. The campaign collapsed, and he made his way to Rome. Now, at about forty, he begins to teach.

He did not lecture the way we imagine philosophers lecturing. His student Porphyry tells us that the sessions were open conversations. People put questions, and Plotinus answered them. Sometimes this wandered. Porphyry says that a man named Thaumasius once complained that he wanted to hear a proper set speech, and that the way Plotinus ran things, there was too much talk of questions and answers. Plotinus did not change his habits for him.

What did the teaching look like? The students read the commentaries of earlier thinkers aloud, and then Plotinus would speak. Porphyry says he drew on the Platonists and on Aristotle, but that his own thought was not borrowed from them. One detail is lovely. When Plotinus was thinking, he seemed to think straight through a conversation without losing his thread. Porphyry reports that he could chat with someone, attend to every word, and then go back to his work without a pause, as if there had been no interruption.

Who came to hear him? A mixed company. There were doctors, and a rhetorician, and poets. Porphyry names a physician from Alexandria called Eustochius, who stayed with Plotinus until the end. There were also senators, men of the governing class in Rome, and this is where one of the most striking stories in the whole Life appears.

A senator named Rogatianus was drawn so deeply into the philosophy that he gave up his public life. Porphyry says he let go of his property, dismissed his servants, and resigned his office. On the day he was due to appear as a magistrate, with the officials waiting, he refused to go out at all. He gave away his house and freed his slaves, and kept nothing. He is said to have eaten only every other day, and his gout, which had made him a cripple and had to be carried about in a chair, was reported to have eased. Plotinus thought well of him, and praised him as a model for those who wish to be philosophers.

Hold that for a moment. A man of rank puts down his whole station because an idea has taken hold of him. We will come to what the idea was. For now, notice only that Plotinus was not a distant figure behind a closed door. He was a person who changed the lives of the people around him.

And he was gentle with them, in practical ways. Porphyry tells us that many men and women of good family, when they felt death approaching, brought their children, boys and girls, to Plotinus and left them in his care, along with their property. The house filled with young people. He became a guardian, and he kept the accounts. Porphyry says he looked after the money of these orphans with great honesty, and told their guardians to hand over every penny only when the child took up philosophy or came of age, and that he examined the accounts with great care. Here was a man who believed the body and its goods were not the true self, and yet he saw to it that a child's inheritance was counted correctly.

He also settled quarrels. Porphyry says that Plotinus was often chosen as an arbitrator in disputes, and that in all the years of this, he made no enemy among the people of Rome.

Then there is his health, which was poor. Porphyry says that toward the end of his life Plotinus suffered from a disease of the bowels at first, and that later his voice grew hoarse, his sight dimmed, and sores appeared on his hands and feet. Many scholars have guessed at the illness, and nobody can say for certain what it was. We should be honest about that. What Porphyry gives us is a description from a pupil's eyes, not a diagnosis.

As the illness grew, the school thinned. Porphyry says that Plotinus's friends began to shrink from him, because of the sores and the hoarse voice. He did not hold it against them. He is said to have left Rome and gone to Campania, to an estate that belonged to a friend who had died, and there he was supplied with what he needed from the estate, and from the friend's property. Eustochius was the one who stayed. In the last hours, Porphyry reports, Plotinus spoke to him and said that he had been waiting for him, and that he was trying to bring the divine in himself back to the divine in the universe. That is what Eustochius reports as his last words. We have them secondhand, and the Greek is a little difficult, so the exact wording is not secure. Still, it is the sentence people have remembered.

You are somewhere between Rome and that last room now, with the lamps low and the long years of teaching behind him. Something in all these years was being written down. Porphyry is the reason we have any of it.

Plotinus wrote nothing at first. For about ten years he taught by speaking, and only later began to put his thoughts on paper. He wrote in a hurry, and he did not like to read his work over. Porphyry says his handwriting was poor, his spelling uncertain, and that he would not look at a page again once it was finished, because his eyesight troubled him. The treatises circulated among his students, and some passed from hand to hand in a rough state.

When Porphyry joined the school, he began to collect them. He found fifty-four writings in all, and he did what no one had done: he sorted them. He divided them into six groups of nine, which is why the work is called the Enneads, from the Greek word for nine. Six times nine makes fifty-four, and the number pleased him, as he says himself. He took the treatises out of the order in which Plotinus had written them, and grouped them by subject, putting the plainer ethical matters first and the highest questions last. He also tidied the text, added headings, and wrote the biography that opens the whole thing.

This has a consequence worth keeping hold of. When we read the Enneads, we are reading Plotinus as Porphyry shaped him. The arrangement is the student's. A reader who wants to see how Plotinus's mind actually developed can look at the chronological order that Porphyry also preserved, and a good deal of modern scholarship does exactly that.

The nineteenth century gave us the English translations that this programme draws on. Thomas Taylor, who worked in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, produced a Plotinus in English, and G. R. S. Mead gathered his selections in eighteen ninety-five. Later, Kenneth Guthrie published his own translation in nineteen eighteen. Neither is the last word, and each has its habits and its blind spots. We will say which one we are quoting when we quote.

So the picture so far is this. A teacher in Rome, patient and generous, with a failing body and an open door. A scatter of treatises, rescued and ordered by a loyal student. Fifty-four pieces of writing, on many subjects, on the soul, on beauty, on fate, on time, on the good.

And now a question hangs over all of it, the way a question hangs over any large body of work. If you were to stand back from the fifty-four treatises, and ask what single idea they are all circling, what would it be?

The One

On an evening in the middle of the third century, the students of Plotinus are taking their places for the lecture. A lamp burns low. A man of about fifty sits among his students, and he is speaking quietly, almost as though thinking aloud. His name is Plotinus. The people around him are listening for a particular question, because they have heard him ask it before. He asks it again. What is it that makes a thing one thing?

Porphyry, who sat in rooms like this one for years, tells us that Plotinus taught by conversation and questions, and that his classes were open to discussion rather than fixed lectures. Out of those evenings came the treatises that Porphyry later arranged into the Enneads. And among them, again and again, the teaching comes back to one idea. That is where the story has arrived tonight. We have met the man, his teacher, his school and his students. Now we meet the thing he spent his life trying to describe.

Begin with an ordinary object. Take an army. An army is many soldiers, yet we call it one army. Take a chorus, a house, a flock. Plotinus points out that nothing is a thing at all unless it has some unity. In the treatise on the good, or the one, he says that all beings are beings by virtue of unity. Remove the unity from a chorus and you do not have a smaller chorus. You have scattered people. Remove it from a house and you have a heap of stones.

Now the argument begins to climb. Everything we can point to is made of parts, or has qualities, or can be described as this and also that. A living creature has a body and a soul. A thought has a thinker and something thought about. Even the simplest thing we can imagine has a being and a nature, a what and a that. Plotinus notices that anything made of parts depends on those parts. It is a composite, and a composite is not first. Something must have put it together, or something simpler must stand behind it.

Here is the move that gives the teaching its force. Suppose the simpler thing is itself made of parts. Then we must look behind that too. The chain cannot run backward forever, and it cannot end in something that is still many. So the source of everything must be what is not many at all. Plotinus names it the One. It is not one in the way a single stone is one among other stones. It is one in the sense that it is simple, without division, without a second thing inside it.

This is the point where his thought becomes strange, and where a listener may feel the ground change. For Plotinus, the One is not a being among beings. It is not even the highest being. It is beyond being. He says that it is not any thing, but prior to all things. In his sixth Ennead he writes that the One is not a being, but is before being. The reasoning is gentle but strict. To be something is to be this rather than that, to have a shape and a limit. But the source of every limited thing cannot itself be limited by being one particular kind of thing, or it would be only one of the things it was meant to explain.

So we come to a quiet difficulty. If the One is beyond every description, how do we speak of it? Plotinus is honest about this. He says that we say what it is not, rather than what it is. We even call it the One only because we must call it something. In the treatise on the good he says that the name One carries a denial of multiplicity. It points, but it does not capture. Listen to how he handles this. He does not offer a grand picture and hope you will be impressed. He keeps taking things away. It has no parts, so take away parts. It has no form, so take away form. It does not think the way a mind thinks, because thinking needs a thinker and an object, and that would be two.

Pause there a moment, and let the day's noise settle. Imagine a pool of water with no ripple on it, so still that it shows nothing, because there is nothing on its surface to show. This is only an image, and Plotinus knows images fall short. But he uses images constantly, because the mind needs something to lean against while it learns to let go.

His favourite is light. Think of the sun. The sun is one thing, a bright source, and from it light goes out in every direction. The light is not the sun, yet it comes from the sun and could not exist without it. And here is the important detail. The sun does not shrink by giving light. It does not choose to give it, or strain, or decide. It simply is what it is, and light flows from it. Plotinus uses this picture to show how the One can be the source of all things without being diminished and without acting like a craftsman at a bench. The source remains whole, and what comes from it is a kind of radiance.

He presses the image further. The light is brightest near the source and grows fainter with distance. Far enough away, it fades into darkness. In just this way, he suggests, the things that come from the One are more unified and more real the nearer they stand to it, and more scattered, more mixed, the farther they are. This is the quiet idea that will carry the rest of the programme. Reality is not flat. It has a direction, a slope, from the simple toward the many.

There is also something in this teaching that touches the listener directly. Because everything is unified by the One, you are too. Your own sense of being a single person, one self through a whole life of changing moods, is a small echo of the same unity. Plotinus says that the soul is drawn toward the One because the One is where it began. Not as a duty, but as a kind of homesickness. The Greek language he wrote in had a word for the Good, and he treats the One and the Good as the same, the thing that all things want without knowing that they want it. Every living thing, he says, reaches for its own good. And behind each particular good stands the one source of goodness.

Notice what this does not claim. Plotinus does not say that the One is a person who watches us, or a ruler who issues commands. He does not say it hears prayers in the usual sense. When he speaks of the One as the Good, he means the source toward which things naturally turn, not a judge sitting above them. The One does not turn toward the world. The world turns toward it. That is a very different picture from the gods of the temples and the stories of his time. It is, in a way, much quieter.

We should also be honest about what is disputed. Readers have long argued over whether Plotinus believed the One was truly beyond all thought, or only beyond the ordinary kind of thought. They have argued over whether the light image is a metaphor or something closer to a literal account. The Enneads were arranged by Porphyry, not by Plotinus himself, so the order we read them in was a student's choice. And the English of Thomas Taylor, who translated Plotinus in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, is full of his own devotion to the subject, so that he sometimes sounds more ornate than the Greek does. The later translation by Guthrie, published in the early twentieth century, reads more plainly. Where the two differ, the plainer wording often follows the Greek more closely. None of this changes the central idea, but it reminds us that we are listening across a very long distance.

Let us gather what we have carried so far, and let it rest. Whatever is, is one in some measure. Whatever is made of parts needs something simpler behind it. That simpler thing, followed upward, leads to what has no parts at all, the One, beyond being, beyond description, beyond the need to think. Everything else shines out from it as light shines from the sun, nearest bright, farthest dim. And the One loses nothing in the giving.

Porphyry tells us that Plotinus himself seemed, in his last years, to live very near this idea, speaking little about himself and always turning the conversation toward what was first. A man who would not sit for a portrait, who would not name his birthday, could hardly have said more about what he thought was real. The face does not matter. The date does not matter. What matters is the source.

But now a question stands open, and it is a good one, the kind he would have set before his students at the end of an evening. If the One is perfectly simple, perfectly complete, and wants nothing, then why is there anything else at all? How does a single source become a world?

Overflow

In Rome, in the house of a woman named Gemina, a man with weak eyes sits down to write, and Porphyry, one of his students, watches him do it. Plotinus does not stop to plan. He writes a line, and then the next, and the page fills as if he were copying from a book lying open in his mind. When he has finished, he does not read it over. His sight is too poor for rereading, so he hands the sheets on exactly as they came.

Porphyry did not say this was a picture of his teacher's philosophy. But you may find, as the night goes on, that it is one. Something full, and quite undisturbed, gives out what it has, and is no poorer for it.

This is the fourth part of our story. So far we have come to the One, which is beyond all the things that are, and we have seen it as a sun whose light reaches out without the sun being used up. Now we ask how that works. If the One lacks nothing and wants nothing, why is there anything else at all? Let your breathing slow, and let us take the question gently.

Plotinus answers it with a principle that sounds almost too simple. Whatever is complete, he says, produces something. A thing that has reached its fullness does not stay barren. He gives ordinary examples. Fire gives off heat, snow gives off cold, and a fragrant thing gives off its scent into the air around it. None of these is trying to. They are simply what they are, and something follows from it.

So the One, being the most complete of all, could not be the one thing that produced nothing. But notice what he refuses to say. He does not say the One decided, or planned, or leaned toward making a world. In his account the One is not moved, does not will, and does not incline. What comes after it comes without the One changing at all. The sun does not strain to shine. Plotinus uses just this picture, a radiance around the sun that streams out while the sun stays as it is.

His favourite word for the process is overflow. In the treatise on the origin and order of the things that come after the first, he says that the One, being perfect, as it were overflowed, and that its overflowing made something other than itself. The words "as it were" matter. He knows that overflowing is a picture borrowed from water, and he asks you to hold it loosely.

A small note on a big word. The English word usually given to all this is emanation, and you will meet it in every book about him. It comes from Latin, and it was settled later. Plotinus himself reaches for several images instead, and none of them is the single technical term. The tidy pattern of staying, going forth and returning was set out more formally by a later Neoplatonist, Proclus, about two centuries after Plotinus. The pattern is in Plotinus. The neat labels are mostly not.

Now the image the episode is named for. In his treatise on nature and contemplation, Plotinus asks you to picture a spring. This spring has no other source above it. It gives itself to all the rivers, and it is not used up by them. It remains quietly what it was. That last part is the heart of the matter. A spring that feeds a hundred streams is not a hundred times emptied. The giving does not subtract.

He goes on. Before the rivers flow apart, he says, they are still together in the source, and each already, in a manner of speaking, knows the course it will take. Then they run out into their separate ways, and he lets them run.

He offers a second picture beside the first. Think of a great tree. Its life runs through the whole of it, through trunk and branch and leaf, while its origin stays where it was, seated in the root and not scattered over the tree. The root does not become the branches. It stays the root, and the branches live from it.

Here is where we are in the story. A spring has fed its rivers, and a root has fed its tree. Let us ask what Plotinus says the rivers are.

He says there are three great levels, which he and later writers call hypostases, a Greek word that means something like a real standing of its own. They are the One, Intellect, and Soul. The One is first. Intellect comes next, and Soul after that. We will spend the next parts of the programme on each. For tonight, we are only watching the flow from one to the next.

Because the One is the source, it cannot be one of the things it gives rise to. This is a hard thought, and Plotinus states it plainly. The One is all things, he says, in the sense that all things come from it, and yet it is not any one of them, since the source of everything cannot itself be everything. If the spring were already a river, it could not feed every river. It has to be of a different kind, simpler than all that follows it.

There is a strange consequence, and Plotinus accepts it. The One gives what it does not itself possess. The forms and the variety that fill the world are not stored inside the One like goods in a warehouse. They come from it, but they are not in it as parts. The simplest thing can be the source of the many, precisely because it is not already many.

Now to the second step, the one that makes the whole picture work. What flows out does not simply drift away. Plotinus says that what comes from the One turns back toward it. At first it is something loose and unformed. It looks to its source, and in looking is filled and given shape, and that turning, he says, is how the second level becomes Intellect.

So every level is made by two movements. It comes out from what is above it, and it turns round to look at what is above it. The going out gives it being. The turning back gives it form. You might hold that in your mind for a moment, as a sort of breathing. Out, and then back, and the breath is whole.

The same pattern continues down the line. Soul comes from Intellect and turns toward it, and Soul in turn gives rise to the ordered world we see. At the very end of the line, Plotinus places matter, the faintest and darkest thing there is. It sits at the edge of the whole procession, where almost nothing of the light remains. Each level is a little less than the one before it. Each is an image of the one above it, and each depends on the one above for everything it has.

You might ask when all of this happens. Was there a first morning, a moment when the One overflowed and the world began? Plotinus does not think so. He argues that the ordered world has always been there, flowing out continuously, as a light has always shone for as long as there has been a sun. The priority of the One is a priority of source, and not a priority of time. Nothing about it is a past event. It is happening now, in the same way, and always has been.

This is part of why he could not agree with some of the thinkers around him. In Rome there were teachers, whom Porphyry calls heretics from the Christian fold and who are usually called Gnostics, who held that the world was a flaw or a prison made by a lesser being. Plotinus wrote a treatise against them, and Porphyry later gave it the title Against the Gnostics. His answer follows from what we have just heard. If the world overflows from the One by way of Intellect and Soul, then it is not an accident or a trap. Its beauty is a real, if lesser, shining of the source. He did not mean that it was perfect. He meant that it was a gift and not a mistake.

One more thing, and it is a quiet one. Because the One loses nothing, it is not far away. Plotinus returns again and again to the thought that what is truly real is present everywhere as a whole, one and the same, and not carved up among the things that share in it. He argues this at length in a pair of treatises on the presence of being everywhere. The spring is not at a distance from the river. The root is not apart from the leaf. Everything that flows is still, in some way, touching its source.

So the picture, as the night settles, is this. At the head of everything is a source that needs nothing. Because it is full, it overflows. Because it overflows without loss, it is never diminished. And what flows out turns back to look at where it came from, and by looking is made into something.

The first thing to come from the One, and the first to turn and look, is the second level. It is not the One, and it is not yet the world. Plotinus calls it Intellect. What is Intellect, and what does it see when it looks?

The Mind of the World

In the house of a lady named Gemina, in Rome, a few students are waiting for their teacher to begin. The lamps are lit, and a small group of students sits on benches. Plotinus, the teacher, is tired, and his voice is hoarse. Porphyry, who attended him for years, tells us that his throat and voice grew weak toward the end of his life, but that in the school he spoke with great ease. Tonight he is going to ask them a question that sounds simple. Where is the knowing done?

This is the fifth part of the story, and the story so far has climbed a ladder. First came the One, which is beyond everything we can name. Then came the overflow, the way a second thing, which Plotinus called Intellect, comes forth from the One without the One being any less. Now we stay with that second thing for a while, because Plotinus spent a great deal of his teaching on it.

Begin with the word itself. The Greek is nous, and Taylor and Guthrie both render it as Intellect, or sometimes Mind. It does not mean cleverness, and it does not mean a mind belonging to some person. It means a whole realm, the place where real knowing takes place.

Plotinus asks his students to imagine how we usually think. You see a horse, then you think about the horse. You see a friend, then you remember what he said. One thing comes after another, and the thinking has to travel from here to there. That kind of knowing takes time, and it always leaves something out.

Now imagine, he says, a knowing that does not travel. Everything is present to it at once. Nothing is behind it and nothing is yet to come. That is what he means by Intellect. In the Taylor translation of the treatise on the intelligible beauty, the picture is of a place where each thing contains all the rest, and each shines in every other. The students are asked to see it as a kind of light in which nothing casts a shadow, because every part is transparent to every other part.

Here is the first thing to hold in mind. Intellect is not a store of information that someone consults. It is what the knowing is made of.

The second thing is the Forms. If you have heard of Plato, you may know the word. Plato, who lived some six centuries before Plotinus, taught that beneath the changing things we see lie lasting patterns, the Form of the beautiful, the Form of the just, the Form of the circle. Plotinus calls himself a faithful reader of Plato, and he takes this teaching and gives it a home. The Forms, he says, are not floating somewhere beyond Intellect, waiting to be looked at from outside. They are inside Intellect. They are its contents, and in a deeper sense they are Intellect itself.

Why does this matter? Because of a worry that Plotinus clearly felt. If the Forms stood outside the mind that knows them, then the mind would only ever be looking at copies of the truth, and never at truth itself. Knowing would be like reading a report about a country you have never visited. Plotinus will not accept that. In the fifth Ennead he argues that Intellect and the things it knows must be one and the same, or else the knowing would be only an image of the real, and not the real itself.

This is the heart of the part, so let us say it slowly. In the highest kind of knowing, the knower and the known are not two things. The one who sees and the thing seen are joined. Plotinus does not mean that Intellect looks at a great truth and then agrees with it. He means that Intellect is the truth, living and aware of itself.

You might wonder how that can be. Think of a small, ordinary example. When you understand a proof in geometry, there is a moment when you stop looking at the lines and the steps, and simply see why it must be so. At that moment, nothing stands between you and the idea. The idea is, so to speak, alive in you. Plotinus would say that this moment is a faint taste of what Intellect is always doing, with no effort and no end.

Mark what this does to our usual picture of the world. We tend to think the real things are the solid ones, stones and rivers and bodies, and that ideas are pale shadows of them. Plotinus turns it around. The things we touch are the copies, and the Forms in Intellect are the originals. A beautiful face is beautiful because it shares, for a while, in Beauty itself. When the face ages, the Beauty is not damaged, because it was never in the face to begin with. It was only showing through.

Now a question a careful student would ask. If Intellect holds all the Forms at once, is it a crowd? Is it a heap of separate ideas? Plotinus says no, and this is one of his most delicate points. Intellect is both one and many. It is many, because there are many Forms, each with its own character. It is one, because they all belong together and none is cut off from the others. He reaches for an image from the sciences of his day. Think of a single body of knowledge, he says, in which every theorem is a separate thing and yet each one contains the whole.

And here we notice a gentle difference from the One. The One is perfectly simple, without any division at all. Intellect already has a kind of two in it, the knowing and the known. They are united, but they are two aspects of a single life. Plotinus says that this is the first step away from absolute simplicity, and the reason Intellect is second and not first. It is as though the One is a pure silence, and Intellect is the first sound that silence makes.

So why does Intellect exist at all? Plotinus gives an answer that has the shape of a story. When the One overflows, what comes forth is at first unformed, a kind of reaching. It then turns back toward its source and looks at it, and in that looking it becomes fixed and full and complete. The turning is what makes it Intellect. Its whole existence is a gazing at the One, and the Forms are what the gaze is filled with. They are, one might say, the shape that the light takes when it is received.

The students sometimes asked Plotinus whether Intellect thinks about the world below, about us, about the rain. His answer, as far as we can tell from the Enneads, is that it does not need to think about anything outside itself. It has everything within. It does not deliberate or plan or look for a solution, because deliberation belongs to those who do not yet know. Intellect simply sees, and what it sees is the whole.

Let us pause where we are. We are in a lecture room in Rome, lamps burning low. Plotinus has told his students that there is a mind that is not anyone's mind, in which knower and known are one, where all the Forms live together and are never lost. It comes from the One by overflow, and it lives by looking back toward its source.

There is a line in the sixth Ennead that students have always loved, because it makes the vast thing feel close. Plotinus says that all things there are transparent, that nothing is dark or resistant, and that everything is clear to everything, inward and outward alike. He speaks of the place as a land where there is no strangeness, because whatever you meet there is yourself, seen from within.

And he adds something that explains why he taught at all. This Intellect is not far away. The best part of each of us belongs to it. Porphyry reports that Plotinus seemed ashamed to be in a body, which gives a hint of how deeply he felt this. When we are most awake, when we grasp a truth or see a deep beauty, we are not doing something strange. We are remembering where we come from.

Even so, there is a danger in making Intellect too grand. Plotinus never says that it is the end of the road. It is the second of three great realities, and it is itself only a looking toward something beyond. He repeats that the One is prior to Intellect, and that Intellect, however luminous, is still a unity that has some multiplicity. The mind of the world is wonderful, but it is not the source.

There is also the matter of what Plotinus did not claim. He did not say that Intellect is a god who made the world by a decision, as some later thinkers would. In his account, the world comes about by a calm outflow, without a plan or any act of will. He did not say that you must believe all this on faith, either. Again and again he asks the listener to look inward and test it.

In the treatise on how we ought to think of the intelligible world, he even suggests an exercise. Imagine the whole sphere of the heavens, with the sun and moon and stars and earth all visible at once. Then hold that picture steady, and remove from it all size and mass, so that every part keeps its shape but loses its bulk. What remains is a glimpse of the way all things stand together in Intellect, each one whole, each one bright.

If you try this in the dark, as you lie here with your eyes closed, you may find that the picture does not need much effort. It simply stays, a little way off, quiet and complete. That quiet completeness is the nearest an image can come to what Plotinus meant.

One more small detail from Porphyry. He tells us that in his teaching Plotinus would sometimes sit in silence, with his eyes steady, and that those who watched him felt they were seeing a mind at rest within itself. Perhaps that is as good a picture of Intellect as any. Not a loud machine of thought, but a stillness in which everything is known.

Yet a question lingers, and the lecture room feels it. If Intellect holds all the Forms in perfect rest, and the One stands above it, then what of us? What of the part of us that wanders, that forgets, that suffers and learns in time? Where does the soul fit?

The Soul's Descent

Late in the day, in the house of a widow named Gemina in Rome, a philosopher is dictating to a pupil. The lamp is lit. The pupil is Porphyry, and he is writing down a treatise that will one day be numbered the first of the fourth Ennead, though Plotinus himself never numbered anything. Porphyry tells us that the master did not even reread what he wrote. He simply set it down and handed it on.

The story so far has climbed upward. We began with the One, beyond all being. We followed its overflow into Intellect, the mind where everything is known at once, and Intellect's own overflow into Soul. Tonight the story turns downward, to the third of these, and to the strange question of why a soul that belongs above should ever come to live in a body.

Plotinus asks it with real feeling. In the treatise on the descent of the soul, he writes of how often he has woken up out of the body to himself, standing outside everything else and inside himself, and has seen a beauty that was very great. Then he asks how it is that he has come down at all. That is the question we hold tonight, and it will not be answered quickly.

Begin with where the soul stands. For Plotinus, Soul is the last of the three divine things, and it has two faces. One face looks up toward Intellect, and receives from it. The other looks down, and gives. It is like a person standing at a doorway, with one eye on the house and one on the street.

There is also a great Soul, the Soul of the All, and there are many individual souls that belong to it. Plotinus insists they are not separate islands. They are one and many together, as the single science of geometry is one and yet holds many theorems. So when we speak of your soul and mine, we are speaking of members of one family that have not fully forgotten each other.

Now to the descent. The honest thing to say first is that Plotinus does not give one tidy answer. He gives several, and he lets them sit side by side. Porphyry did not hide this in his edition. In the treatise on the descent, Plotinus admits that what Plato says about it is not always consistent.

Plato had spoken of the soul as sometimes sent down by necessity, and sometimes as falling by its own choice. He had called the body a prison, and he had also called the world a god, blessed and beautiful. Plotinus takes these seemingly clashing remarks seriously and tries to hold them together. He says that both can be true, because the soul has a higher part that never descends at all.

This is one of the boldest things he teaches. A part of every soul remains above, in the intelligible world, undisturbed. The descent is real, but it is not total. You might think of a person who dreams a long and troubled dream, while the sleeper lying in the bed is untouched.

Why then does the soul go down? Here is the first answer. It is a kind of generosity. Soul is made to care for what is lower than itself, as a gardener is made to tend the garden. Plotinus compares the soul's care for the body to the ordering of a great house, and he says that this is no disgrace, so long as the soul keeps its head above. The world needs ordering, and Soul is what orders it.

The second answer is darker. Some souls, he says, descend through a kind of daring. In another treatise he names the beginning of the trouble. It is boldness, and the wish to belong to oneself, and a pleasure in independence. The soul turns from the whole to its own small part, much as a child might let go of a parent's hand just to feel what it is to walk alone. And having let go, it forgets whose hand it held.

See the gentleness of the picture. He does not describe a crime followed by a punishment. He describes a turning away that is also, in a sense, natural. Souls go forth, he says, and the world is the field where that going forth takes place.

Now we come to the body, and here the story turns surprising. A listener who knows only a little about the old philosophies might expect Plotinus to despise the body. There are stories that he did. Porphyry opens his Life by saying that Plotinus seemed ashamed of being in a body. It is the very first line, and it is the reason he gave for refusing a portrait, which is where this whole series began.

But look at what Plotinus actually argues. He wrote a whole treatise against the Gnostics, who held that the world was a botched or wicked thing made by an inferior power. He answers them with anger, which was rare for him. The world, he says, is a beautiful image of the intelligible realm, and anyone who scorns it scorns its maker. He allows that the body is a lower thing. He will not allow that it is evil in the way they claim.

So what is the body, in his picture? The most helpful image is a mirror. In the treatise on the descent, he describes how Soul, looking down, finds something prepared to receive it, and how it illuminates what it finds. Elsewhere he uses the story of Narcissus, who reached for his own reflection in the water and was lost. The body is a surface that shows us an image of ourselves. The danger is not the mirror. The danger is taking the reflection for the real thing, and plunging in after it.

Think of how light falls on still water at evening. The image of the sky is lovely, and it is true as far as it goes. But it is a reflection, and no one who knew the sky would try to live in it. So too with matter. Plotinus calls it the last and lowest thing, a kind of darkness with no shape of its own, which receives form without ever holding it. The body is shaped matter, a place where the light shows up, borrowed and faint.

This is why the descent can go wrong without going evil. The soul gives life to the body, and then it grows so absorbed in the giving that it begins to share the body's fortunes. It feels the body's pains and wants. It starts to think of itself as the thing it tends.

Here Plotinus makes a distinction that is worth holding. The pain belongs to the body, and the soul perceives it. But the higher part of us, the part that stayed above, is not touched. He says that the man within may be distinguished from the composite animal, the living creature made of body and soul together. We might say that you are not simply the one who feels the cold. You are also the one who knows that the cold is being felt.

Let us gather the thread, for the night is long and there is no hurry. Soul stands between two worlds. It looks upward to Intellect and downward to matter. It descends because it is made to order and care for what is below, and also because it grows bold and wishes to be its own. Yet part of it never leaves. And the body, in all this, is neither prison nor enemy but a mirror, a place where the soul sees an image of itself, and may forget that it is only an image.

Porphyry tells us that Plotinus lived out this teaching in a quiet way. He was attentive to those around him, he looked after the orphans entrusted to him, and he was never harsh. And yet, Porphyry says, he was never wholly absent from the intelligible even in the middle of the day's business. That is the picture of a soul with its two faces held in balance, one turned gently to the street and one turned always to the house.

But a soul that has descended, and half forgotten, must have some way to remember. A mirror that has grown clouded must be cleared. So the question that hangs in the dark room tonight is a simple one, and a patient one. If we have come down, what is the way back.

Beauty as a Road

Imagine a workshop in Rome, in the middle of the third century, late in the afternoon. A sculptor has been working all day on a block of marble, and the floor around him is white with chips and dust. He is not adding anything to the stone. He is taking away. Hold that picture, because Plotinus held it too, and he used it to explain one of the most beautiful passages he ever wrote.

You are in the seventh part of this story. We have followed Plotinus from Egypt to Rome, we have met the One, the Intellect and the Soul, and we have watched the soul come down into the body. Now he turns to a gentler question: how does a soul find its way back? His answer begins with beauty.

The treatise on beauty is the sixth in the order Porphyry gave the writings, which is the first Ennead, sixth treatise. Porphyry, his student and editor, arranged the fifty-four treatises into six groups of nine, and this one stands near the very start of the collection. It is short, and it reads almost like a walk with a guide who keeps asking you to look again.

It opens with a plain observation. Beauty, he says, is mostly in sight, but it is also in hearing, in the combining of words, in music, and in conduct and ways of life. Then he asks what it is that makes any of these things beautiful. Many people in his day said that beauty is a matter of proportion, of parts fitting well together with a pleasing colour. Plotinus is not satisfied with that.

His objection is simple, and you can feel its force. If beauty were only a good fit of parts, then a single thing with no parts could never be beautiful. But a single colour, or a single ray of light, or a single note, can be beautiful. So proportion cannot be the whole answer. Something else must be shining through the things we call lovely.

He goes on to notice that the same fire of beauty can be seen in a face and then again in a sound, and that the soul recognises it each time. Here he makes a quiet and important claim: the soul is not astonished like a stranger. It greets beauty like something it already knows. When it meets what is ugly, it shrinks back and turns away, as if the thing were foreign to its nature. In Thomas Taylor's rendering, the soul, being what it is, is delighted at what it finds akin to its own essence. That is the sense, and it is worth keeping, though the exact wording differs between translators.

So beauty is a kind of reminder. A beautiful body is lovely because it shares in a form, and the form has come into the matter from something above. The beauty you see in a face is a trace of something that is not itself a face. Plotinus does not despise the body for this. He simply says that it points beyond itself, and that a wise person follows the pointing.

Now the road begins to climb. From beautiful bodies, he says, we must go on to beautiful ways of life, to the beauty of laws and customs, and to the beauty of the sciences and of the virtues. Each step is less bound to matter than the one before. A person who has learned to see the beauty of a just act is already standing higher than a person who sees only a handsome face.

And then comes the image of the sculptor, which is the heart of the whole treatise. Plotinus asks what you should do if you cannot yet see your own beauty. His answer is to do what the maker of a statue does. The sculptor cuts away here, smooths there, makes one line lighter and another cleaner, until a lovely face grows out of the marble. So you too must cut away what is excessive, straighten what is crooked, and bring light to what is dark, and never stop working on your own statue until the divine glory of virtue shines out of it.

Hear what this says about where beauty lives. The beauty is not brought in from outside and fastened on. It is uncovered. The statue was latent in the stone, and the work is a work of removal. In the same way, Plotinus holds that the soul does not need to become something new. It needs to be freed of what covers it.

It matters, too, that the sculptor in this picture is yourself. Plotinus does not say that a teacher or a ritual will carve you. He says you must not cease chiselling your own statue. The treatise is gentle, but it is not passive. It asks for steady labour, day after day, the way the sculptor in the workshop returns each morning to the same block.

Here is where the road turns inward. He asks the listener to look within and see. If you do not yet see yourself as beautiful, he says, then do not stop working until the beauty is plain. The seeing he means is not seeing with the eyes. He says that to behold such beauty one must have an eye that has become like the thing it sees. Only what is akin can know what is akin. A clouded eye cannot see a clear light, and a soul that has not been made beautiful cannot see the Beautiful.

This is why the virtues matter so much to him. He does not treat them as rules or as a way of earning praise. He treats them as a cleaning. In another treatise, the one on the virtues, he says that the soul's purification is the real work, and that the civic virtues, the ordinary good habits of a decent life, are a first stage of it. Beyond them lie the virtues of a soul that has begun to turn away from the body altogether. In that treatise he describes purification as a separation of the soul from what is alien to it, so that it may stand alone and recover its own nature.

Porphyry, in his account of his teacher's thought, drew a ladder out of this idea, with the civic virtues at the bottom and the purifying virtues above them. We should be careful here. The idea of purification is Plotinus's own, but the tidy fourfold ladder of virtues is a later schooling of his thought, set out by Porphyry and carried forward by those who came after. When you hear it described, remember that it is a pupil's arrangement of a teacher's more flowing words.

Let us stay a moment with the quiet strangeness of what he is saying. We usually think of virtue as doing: acting justly, acting bravely, acting kindly. For Plotinus it is also, and more deeply, a kind of becoming. A person grows lighter as the clinging things fall away, as the sculptor's floor fills with chips and the figure stands cleaner. The virtues are the chisel, but the work they do is a work of simplifying.

And simplicity is the thread that runs through the whole teaching. You will remember that the One is utterly simple, and that everything which comes from the One is a little less simple, a little more divided. A soul that sheds its divisions, its worries, its scattered wants, moves back toward that simplicity. Beauty, then, is the gentle hand that first shows the soul which way is up.

Near the end of the treatise he gives his famous call to the traveller. Let us flee, he says, to our dear fatherland. It is a line borrowed in spirit from the old poem of Odysseus, who longs to reach home, and Plotinus asks what the fatherland and the road to it could be. He answers that it is not a place you reach by ship or by foot. It is something you carry, something you have only forgotten how to see.

So the workshop is quiet now, the marble figure standing in the last of the light, and the sculptor putting down his tools. The road we have been following is clear enough: from lovely bodies, to lovely ways of life, to the virtues, and then inward, shedding and smoothing, until the eye is clear. But a road leads somewhere. And so the question that this part leaves open, the one the next part must face, is a simple one. What is the goal of the road.

Union

By late afternoon, in a small room in Rome, Plotinus is past fifty, and the lesson has slowed. The shutters are half closed. A few students sit nearby, and the master has stopped speaking in the middle of a thought. He is looking at nothing. This is the part of the story where we ask what the whole teaching was for, and what he said happened at the end of it.

Porphyry, who came to Rome in the year two hundred and sixty-three, tells us in his Life of Plotinus that his teacher reached the goal four times in the years Porphyry knew him. Porphyry himself, he says, came near it only once, in his sixty-eighth year. So the first thing to hold gently is this. The report comes from a loyal pupil, written some decades after the events, about experiences he mostly did not witness.

Here is what Porphyry says. Plotinus had a goal before him, which was to be united with the God who is above all things. Four times, while Porphyry was with him, that goal was reached, by an inexpressible act and not by any outward form. That is almost all we are given. There is no date for any of the four, no description of a room or an hour, no account of what the body did.

This is, in a way, true to the teaching. Throughout the Enneads, the One is beyond being and beyond thought, so whatever is said of it must be said with care. In the treatise on the Good, Plotinus writes that when we say anything of the One, we are not saying it as a thing named, but trying to point.

Mark how he handles the limits of speech. He does not fall silent. Over many years he wrote about it, sentence after patient sentence. But he keeps reminding the reader that the words are a road and not the place. He compares the thinker to someone who must use images, then set them down. Taylor, in his old English translation, renders one such passage as a plain instruction to take away every addition, and to look.

That brings us to the practice. Plotinus does not offer a technique with steps and counted breaths. What he offers is closer to a direction of attention. In the treatise on beauty, which you heard earlier, he tells the reader to go back into himself and look, and if he does not yet see himself beautiful, to do as the sculptor does, cutting away, polishing, until a shining form appears. The practice, then, is subtraction. Remove the false, the heavy, the borrowed.

In the later treatise on the intelligible beauty, he describes the same movement as a kind of waking. The soul has been absorbed in outer things, and it turns, and finds that what it sought was already within. Guthrie, in his early twentieth century version, has Plotinus speak of closing the eyes and exchanging the ordinary way of seeing for another, which everyone has but few use.

Why stillness? Because, for Plotinus, the One is not somewhere else. It is present to everything, and what stands between the soul and it is not distance but distraction. In the treatise on the Good he says that the One is not absent from anyone, and yet is present to all, so that those who are not ready cannot see it. The soul does not climb to a far country. It quiets, and what was always there is found.

Now we come to the most careful part of the lesson, which is what he says about the moment itself. In the treatise usually called On the Three Primary Hypostases, and again in the sixth Ennead, he speaks of a vision in which there is no longer a seer and a seen. Seer and seen are one, he says, and the soul does not so much look at the One as become it. In the last lines of the final treatise, in the Guthrie rendering, he calls this the flight of the alone to the alone. That phrase has echoed for seventeen centuries.

Yet he is honest about how brief it is. In the fourth Ennead he gives a first-person account, rare in his writing, of waking up to himself out of the body, outside of everything else, and seeing a beauty beyond any he knew. Then, he says, he comes down again from intellect to reasoning, and asks himself how it is that he ever came down at all. It is one of the few places where the teacher shows us a human face. He does not claim to live at the summit. He visits it, and returns, puzzled and grateful.

There is a plain lesson in that. The union Porphyry reports is not a permanent condition, and Plotinus never says it is. It comes four times in about six years, and then, as far as we know, it does not come again before his death. The life around those moments is ordinary. He looks after orphans left in his care. He settles disputes. He suffers from a failing body in his last years. The vision does not replace the work of living. It sits beside it.

Porphyry tells us of his final days with simple tenderness. Plotinus had withdrawn to an estate in Campania, too ill for company, and a friend, Eustochius, came to him late. According to Porphyry, Eustochius reached him as he was dying, and Plotinus said that he had been waiting, and that he was trying to bring the divine in us back to the divine in the all. Scholars note that the exact wording comes through Eustochius to Porphyry, so it has passed through two memories. We should hold it lightly, as a likely echo and not a recording.

Still, the sentence fits everything else. Nothing is added to the soul at the end. Something is given back. The One was never lost, only unseen.

So what has this part taught? A few quiet things. That the highest moment in Plotinus is reported, by a devoted student, as rare, wordless, and unrepeatable on demand. That the method is subtraction and turning inward, not conquest. That he used language faithfully while insisting it could only point. And that the man who reached for the One went on, afterwards, to feed the hungry of his household and to answer questions at the end of a long day.

If you are drifting now, let the room in Rome grow dim. The students have gone quiet, and the master's face has softened. He has come back from wherever he went, and will say, in a moment, something small and kind. The great vision is behind him, and the evening is ordinary.

In a few years he will be gone, and his writings will lie in Porphyry's hands, fifty-four treatises waiting to be sorted. What happens to them then is the last question in our story. How did his teaching travel after him?

What Followed

In the year two hundred and seventy, on an estate in Campania, south of Rome, a man lay dying, and a physician named Eustochius had come to his bedside a little too late. The man was Plotinus. Porphyry reports that he told Eustochius he had been waiting for him, and that he was trying to bring the divine within us back to the divine in the All. Porphyry himself was not in the room. He was far away in Sicily, and he heard of it afterwards.

So we come to the last evening of this long story. Plotinus is gone, and what remains is a pile of manuscripts and a handful of friends. The question for tonight is a gentle one. What became of the teaching once the teacher was gone?

Porphyry answered it first, and in the most practical way. He gathered the treatises into six groups of nine, fifty four writings in all, and the Greek word for nine gave the book its name, the Enneads. Every later reader came to Plotinus through that arrangement. You may rest on that for a moment. A teaching that was spoken in a room in Rome travelled onward because someone sat down, patiently, and put the pages in order.

For a while the teaching lived among those who called themselves simply Platonists. In Athens, more than two centuries later, a philosopher named Proclus led the Platonic school until his death in four hundred and eighty five. Proclus built a very orderly system in which everything proceeds from the One and then turns back toward it, and you will recognise the shape. It is the overflow and the return that we followed together.

Then the schools of Athens met a hard ending. The tradition says that the emperor Justinian ordered them closed in five hundred and twenty nine. Historians still argue about how sudden and how complete that closing was. But the old teaching did not vanish. It had already slipped out of the schools, and it was travelling by quieter roads.

One of those roads runs through a young teacher of rhetoric named Augustine. Later thinkers such as Al Farabi and Avicenna described a world that flows from one first source through a series of intellects. Let us slow down now, and be honest about what Plotinus did not say. Because a teaching that travels so far is often given words it never spoke.

He never called himself a Neoplatonist. That word is modern, and the ancient thinkers we group under it simply thought of themselves as Platonists.

He was not a Christian, and he was not a Gnostic either. In the treatise that Porphyry's tradition titles Against the Gnostics, Plotinus rejects the claim that the world was made by an ignorant or evil maker, and defends the cosmos as beautiful. For him the world is not a mistake to be escaped. It is an image, and a lovely one, of something greater.

He did not think he was founding anything. He said that his teachings were not new, that they had been spoken long ago, and that he was only an interpreter of the ancients.

And he did not picture the One as a craftsman who plans, decides and then builds. The One remains as it is, unchanged and undiminished, while all else arises from it. It does not turn toward us. If there is a turning, it is ours.

That is a fair account. Plotinus gave later ages a language, and each age used it for its own purposes, sometimes faithfully and sometimes not. He would perhaps have smiled at that, for a language of the One was always meant to be borrowed.
