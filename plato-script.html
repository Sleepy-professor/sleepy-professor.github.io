Cold open

In a house near the harbour of Piraeus, a young man named Glaucon sits in the lamplight, listening to Socrates. Socrates asks him to picture something, and to picture it slowly.

Picture a cave. Not a cave you would wander into on a hillside, but a long, low chamber under the ground, with one wide opening at the far end, where the daylight comes in. Socrates calls it an underground den.

Deep inside, in the dim part, people are sitting. They have sat here since they were children. Their legs are chained, and their necks are chained too, so that they cannot turn their heads. They can look at one thing only, and that is the wall in front of them.

Behind them, and higher up, a fire is burning. Between the fire and the prisoners runs a path, and along the path there is a low wall, a little like the screen a puppet player stands behind. People walk along behind that wall, carrying all sorts of things on their heads. Statues, figures of animals, vessels of stone and wood.

The prisoners never see any of this. The fire throws the shadows of the carried things onto the wall in front of them, and shadows are all that anyone in the cave has ever looked at. When a shadow passes, the prisoners name it. They would say that is a horse, or that is a man, and they would be sure of it, because nothing else has ever been offered to them as a horse or a man.

Glaucon, who is quick, answers at once. It is a strange picture, he says, and they are strange prisoners. Socrates replies in three words. Like ourselves.

Hold that for a moment, and let your breathing settle. We are at the very beginning of a long story, and for now we stay inside the cave, where it is quiet, and warm enough, and nothing ever changes.

Now imagine that one morning, though in the cave there is no morning, only a change in the dark, one of the prisoners is let go. Someone unlocks the chains. He is told to stand up. He is told to turn his head, and to walk toward the light of the fire.

It does not feel like a gift. His legs are stiff. His neck aches in a way it has never ached, because it has never moved. When he looks at the fire, his eyes sting and water, and the objects that cast the shadows are blurred and glaring. If someone told him that what he saw before was only a trick of shadows, and that this is nearer to the truth, he would not believe it. He would want to turn back to the wall, where everything was clear.

That is where the story starts. A man stands in the glare, hurting, with a whole life behind him that he cannot yet doubt, and a slope in front of him that leads up toward a light he has never seen.

It is one of the most famous pictures in all of philosophy, and it has been told and retold for more than two thousand years. But before we follow that man up the slope, we should ask a simpler question, the kind you ask about any story you are told in the dark. Who told this story first?

A Wrestler Called Platon

In the year four hundred and twenty-seven before the common era, give or take a year, a boy was born in Athens to a family with a long name behind it. His father was called Ariston, his mother Perictione, and the boy was given the name Aristocles, after his grandfather. You will not know him by that name. He is remembered by a nickname, and the story of the nickname is where we begin tonight.

It is told that he was a broad young man, and that a wrestling master gave him the name Platon, which in Greek means broad. Some said the name came from the breadth of his forehead, and some from the breadth of his style. The ancient writers did not agree, and we cannot settle it now. But the wrestling part is worth holding on to. The same writer who tells us this adds that Plato wrestled at the Isthmian games, the great festival near Corinth. So the man who would one day write about gentle, patient thinking began his life in the dust of the wrestling ground, among oil and sand and sweating young men.

Picture the city he grew up in. Athens was loud, bright, and sure of itself. It sat under its rock, and on the rock stood the great temples, newly built in his parents' lifetime. Below it ran the market, the courts, and the narrow streets where citizens argued about everything. This was a democracy, in which ordinary men voted in an assembly and sat on juries chosen by lot. It was also an empire of sorts, with a fleet that ruled the sea.

Now, the story so far is a boy, a nickname, and a proud city. Hold those three things, because the city is about to change.

Plato's family stood close to the heart of Athenian life. His father's line, it was said, went back to the old kings of Athens. His mother's side was more famous still. Perictione was related to Solon, the lawgiver who had shaped the city's constitution two centuries before. And she had relatives who would, in Plato's youth, hold great power. Her cousin was Critias, and her brother was Charmides. Keep those two names in mind. They will matter in a moment, and not happily.

After Ariston died, Perictione married again. Her new husband was a man named Pyrilampes, who was a friend of the great statesman Pericles. So the boy grew up in a house where politics was not a distant thing. It was dinner conversation. It was the weather of the household. And it was widely expected that a young man of this family would, when he came of age, step into public life and take his place among the leaders of Athens.

But the world he was growing up in was at war.

When Plato was a small child, Athens and Sparta went to war, and the fighting dragged on, with pauses, for twenty-seven years. We call it the Peloponnesian War. Plato's childhood and youth passed inside it. The great historian Thucydides, who lived through it, tells us that a plague struck Athens in the second year, killing a great many people, and that the city's own leader Pericles died of it. Later came a disastrous expedition to Sicily, in which an entire Athenian army and fleet were lost. Imagine a young man listening to the news of that. Hearing the grief of neighbours. Watching a confident city learn what fear was.

The war ended in four hundred and four, with Athens beaten and starved into surrender. Sparta installed a new government of thirty men, which history calls the Thirty Tyrants. And here the two names we held return. Critias was among the leaders of the Thirty, and Charmides was one of the ten who governed the port. Both were Plato's own kin. Both died in the fighting when the democracy fought back and won.

Plato was about twenty-three at the time. He tells us, in a letter that scholars generally accept as his, what he felt. He says he hoped these men would lead the city out of an unjust life into a just one, and that he watched closely to see what they would do. Then he says they made the earlier government look like a golden age. They tried to draw his old friend Socrates into their crimes, and Socrates refused. Plato turned away in disgust.

We should be careful here. That letter, the Seventh Letter, is the one place where Plato seems to speak about his own life, and a few scholars still doubt that he wrote it. Most think it is authentic or close to it. We will use it only where it is steady, and this is such a place.

Now we come to the man who has been standing at the edge of this whole story. Socrates.

Socrates was already an old figure in Athens, a stonecutter's son, short, snub-nosed, bare-footed, and famous for asking questions in the market and the gymnasia. He wrote nothing. Everything we know of him comes from others, and above all from Plato. He talked to young men, to generals, to craftsmen, to poets, and he asked each of them the same sort of simple thing. What is courage? What is piety? What is a good life? And he seemed to find, again and again, that the people who sounded surest did not really know.

The ancient biographer Diogenes Laertius tells a small story about how Plato met him. On the eve of the day Plato was to take part in a tragic contest, he heard Socrates talking, and he burned his poems, and gave himself to philosophy. That is the tale, written down some five centuries afterwards, and it may be only a tale. But it points at something real. Plato was probably in his late teens or early twenties when he began to follow Socrates, and he stayed with him until the end.

Why did a young man of such a family, a man who might have ruled, choose to follow a barefoot questioner? Plato himself never says in so many words. But in the dialogues that he later wrote, we can see what drew him. Socrates did not teach by lecturing. He took a person's own words and turned them over, gently, until the person could see their shape. It was a kind of wrestling too, but done with words, and with no one thrown to the ground.

And in those years, in the dry hot streets of a defeated city, something was gathering around the old man. He was admired, and he was also resented. He had friends among the very men who had ruined the city, and the city had not forgotten it. Athens, restored to democracy and nervous about its own safety, was looking for someone to blame. Plato, still a young man, was standing close, and listening, and learning.

So we leave him there tonight. Not yet a writer, not yet a teacher, but a broad-shouldered young Athenian of good family, who has seen the empire fall, who has seen his own relatives turn tyrants, and who has found in a stonecutter's son the one person who makes sense to him. And we leave the old man too, walking in the evening light, with his questions still in his mouth, and the city beginning to turn against him. What happened to Socrates is where we go next.

The Trial

Early in the spring of the year three hundred and ninety-nine before the common era, a man named Meletus walked to the porch of the King Archon in Athens and lodged a charge against Socrates. The King Archon was the magistrate who dealt with matters of religion, and the porch where he sat was a modest place for the beginning of something so large. Meletus was young, and little known. Two older men stood behind him, Anytus and Lycon.

The charge, as Diogenes Laertius gives it, said that Socrates did wrong by not believing in the gods the city believed in, by bringing in other new divinities, and by corrupting the young. Plato, who was perhaps twenty-eight years old, would remember every part of what followed. You are following him now through the second hour of his life that mattered most, the hour in which the teacher he loved was taken from him.

Socrates was seventy. He had spent most of those years walking the agora, asking people what they meant by the words they used so easily, words like courage and piety and justice. Those who were questioned often found they could not answer, and some did not forgive him for it. In the Apology, Plato's version of the speech Socrates gave in his own defence, Socrates says plainly that this was the root of the trouble.

The trial was held before a jury of five hundred and one citizens, chosen by lot. Plato was present. We know it because Socrates names him in the Apology, among the friends standing near, and offers to pay a fine on his behalf. It is a small moment, and a tender one. Out of all the crowd, the old man points to the young man.

Socrates begins his defence with a mild joke. He says his accusers spoke so persuasively that he almost forgot himself, and yet hardly a word of what they said was true. Then he explains how he came to be disliked. A friend of his, Chaerephon, had once gone to the oracle at Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The priestess answered that no one was.

Socrates says he was puzzled. He knew he was not wise. So he went to find someone wiser, a politician, then a poet, then a craftsman, to prove the god wrong. What he found, over and over, was that these men thought they knew what they did not. He concludes that he is wiser in only one small way, that he does not think he knows what he does not know.

You can hear in this how irritating he must have been, and how gentle. He was not boasting. He was describing a habit of mind. But every person he had questioned in the street had walked away a little smaller, and they had friends.

Now the story is in the middle of the trial, and the jury is about to vote. Socrates did not plead for his life. He told the jurors that he would not stop philosophizing, even if they offered to let him go on that condition. He said he would go on asking, as long as he had breath. He compared himself to a gadfly sent to a large and sluggish horse, the city, to sting it awake.

The jury voted, and he was found guilty by a narrow margin. In Athenian practice the accuser proposed a penalty, and the defendant proposed another, and the jury chose between them. Meletus asked for death. Socrates was expected to propose exile, or a fine. Instead he said that, for what he had done, the right treatment was to be fed at public expense in the town hall, the honour given to Olympic victors. Then, with Plato and a few others urging him, he offered a fine of thirty minas, which friends would guarantee.

The jury voted again, and this time a larger number chose death. Socrates spoke once more. He told the jurors who had condemned him that he did not regret the manner of his defence. He would rather die having spoken as he did than live by speaking otherwise. And to those who had voted for his acquittal, he said that nothing evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.

Then the room emptied, and a strange delay began. In Athens, a death sentence could not be carried out while the sacred ship was away on its yearly voyage to Delos, in memory of Theseus. The city kept itself clean of public executions during that voyage. The ship had set out the day before the trial, so Socrates waited in prison for about thirty days.

This is the setting of the Phaedo. Plato was not there on the final day. The dialogue says so, in one of the most quietly honest lines in all his writing: Plato, it says, was ill. We do not know whether he truly was, or why he put the remark in. He is telling us, gently, that he was absent from the room he is about to describe, and that what follows is a report, carried to a friend by someone who was there.

What the report describes is a day of talk. Socrates spends his last hours speaking with his friends about whether the soul survives the body, and he does so without fear. His wife, Xanthippe, had been to see him earlier and had gone away weeping. He sends her home, so that she will not disturb the conversation. Then he bathes, so the women will not have to wash his body after he is dead.

When the jailer comes at sunset, he is not harsh. He says that of all the men who had come to that place, Socrates was the noblest and the gentlest, and he weeps as he turns away. Socrates asks for the cup. A friend, Crito, tells him the sun is still on the mountains, and that others have delayed. Socrates says they were fools to think they gained anything by it, and that he will not make himself ridiculous by clinging to a life that is spent.

The man who gives him the poison tells him to walk about until his legs grow heavy, and then to lie down. Socrates does so. The man presses his foot, and asks if he can feel it. He cannot. The numbness rises. Socrates covers his face. Then he uncovers it, and speaks his last words, which are to Crito: we owe a cock to Asclepius, so do not forget to pay the debt. Asclepius was the god of healing. Scholars have argued ever since about what he meant, and the argument has never closed.

Plato, hearing all this afterward, was a changed man. Diogenes Laertius, writing centuries later, says that after the death of Socrates Plato went with other followers to Megara, to stay with Euclides, a friend of the master. The detail is reported late, and we should hold it loosely, but it fits what we would expect. Athens had become dangerous for the friends of Socrates, and a young man in grief might want to be somewhere else.

What the death did to Plato is not something he tells directly in the dialogues. He is silent about himself. But a letter in his name, the Seventh, says that watching the laws and customs of the city go wrong, and the men in power act unjustly, he came to believe that the only hope for cities was true philosophy, and that rulers must become philosophers or philosophers rulers. Scholars still debate whether Plato wrote that letter. Many think the voice is his, even if the hand is uncertain, and we will hold it with that care.

So here is where the story rests tonight. A young man stood in a crowded court and heard his teacher condemned. He did not speak out, so far as we know, apart from his name being spoken. He was absent from the last room. And he left Athens, quiet, carrying a question that would take him the rest of his life to answer: what sort of city could do this, and what sort of city could not?

What did Plato do next?

The Academy

A ship leaves the harbour of Athens in the early years of the fourth century before the common era, and a man of about thirty stands at the rail. He is Plato, and Socrates has been dead for some years now. The ancient biographer Diogenes Laertius says that after the death of Socrates, Plato withdrew to Megara, to the philosopher Euclides, and that he then went on to Cyrene and to Egypt. Some of these journeys may be later embroidery, and the ancient sources do not always agree. But the pattern is clear enough. A young man whose teacher has been put to death by his own city does not stay at home in comfort. He goes looking.

Where the story stands, then, is this. We have followed Plato from his noble family to the shadow of Socrates, and through the trial that took Socrates from him. Now the questions are what Plato did with his grief, and how he turned it into something that would outlast him.

The most famous of his journeys was to the west, to Italy and to Sicily. Diogenes Laertius reports that Plato went to Italy to meet the Pythagoreans, and that he spent time with followers of Pythagoras there. It is here that a good deal of his later thinking about number, about the soul, and about a life ordered by study may have taken shape. We should say honestly that the details of what he learned are disputed, and scholars still weigh how much he owed to these western teachers.

Then comes Sicily, and a story that matters a great deal for the Academy. The island's greatest city was Syracuse, and it was ruled by a tyrant named Dionysius, the first of that name. Plato, according to Diogenes Laertius, went to Sicily to see the volcano, and there he met the tyrant. Whether the visit was truly about a volcano, we cannot say. What the ancient tales agree on is that the conversation went badly. A philosopher who has just watched one government kill the best man he knew was not likely to flatter another ruler who held power without answering to anyone.

Diogenes tells that Dionysius grew angry, that the two quarrelled, and that Plato was handed over to be sold as a slave. The tale goes that a man from Cyrene named Anniceris happened to be there and paid the price, and so set Plato free. The story comes to us late, and it may be coloured. But notice what it shows about how the ancients pictured him. They saw a man who could walk into a tyrant's palace and say what he thought, and who very nearly paid for it with his freedom.

Whatever the exact truth, Plato came home to Athens, and he came home with a purpose. Socrates had taught by walking about the city and talking with anyone who would stop. That had ended in a cup of hemlock. Plato would try something steadier. He would gather young people in one place, and give them time, and a shared life of study.

The place he chose lay outside the city walls, a short walk from the gate called the Dipylon. Diogenes Laertius says that Plato taught in the Academy, and afterwards in the garden at Colonus. The name comes from an old hero, Academus, whose sacred grove of olive trees lay there. It was a public park, with shaded paths and running water, and gymnasia where young men exercised. Plato did not need to build a palace of learning. He needed a quiet corner of a place that already belonged to the city.

The founding is usually placed around the year three hundred and eighty seven before the common era, after his return from the first Sicilian journey. The date is an estimate, drawn from the order of events in the ancient sources, and we hold it loosely. What matters is what grew there. The Academy lasted, in one form or another, for centuries, which makes it one of the longest lived schools of thought the ancient world ever knew.

So how did it teach? Here we have to be careful, because Plato left no rulebook for the school. What we have are his dialogues, and from them we can see what he valued. In the Republic, which we will come to, he lays out a long course of study for those who are to govern: arithmetic, then geometry, then astronomy, then music, and at the end, dialectic, the art of questioning and answering. Whether the Academy itself followed that exact order, no one can prove. But the spirit is plain. Number and measure first, to train the mind to leave off looking at what changes and to turn toward what stays the same.

There is an old story, repeated by later writers, that over the entrance to the Academy was written a warning: let no one ignorant of geometry enter. We should say plainly that this tale comes from sources written many centuries after Plato, and no early evidence supports it. It may well be a legend. Yet it was told for so long because it caught something true about the place, that geometry was thought a doorway, a way of learning to love what is certain.

And dialectic, the heart of the matter, was a form of conversation. It is the same art we see in Socrates, now made into a discipline. In the Symposium, Plato has Socrates describe a ladder of love, which begins with the love of one beautiful body, and climbs to love of all beautiful things, then to beautiful laws and knowledge, and at last to the beautiful itself. We will not go up that ladder tonight. But it shows what the Academy was for. It was meant to lead a person, step by step, from the many things that appear to the one thing that is.

Think of the students arriving there. Some came from rich families, some from far cities. The best known is a young man from Stagira, in the north, named Aristotle, who came to the Academy at about the age of seventeen and stayed for some twenty years. He would one day become a great philosopher in his own right, and would disagree with his teacher on many points. That is worth pausing over. A school that produces its own critics has done something right. Plato did not demand that his students repeat him. He asked them to think.

Women, too, may have studied there. Diogenes Laertius names two women among Plato's pupils, Lasthenia of Mantinea and Axiothea of Phlius. The report comes late and its details are uncertain, so we offer it with some caution. But it fits with what Plato wrote in the Republic, where he argues that women of the right nature should share in the guardianship of the city. We will meet that argument in its own place.

There is one more Sicilian chapter, and it is a sad one. Dionysius the elder died, and his son, Dionysius the younger, came to power. The tale goes that Dion, a relative of the family and a friend of Plato, hoped to turn the young ruler into a philosopher king, the very figure the Republic imagines. Plato, whom Diogenes counts among those who sailed again to Sicily, went back, and went a third time, and both times the hope came to little. The young tyrant was not the stuff of which philosopher kings are made. Dion was later murdered, and Plato was left to mourn another friend.

A Seventh Letter, which has come down under Plato's name, tells this story at length and in the first person. We should be careful with it. Scholars still argue about whether Plato wrote it, and so we use it only where we are sure, and here we are not. What we can say is that the tradition of a failed attempt to turn Sicily's rulers toward wisdom is old, and that it fits the pattern of the man.

What do we make of this? Plato had seen that cities could kill the best of men. He had tried, twice, to reform a city from the top, and failed. So he turned to the slower work of the Academy, which was the shaping of minds, one at a time, in a quiet grove. It was a kind of patience. And it shows us that when he later wrote about a city and its rulers, he was writing as a man who had both hoped and been disappointed.

In the Phaedo, Plato has Socrates speak of the soul that is purified by philosophy, and of the long practice of dying that is, in a sense, the work of a philosopher's life. The Academy was where that practice was carried on. It was a school, yes, but a school as a way of life, with its study and its walks and its meals shared and its conversation going on into the evening.

Now the lamps are being lit under the olive trees, and the students are drifting back toward the city along the road. Plato is no longer a young man. He has seen Athens at war, and a tyrant's palace, and the loss of his teacher and his friends. He has a school, and he has his thoughts. And he is beginning to set down, in a long and careful work, the question that has haunted him from the start: what is justice, and what would a just city, and a just soul, truly look like?

That work has a place and a time. It opens on a festival night, in a harbour town, with a walk down to the sea. So let us ask, as the evening settles over the Academy: where does the Republic begin?

The Republic Begins

The torches are being lit along the road from the harbour. It is evening, in a house at Piraeus, the port of Athens, some five miles from the city, and a man named Socrates is walking back up toward the town with his friend Glaucon when a slave runs up and takes hold of Glaucon's cloak. Wait for us, the boy says, because his master is coming up behind.

This is how the Republic begins, and it begins very simply. Socrates tells the story himself, in the first person, as something that happened the day before. He says he went down to the Piraeus to say a prayer to the goddess and to see how the festival would be managed, since it was a new festival. In Jowett's English, his first sentence is plain and quiet: "I went down yesterday to the Piraeus with Glaucon the son of Ariston."

You are in part four of the story of Plato and his great book. So far you have followed the young man and his teacher, the trial, and the years that led to the Academy. Now the book itself opens, and it opens in a house full of people.

The master who is coming up behind is Polemarchus, and he arrives with Adeimantus, Glaucon's brother, and others. They are friendly and a little playful. They count themselves, and they say they are stronger than Socrates, and that he must either prove stronger than all of them or stay in the port. Socrates answers that there is another way, which is to persuade them to let him go. They ask whether they could persuade him if he would not listen. Glaucon says no, and Socrates yields. So the walk home is cancelled, and they turn back toward the house.

It is worth lingering here, because this is a book about justice that begins with a small friendly use of force, and a small friendly surrender. Plato wrote nothing by accident, and readers have noticed it for centuries.

The house belongs to Polemarchus's father, a man named Cephalus. He is old, and he is wealthy. He is sitting on a cushioned seat, with a garland on his head, as if he had been offering sacrifice. He greets Socrates warmly, and complains, without any bitterness, that Socrates does not come down to see him often enough. Then he says something that sets the whole mood of the opening.

He says that as the pleasures of the body wither, the desire for conversation grows. So Socrates may come and talk with these young men, and think of the house as his own.

Socrates takes him at his word, and asks him a question that is both kind and curious. He has always liked to talk with the very old, he says, because they have gone before him on a road he may also have to travel. So what is it like? Is it a hard time of life?

Cephalus answers with great ease. Many of his friends complain, he says, that they can no longer enjoy wine, or feasting, or the other pleasures of youth, and they mourn them as though life had been taken away. But he thinks they are blaming the wrong thing. If old age were the cause, he says, then he would feel the same, and so would everyone else who has grown old. Yet he has known others who do not complain.

And he tells a short story. Someone once asked the poet Sophocles whether he was still able to love, and Sophocles replied that he was glad to have escaped from it, as from a mad and furious master. Cephalus agrees. Old age, he says, brings peace and freedom from the passions. The cause of the complaints is not age, but character. For a man of orderly and contented temper, age is only moderately a burden. For the opposite sort, youth and age are alike hard.

Now comes a moment that shows the way Socrates works. He is delighted by this, but he suspects that many people would say Cephalus bears his age so lightly only because he is rich. Cephalus does not brush the point aside. He concedes that there is something in it. He recalls the saying of Themistocles, who was told that he owed his fame to Athens rather than to himself, and who replied that a man of the small island of Seriphos would not have been famous even if he were Athenian, and that he himself would not have been famous if he had been born there. Wealth does not make a good man content, Cephalus says, but poverty and a good man together could hardly be cheerful.

Then Socrates asks the question that turns the evening. What is the greatest blessing that Cephalus has had from his wealth? And Cephalus gives a surprising answer. When a man comes near to death, he says, fears he never felt before begin to trouble him. Stories about the underworld, which he used to laugh at, how the one who has done wrong here must pay the penalty there, begin to torment his soul, and he looks back over his life to see whether he has wronged anyone. The man who finds his life full of injustice wakes up often in the night, like a child, and lives in fear. But the man who is conscious of no wrong has a sweet hope for a companion.

Here, Cephalus says, is where wealth is useful. A rich man has no need to cheat anyone, even unwillingly, and he does not need to depart for the other world in fear, owing sacrifices to a god or money to a man.

Hear what has happened. A man was asked about old age, and has ended by speaking of justice, and has defined it, without noticing, in two small pieces. Socrates seizes on this at once. Is that really what justice is, he asks? Simply to speak the truth and to pay back what one has borrowed? Or can these things sometimes be done justly and sometimes unjustly?

And he gives an example, which has become one of the most famous in philosophy. Suppose a friend, in his right mind, lent you weapons, and then went mad and asked for them back. Would you return them? Everyone would say that you ought not to, and that it would not be right to tell the whole truth to a man in that state.

Cephalus agrees that this is so. But he does not stay to pursue it. He says he must see to the sacrifices, and he hands the argument to his son Polemarchus, and goes out. It is a gentle exit, and a rather wise one. Some commentators have remarked that a man so sure of what justice is should not have been afraid to follow the question further.

Polemarchus takes over, and he proposes a saying he has from the poet Simonides, that justice is giving each man what is due to him. Socrates presses it, and it grows harder and stranger. If justice means helping friends and harming enemies, as Polemarchus eventually says, then what is the work of the just man? And can a just man ever do harm at all? Socrates draws the conclusion that it is never the work of the just man to harm anyone.

You are halfway through the first book now, and the conversation has been courteous and almost warm. The old man spoke of peace. The son gave a poet's line. And then, with no warning, the mood breaks.

Thrasymachus, who has been trying to speak for some time and has been held back by those around him, can bear it no longer. He gathers himself like a wild beast, Jowett says, and springs at them as if to tear them in pieces.

He is a sophist from Chalcedon, a teacher who takes money for speeches, and he is angry. What nonsense is this, he asks, that you have been talking all this time? Why do you play the fool, giving way to one another? If you really want to know what justice is, do not only ask questions and win praise by refuting whatever answer is given. Answer yourself, and say what you think justice is.

Socrates, quite unmoved, says that he is only too anxious to know, and that they are poor clever men who do not know, and that it is pity, not anger, they should receive from men like Thrasymachus. Thrasymachus laughs a bitter laugh and calls this the usual irony of Socrates. Then he gives his answer, and it falls into the quiet house like a stone. Justice, he says, is nothing else than the interest of the stronger.

Hold that sentence for a moment, and let it settle. In every state, Thrasymachus goes on, the rulers make the laws in their own interest, and they call just whatever serves them, and they punish as unjust whoever breaks those laws. So justice is the same everywhere, the advantage of whoever holds power.

Socrates does not hurry. He asks whether the rulers can make mistakes. Thrasymachus says they can. Then, Socrates says, they may sometimes command what is not to their advantage, and the subjects who obey would be doing what is just by his own definition, yet harming the stronger. Thrasymachus grows more and more heated, and tries to escape by saying that a true ruler, in the strict sense, never errs.

And so the argument goes on, and the questions become wider. Is the shepherd concerned for the sheep, or for the feast? Is the physician concerned for his own gain, or for the patient? Socrates argues that every art, strictly speaking, looks to the good of the thing it rules, not to its own.

Thrasymachus answers by turning the whole matter around. Injustice, he says, is stronger and nobler than justice, when it is practised on a grand scale. The petty thief is punished, but the man who robs a whole city is called fortunate and blessed. The unjust man gets more than the just, in contracts, in taxes, in office. Justice is really a kind of noble simplicity, and injustice is good counsel.

It is a ferocious speech, and it is not a stupid one. You should remember who is listening. These are young men who have lived through war, through tyranny and the return of the democracy, and who watched the city put Socrates to death. They know very well that power often does call itself justice. The question Thrasymachus asks is not strange to them at all. It is the question of the age.

Socrates keeps his temper, and he keeps returning to the same few things. He argues that the just soul lives well, and the unjust soul lives badly, and that justice is a kind of excellence, and injustice a kind of defect. By the end of the first book, Thrasymachus has fallen silent, and even blushed, a thing Socrates says he had not seen him do before. Yet Socrates himself is not satisfied. He says he has been like a glutton who snatches at every dish, and has not enjoyed the first, because he has not found out what justice is.

So the first book ends with no answer, and the evening is not over. Glaucon and Adeimantus will not let it rest. They want more than a refutation. They want to hear justice praised for its own sake, apart from its rewards and reputation. And to do that, Socrates will have to look for it somewhere larger than a single man, in a place where it can be seen written in big letters. He will have to ask what a city is for.

The City and the Soul

Picture the house of Cephalus in the evening, in the harbour town of the Piraeus. The old man has gone out to offer his sacrifice, and he has left the conversation to his son Polemarchus and to the others who came down from Athens with Socrates. Thrasymachus has just been quieted, though not entirely persuaded, and a young man named Glaucon is asking Socrates for something more. He does not want to be told that justice is good. He wants to be shown what justice does to the one who has it.

It is at this point that Socrates makes a strange proposal. Justice, he says, is hard to see in a single person, because a single person is small and the letters are small. But justice is said to belong to a whole city as well, and a city is larger. So let us watch a city come into being in words, and perhaps we will see justice and injustice come into being in it. Jowett puts the thought in the image of reading: it is as if we were asked to read small letters from a distance, and then someone noticed the same letters written elsewhere in larger size.

You should hold on to that, because it is the whole method of this part of the story. The city is not offered first as a plan for Athens or for anywhere else. It is a large drawing of a small thing. The thing being drawn is you.

So the city begins, and it begins very simply. People cannot supply all their own needs, so they gather: a farmer, a builder, a weaver, a shoemaker, each doing the one work he is fitted for. Socrates calls it the city of necessity, and he lets it be quite pleasant. The citizens eat bread and barley, drink a little wine, and sing hymns to the gods. Glaucon, though, is not charmed. He says it sounds like a city of pigs. So Socrates lets the city grow richer, with couches and perfumes and delicacies, and he remarks that this is a feverish city.

A city with luxuries needs more land, and a city that wants more land will go to war. So it needs soldiers, and soldiers who will not turn on their own people. Here Socrates reaches for a picture from the farmyard. A good watchdog is gentle to those he knows and fierce to strangers, and so the guardians of the city must be spirited toward enemies and mild toward friends. How can one nature be both? Socrates says that it must be a lover of learning, as the dog is, who knows a friend by knowing and an enemy by not knowing. He calls this a philosophical dog, and he is smiling as he says it.

Then comes the long matter of how these guardians are to be raised. The old training was gymnastic for the body and music for the soul, and Socrates keeps both. But he is careful about the stories told to children, because the young take in whatever shape is pressed upon them. He would not have the poets tell tales of gods who quarrel and deceive. This is the first place where a modern listener may stop and frown, and it is fair to say so. Plato's Socrates is willing to supervise the poets, and the Republic later sends them out of the city in a famous passage that has been argued over ever since.

Now the story turns inward, and this is the part that matters most. Having found the city's parts, Socrates asks what justice is within it. The city, he says, has three kinds of people: the rulers who deliberate, the auxiliaries who defend, and the producers who supply what everyone needs. Justice, he concludes, is each of these doing its own work and not meddling with the work of the others.

And now the small letters are read. If the city has three parts, perhaps the soul does too. Socrates asks whether we learn with one part, feel anger with another, and desire food and drink and love with a third. He gives a test. The same thing cannot do and undergo opposite things at the same time, in the same respect, toward the same object. Yet a thirsty man may be pulled toward the water and held back from it at once. So something in him pulls, and something in him forbids, and these cannot be one thing.

The part that desires is the appetite. The part that reasons and forbids is reason. And there is a third, spirit, the seat of anger and shame and a kind of honour. Socrates tells of Leontius, who was walking up from the Piraeus and saw some corpses lying beside the executioner. He wanted to look, and he also felt disgust and turned away. At last, overpowered by the desire, he ran up to the bodies, opening his eyes wide, and said to them, "Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight." Here, says Socrates, is anger fighting against appetite, and so spirit is a thing apart, and it is on the side of reason when the soul is healthy.

So a just person is one in whom reason rules, spirit helps, and appetite obeys, each in its own place. Jowett renders the result as a kind of harmony, like the notes of a scale, the high and the low and the middle bound together until the man is one and not many. Injustice, then, is a kind of civil war inside the self. Keep that sentence, because it is the quiet answer to Glaucon's question, and the rest of the evening will lean on it.

Now we must go back to the city, and to something Socrates says that has made readers uneasy for a very long time. The rulers need a way to make the citizens care for the city and for one another. Socrates proposes a kind of story, which he himself calls a noble lie, a single bold fiction. The citizens are told that all their upbringing was a dream, and that in truth they were being fashioned inside the earth, their mother, who then sent them up. They are all brothers, born of one soil. But the god who made them mixed gold into some, silver into others, and iron and bronze into the rest, and so the rulers must watch what metal each child is made of, whatever his parents were. A child of gold may be born to bronze parents, and must be raised up. A child of bronze may be born to gold, and must be set down.

Socrates is not at ease telling it. He says he hardly knows where to find the courage or the words. And what should we make of it? Here we should be honest, and a little careful.

The story is plainly meant as a story, inside the conversation. It is an invention about an imagined city, told by a man who knows it is false, and told to make people feel kinship. Scholars have never agreed on how seriously Plato meant it for the world outside the page. Some read it as a hard truth about how rulers manage their people. Others read it as an irony, a way of showing us how fragile any city is that is built on a falsehood. What is certain is that it sits within a dialogue whose whole purpose is to ask what justice really is, and that Socrates himself calls the thing a lie.

There is something else worth noticing in the tale. The metals are not fixed by birth. Children are to be sorted by their nature, and this includes women, who in this city are to share the guardians' work and training, a notion Socrates admits will be laughed at. The guardians, moreover, are to own nothing and to live in common, so that no ruler can grow rich from ruling. Socrates says the aim is not that one class should be happy beyond the rest, but that the whole city should be as happy as possible.

And who should rule such a city? Not those who are eager for it. Socrates says that the city will be well governed only if the rulers are the ones least in love with power, and he adds that philosophers must either become kings or kings become philosophers, or there will be no rest from troubles for cities, nor for humankind. Glaucon warns that such a sentence will bring a crowd running at them with whatever weapons come to hand. Socrates goes on regardless. He believes that only one who truly knows what is good can rule justly.

Which leaves a question that should sit quietly in the dark with you. If philosophers are to rule, what is it they know that others do not? They have not merely more opinions. They are said to have seen something. Socrates says they are lovers of the sight of truth. But how does a person come to see truly, and what is it that he sees? That is what Socrates must try to say next.

The Sun and the Line

Glaucon, son of Ariston, will not let Socrates go. It is the long night of conversation in the house of Polemarchus, down at the Piraeus, the harbour town below Athens, and the lamps have burned low. Glaucon is young, quick and a little stubborn, and he is the brother of the man who is writing all of this down.

Socrates has just said something large. He has said that the guardians of the city must come to know the good, because every other thing becomes useful only through its relation to it. Now Glaucon wants to be told what the good is.

Socrates does not answer at once. He first points out how badly people have answered before. Most people say the good is pleasure. The more refined say it is knowledge. But when the refined are asked knowledge of what, they cannot say, and in the end they answer that it is knowledge of the good, which is going round in a circle.

Glaucon is not satisfied with a gentle laugh at other people's muddles. He reminds Socrates that he gave an account of justice, and asks for the same kind of account of the good.

Here Socrates does something that surprises many first-time readers. He declines. He says he fears he cannot do it, and that he would only disgrace himself by trying. What he will do instead is describe the offspring of the good, the child that most resembles its parent.

You are in the middle of the Republic now, in Book Six, and the story is about to climb. Behind us lie the city built in speech and the three parts of the soul. Ahead lies the cave. In between sit two images, the sun and the line, and they are the stairs that lead up to it.

First, the sun. Socrates says that the offspring of the good is the sun. Then he explains why, slowly, as a teacher would who wants you to see it for yourself.

Think of how seeing works, he says. We have eyes, and things have colours, but that is not enough. There must be a third thing, light. And light comes from the sun. The sun is not the same as sight, but it is the cause of sight, and the eye, of all the organs of sense, is the one most like the sun.

Now he draws the comparison. What the good is in the world that the mind can reach, the sun is in the world that the eyes can reach. The good stands to the mind and to the things the mind knows as the sun stands to the eye and to the things the eye sees.

He asks you to remember what happens at dusk. When colours are caught in twilight, the eyes grow dim and seem almost blind. When the sun shines on them, the same eyes see clearly. The soul is the same. When it fixes on things lit by truth and being, it understands and knows. When it turns to things mixed with darkness, things that come to be and pass away, it has only opinions, and it shifts about.

So the good does two things at once. It gives truth to the things that are known, and it gives the power of knowing to the one who knows. Light does the same for the visible world. It makes things seeable, and it makes the eye able to see.

But the sun does more than give light. Socrates reminds Glaucon that the sun is also the source of generation, growth and nourishment, though it is not itself any of these things. Then he takes the last step, and it is the boldest sentence in the passage. The objects of knowledge receive from the good not only their being known but their very being and essence, and yet, in Jowett's words, the good itself "far exceeds essence in dignity and power."

You may feel, listening, that something has been said that is hard to hold. Glaucon felt it too. Jowett's version has him cry out in an almost comic solemnity, something like "by the light of heaven, how amazing." The wording differs from translator to translator, but the feeling is the same in all of them. Glaucon laughs, and then he asks Socrates to go on.

It helps to be plain about what the image does and does not claim. Socrates does not say here that the good is a god, or a person, or a place. He says it is to the mind what the sun is to the eye, the thing that makes understanding possible at all. How exactly that works, Plato leaves for later readers to argue over, and they have been arguing for more than two thousand years.

Glaucon asks him not to stop, and Socrates agrees to give a second image, which he calls a line.

Picture a line drawn on the ground. Cut it into two unequal parts. Then cut each part again, in the same proportion as the first cut. One long part stands for the visible world. The other stands for the world of the mind. And each is divided again into two grades of clearness.

Begin at the bottom. The lowest section of the visible world holds images. These are shadows, and reflections in water and in smooth bright surfaces. The next section holds the things that cast them, the animals around us, the plants, and everything that people make. The shadow of a horse is a likeness of the horse. The horse is a likeness, in turn, of something truer still, but Socrates comes to that by degrees.

Now cross into the world of the mind. Here the lower section belongs to those who think as mathematicians do, and Socrates takes geometry as his example. The geometer draws a square in the dust, and draws its diagonal. But he is not really thinking about the marks in the dust. He is thinking about the square itself and the diagonal itself, of which the drawing is only an image.

He begins, too, from assumptions. He says, let there be odd and even, let there be three kinds of angle, and he treats these as plain to everyone. He does not stop to give an account of them. He goes forward from them to his conclusions.

Then comes the highest section, and it is the hardest to describe. Here the mind also uses assumptions, but it does not rest on them. It treats them as steps, as footings for a climb, and it goes up through them to something that needs no assumption, the first principle of everything. Socrates calls this way of going dialectic. The mind in this section makes no use of drawn figures at all. It moves from idea to idea, and ends in ideas.

So there are four sections, and Socrates gives each a name for the state of mind that belongs to it. In Jowett's rendering they are intelligence for the highest, understanding for the second, faith or conviction for the third, and the perception of shadows for the last. Other translators choose other English words, so if you meet this passage elsewhere, the labels may look different while the order stays the same.

The labels are less important than the direction. The line says that clearness comes in degrees, and that the clearness of the mind's grasp matches the truth of what it grasps. Someone who sees only shadows is not stupid. He is simply at the bottom of the line, and has never been turned around.

This is why Plato took such trouble, back in Book Five, to say what knowledge is and how it differs from opinion. There Socrates meets the lovers of sights and sounds, people who delight in fine colours and fine voices and fine shapes, but who cannot bear to be told that there is such a thing as beauty itself. Knowledge, he says, is set over what is. Ignorance is set over what is not. And opinion lies between, because its objects both are and are not, in the way the many beautiful things of this world are beautiful in one light and plain in another.

Listen again, and you will hear that the sun and the line say one thing in two ways. In the sun, the good is the source of both light and sight. In the line, the mind climbs from the likeness to the thing, and from the thing to the idea, and from the idea toward the good. In both, there is a lower region where things seem solid but are only borrowed, and a higher region where things are fewer, and more clear, and more real.

Glaucon, who has followed all this with great patience, understands that something has been described, a kind of upward road. But he has not yet been shown what it is like to be on it. He has been told about shadows and reflections and the bright light, but only in the cool language of proportions. What he does not have is a picture of a person, with a body and a back and a pair of aching eyes, who actually has to make the climb.

Socrates sees this. The night is still long, and the lamps are low, and he decides to give Glaucon a story instead of a diagram. He asks him to imagine human beings living underground, in a kind of den, with their faces turned to a wall. And the question that sits waiting at the mouth of that den is a simple one. What is the cave?

The Cave

On some evening in Piraeus, at the edge of a great city, a few men sit close together in a low room, with a lamp burning. Glaucon is there, and Socrates, and the talk has just come from the sun and the divided line. Now Socrates says he will try a picture, and he asks Glaucon to imagine something.

He asks him to imagine human beings living in an underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light, and a passage running all the way up to it. The people in this den have been there since childhood. Their legs and necks are chained, so that they cannot move, and they can only see in front of them, because the chains prevent them turning their heads round.

So they sit facing the back wall of the cave. That is all they have ever faced.

Behind them, and a good way above, a fire is blazing. Between the fire and the prisoners there is a raised way, and along that way Socrates asks Glaucon to see a low wall built, like the screen which marionette players have in front of them, over which they show their puppets.

Now, along this wall, other men are carrying all sorts of vessels and statues and figures of animals, made of wood and stone and various materials. Some of the carriers are talking, and some are silent.

Glaucon says it is a strange image, and they are strange prisoners. And Socrates gives the answer that the whole picture turns on. They are like ourselves, he says.

Hold the scene a moment. The prisoners are facing the wall. The fire is behind them. The carriers pass, and the things they carry throw their shadows on the wall in front. The prisoners have never seen anything of themselves or of one another, except the shadows which the fire throws on the opposite wall of the cave.

Then Socrates asks a plain question. If the prisoners were able to talk with one another, would they not suppose that they were naming what was actually before them? And suppose the prison had an echo from the wall opposite. When one of the passers by spoke, would they not imagine that the voice came from the passing shadow?

To them, Socrates says, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images.

You may notice how ordinary the prisoners are. They are not stupid, and they are not wicked. They are simply placed. They have names for the shadows, and they are clever at them. The story will say, a little later, that they give honours to the one who sees the passing shadows most sharply, and remembers which came first, and which after, and which together. That is a quiet touch. Even in the dark there is a kind of skill, and a kind of pride.

Now Socrates asks Glaucon to consider what would happen if the prisoners were released and cured of their folly. Let one of them be freed, and compelled suddenly to stand up and turn his neck round and walk and look towards the light.

It would hurt him. Socrates says he would suffer sharp pains, and the glare would make him unable to see the realities of which in his former state he had seen the shadows. And if someone told him that what he had seen before was an illusion, and that now he was approaching real being and had a truer sight, what would he answer?

Socrates goes on. Suppose his instructor pointed to the objects passing by and compelled him to name them. Would he not be perplexed, and imagine that the shadows which he had formerly seen were truer than the objects which were now shown to him?

This is a very human thing, and Plato has put it in on purpose. The man is not grateful at once. He is confused, and he wants to go back. If he is forced to look at the fire itself, Socrates says, his eyes will hurt him, and he will turn away and take refuge in the things which he is able to see, and will think them truly clearer than the things which are now being shown to him.

And now the story takes the freed man further, up the steep and rugged ascent. Socrates says he is dragged by force, and not let go until he is brought out into the light of the sun. There he will be pained and irritated, and when he approaches the light his eyes will be dazzled, and he will not be able to see anything at all of what are now called realities.

So the way up is slow, and Plato tells it slowly. First, Socrates says, he will want to grow accustomed to the sight of the upper world. At first he will see the shadows best, next the reflections of men and other objects in the water, and then the objects themselves. After that he will gaze upon the light of the moon and the stars and the spangled heaven, which he will find easier to see by night than the sun or the light of the sun by day.

Last of all he will be able to see the sun, and not mere reflections of him in the water, but the sun himself, in his own proper place, and he will contemplate him as he is.

We are in the middle of the story now. A man who once knew only shadows on a wall has climbed out, and stands under an open sky. Next comes what Socrates says the man will understand, and then what he will do.

He will reason about the sun, Socrates says, and conclude that this is he who gives the seasons and the years, and is the guardian of all that is in the visible world, and in a certain way the cause of all things which he and his fellows have been accustomed to behold. You will hear in this the sun of the last part, the image of the good. Plato is joining his pictures together, and the listener is meant to feel them click.

And then Socrates asks about the memory of the cave. When the man remembers his old habitation, and the wisdom of the den, and his fellow prisoners, would he not consider himself happy on the change, and pity them? He would. Socrates adds that if there had been honours and glories among the prisoners, he would care nothing for them. He would rather, in the words of Homer, be a poor man's servant, and endure anything, than think as they do and live after their manner.

That line of Homer comes from the Odyssey, where the shade of Achilles says it in the underworld. Plato's readers would have known it at once, and heard the weight in it.

Now the story turns, and this is the turn which gives the whole picture its meaning. Socrates asks Glaucon to consider this. If the man were to come down again into the den and take his old seat, would he not find his eyes full of darkness, coming suddenly out of the sun?

He would. And if there were a contest, and he had to compete in measuring the shadows with the prisoners who had never moved out of the den, while his sight was still weak and his eyes had not become steady, and the time needed for getting accustomed to the dark was not short, would he not be ridiculous?

Socrates says men would say of him that he had gone up and come down without his eyes. They would say it was better not even to think of ascending. And if anyone tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.

Those are quiet words in the text, but they carry a shadow of their own. Plato's first readers knew very well that an Athenian jury had once put a man to death for troubling the city with his questions. Whether Plato meant the line as a direct memory of Socrates is something readers have long weighed, and it is fair to say that the text never says so itself. But the echo is there, and it has been heard for more than two thousand years.

Now, having told the picture, Socrates does something Plato does not always do. He explains it, at least in part. Dear Glaucon, he says, the whole of this image may be compared to the previous argument. The prison is the world of sight. The light of the fire is the sun. And the journey upwards, if you interpret it as the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world, will be a correct statement of his hope and fear.

He is careful, though, about how sure he is. God knows whether it is true, he says, and he gives only what appears to him. It is a modest way to hand over a great thought. Plato lets his teacher say, in effect, here is how it looks from where I stand.

And then comes the part of the explanation that matters most. In the world of knowledge, he says, the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort. And when seen, it is inferred to be the universal author of all things beautiful and right, parent of light and of the lord of light in this visible world, and the immediate source of reason and truth in the intellectual. This is the one, he says, who must have his eye fixed upon it, who would act rationally either in public or private life.

It is worth pausing on what this does and does not claim. The cave is a picture of education. Socrates says elsewhere in the same passage that education is not what some people declare it to be. They say they can put knowledge into the soul, as if they were putting sight into blind eyes. But the true view, he says, is that the power and capacity of learning exists already in the soul, and that the whole body must be turned round, like the eye turned from darkness to light, until the soul can endure the sight of being, and of the brightest and best of being. Education, then, is the art of this turning round. It is not the art of implanting sight.

You may feel the gentleness of that. The prisoner is not filled up like a jar. He is turned, and the turning is painful, and it is slow, and it needs someone to lead him. But the eye was always there.

And the story is not finished, because Socrates does not let the freed man simply stay in the light. Those who have reached the heights, he says, will not be willing to descend to the labours and honours of the cave, for their souls are ever hastening into the upper world where they desire to dwell. That is natural enough. Who would go back? But Socrates says it is the duty of the founders of the city to compel the best minds to attain the highest knowledge, to look upon the good, and to ascend. And when they have ascended and seen enough, they must not be allowed to do as they do now, and remain in the upper world. They must be made to go down again among the prisoners, and share their labours and their honours, whether they are worth having or not.

Glaucon objects. Will this not be unjust to them? Will we give them a worse life, when they might have a better? And Socrates answers that the law is not concerned with the special happiness of any one class in the state, but with the happiness of the whole. So the ascent was never meant to end in the sunlight alone.

You are now very near the end of the picture. A man has been unchained, turned, led up the steep way, taught to see slowly, and has looked at the sun. And now he is being asked, by his own city and by his own sense of what is right, to go back down into the dark, among the very people who once lived beside him in chains. And Socrates has said, in the plainest words, how some of them will meet him.

What will it be to walk into that room again, with his sight still dazzled, and the old prisoners waiting in their places?

What Plato Did Not Say

Late on an autumn afternoon in a modern university, a teacher stands at the front of a lecture room, a lamp glowing on the desk. A teacher stands at the front, a lamp glowing on the desk, and she asks her students a simple question. What is the cave? Hands go up, and the answers come quickly. The cave is the television. The cave is the telephone in your pocket. The cave is the feed you scroll through at night. She smiles, and then she opens a book to a page that is, in the old translation, a little yellowed, and she says: let us see what the man actually wrote.

This part of the story is about that gap, the gap between what Plato said and what we have come to think he said. You have heard the cave told in full. Now we walk slowly around it and look at what has been added to it since.

Begin with the screens. It is a lovely comparison, and it is not wicked to make it. The prisoners sit in a row, facing a wall, watching shapes move in firelight. Anyone who has watched a glowing rectangle for too long will feel the resemblance. But look at how Plato builds his cave. In the telling, the prisoners are chained from childhood, so that they cannot turn their heads, and behind them, between the fire and the prisoners, men carry statues and figures of animals along a raised road, and the shadows of these are what the prisoners see. The shadows come from objects. The objects are made by people, and the people are not hiding anything for profit.

Plato's point is not that someone has designed a clever distraction. Nothing in the text says the puppeteers are cheating the prisoners, or even that they know the prisoners are there. The text says only that some of the carriers speak and some are silent. The chains are not a trick played by a villain. They are simply the condition of the prisoners. That is a different kind of claim from the modern one, which is usually about being manipulated by someone.

Then there is the matter of what the prisoners are, in Plato's own words. When the speaker Socrates has laid out the picture, his companion Glaucon says that it is a strange image and strange prisoners. And Socrates answers: "Like ourselves." It is a short line, in the Jowett translation of the Republic, and it carries the whole force of the image. The prisoners are not other people, the ones who watch too much. They are us, all of us, in our ordinary state.

So the cave is not a warning about a technology. It is a picture of ordinary human understanding, which takes the first things it is shown for the whole of what is real. A child in Athens, twenty-four hundred years ago, with no screen of any kind, was in the cave just as surely as we are. The comparison with screens is not forbidden. But if it is the only thing we take from the passage, we have taken the smallest part.

There is a second thing Plato did not say, and it concerns the word that people most want to attach to the cave: the word truth, set against the word illusion. Plato does say that the freed prisoner is pained by the light and wants to turn back to the shadows he can see clearly. But he also says something odd that is easy to miss. The ones who are still in the cave honour and praise those who are quickest at spotting the passing shadows and best at guessing which will come next. The cave is not a place of stupid people. It has its own skills, its own prizes, its own experts. It is a world that works perfectly well on its own terms, and that is exactly the difficulty.

Now we come to the ideal city, and here the misreadings run in both directions. The Republic is built around a city made in speech. Socrates and his friends say, in the early going, that they will look at justice first in the large, in a city, because it may be easier to read there than in a single soul. Then they build the city, and what they build is strange to our ears. Rulers who hold no private property. Children raised in common. Poets watched closely, or sent away. Some readers have looked at all this and seen a blueprint, a programme for a state to be carried out.

The earliest famous charge of that kind came from a pupil of Plato. Aristotle, in his Politics, goes through the Republic's proposals one at a time and finds them wanting, the common ownership of wives and children most of all. For Aristotle these were real proposals, to be tested against how people actually behave, and he thought they would fail. That is one old way of reading the book, and it has never gone away.

In the last two centuries the same reading was turned against Plato with great heat. After the terrible years of the twentieth century, some writers looked at the Republic and saw a forerunner of the closed, controlled state. The philosopher Karl Popper, in a book written in the nineteen forties, called Plato an enemy of the open society. It was a serious and angry argument, written by a man who had watched a continent burn, and it has shaped how many people first meet the book.

But other readers have said that this misses something Plato himself puts on the page. In the ninth book, Glaucon asks whether such a city could ever exist on earth, and Socrates replies that it does not matter, because it is laid up as a pattern in heaven, for anyone who wishes to look at it and found a city in himself. Mark what that says. The city is a pattern for looking at, and the city that most needs founding is the one inside a person.

Earlier, in the fifth book, Socrates is plainer still about the limits of the exercise. He says that a painter who paints a beautiful model of a man is not a worse painter if he cannot show that such a man could exist. The picture is judged as a picture. So the question of whether Plato meant it to be built has, honestly, two long answers, and scholars have given both.

Here is a quieter point that often gets lost. Within the book, the whole city is begun to answer a question about justice, and the question is put by the brothers Glaucon and Adeimantus, who want Socrates to show that being just is good for the person who is just, even if no one ever sees it. The city exists to serve that answer. If you forget the question, the city looks like a political manifesto. If you remember it, the city looks like a lens held up to the soul.

Now to what scholars still argue about, because it is a good deal. First, the matter of whether the speaker is Plato. The Republic is a dialogue. Plato never says "I" in it, and Socrates, the chief speaker, was a real man who left no writing of his own. So when Socrates says something strange in the book, how much of it did the historical Socrates believe, how much did Plato, and how much is simply a character in a drama? Scholars divide, and the evidence does not settle it.

Second, there is the matter of the famous unwritten teachings. Aristotle reports that Plato taught certain doctrines that he did not put into his dialogues, and that these concerned the Good and what he called the One. Some scholars in the German school have built a whole reading of Plato around this. Others think the report is too thin to carry so much weight. If it is true, then the cave and the sun are a pointer to a teaching we only half have. If it is not, they are the whole of what he meant to say.

Third, there is a plain puzzle about the cave itself. The freed prisoner is forced back down, and Socrates says the guardians must be made to return and share the labours of the city, even though they would rather stay above. Yet in an earlier part of the book Socrates has argued that the just life is the happier one. So is it a hardship to go back or not? Readers have wrestled with this for as long as there have been readers, and thoughtful people still disagree about whether Plato resolves it.

Fourth, there is the matter of the noble lie, which we met earlier. Socrates proposes that the citizens be told a story, that they were born from the earth and that the gods put gold, silver or bronze into their souls. Was he joking? Was he serious? Was it a comment on the way all cities tell founding stories about themselves? The text does not say, and so the argument continues in good faith.

Fifth, and most delicate, there is the question of Plato's own life and what it tells us. There is a letter, the Seventh Letter, in which a writer who gives his name as Plato describes his hopes for a just government and his dismay at what Athens did to Socrates. Many scholars treat it as genuine. Others have doubted it, and we have said we would use it only where certain. We are not certain enough to lean on it, and so we leave it where it stands, a possible voice, not a proved one.

And there is a last thing to say about the way Plato was remembered, because it touches the story of his life as much as his books. Diogenes Laertius, writing many centuries after Plato died, tells us that his teacher Socrates once dreamed of a young swan on his knees, which sprouted wings and flew away singing, and that the next day Plato was brought to him. It is a charming tale. Diogenes also tells us that Plato's real name was Aristocles, and that Platon was a nickname given for his broad build. But Diogenes was writing about six hundred years after the fact, and he says himself that he is setting down what others reported. A good teller keeps that distance in mind: a lovely story, told late, from sources we cannot see.

So what has this part done? It has taken away a few things that were never in the cave. It was never only a story about screens. It was never a charge against ordinary people for being foolish. It was never a plain order to build a perfect state. And in taking those away it has left the harder things where they were, unresolved, and honest about that.

The Jowett translation, which we have been using, gives the cave in plain, rather stately English, and it has served readers well for well over a century. But no translation can tell you which of these arguments is the right one. That belongs to the reader, who is allowed, at the end of a long evening, to lay the book down without being sure.

Let the lamp go dim on the lecture room, and the students file out into the dark. They came with a quick answer, and they leave with a slower question, and the teacher is content with that. For Plato's own book ends not on a rule but on a story, and a story is a thing you carry to bed with you. Which brings us to the question the next part will ask. When all the misreadings have been cleared away, and all the arguments left standing, what does the book leave a reader?

The Quiet Question

On the twelfth day after the battle, on a funeral pile somewhere in the country of Pamphylia, a dead soldier opened his eyes. His name was Er, the son of Armenius, and the people gathered around him had come to burn his body.

Ten days earlier, when the fallen were collected, the other bodies had already begun to decay. His had not. So his family carried him home for burial, and laid him on the pile, and waited.

You are near the end of the Republic now, in the tenth and last book. Socrates is still walking and talking with Glaucon and the others, and the long night of argument is almost over. He has one more story to tell, and he tells it as a thing Er reported when he came back.

Er said that his soul had travelled with a crowd of others until they came to a strange place. There were two openings in the earth, side by side, and two openings in the sky above them. Between them sat judges.

When the judges had decided a case, the just were sent upward through the sky, on the right hand, with a sign of the verdict fastened in front of them. The unjust were sent downward, on the left, with the record of their deeds fastened behind.

Er himself was not sent anywhere. He was told that he must be the messenger, to carry word of this place to the living, and that he should listen and watch everything.

So he watched. Souls came up out of the earth, dusty and travel-worn, and others came down out of heaven, clean. They greeted one another like people arriving at a festival, and asked about each other's journeys.

The accounts they gave were sorrowful and sweet by turns. Those who had been below told of a journey of a thousand years. For every wrong a soul had done, it paid a penalty tenfold, once in each hundred years, because a hundred years was taken as the length of a human life.

Socrates gives one famous case. A tyrant called Ardiaeus, who had murdered and ruled cruelly, came to the mouth of the way upward, and the opening roared and would not let him through. Fierce men, glowing like fire, seized him and others like him and dragged them away.

Then the story grows quieter. The souls who passed the test rested seven days in a meadow, and on the eighth they set out again. After four days of travel they came to a place where they could see a straight shaft of light stretching through the whole of heaven and earth, like a pillar.

At the centre of that light hung the Spindle of Necessity, on which all the heavenly circles turn. On each circle stood a Siren, singing a single note, and the notes together made one harmony.

Around the spindle sat three women, the daughters of Necessity. They were called Lachesis, Clotho and Atropos, and they were the Fates, who sing of what was, what is, and what will be.

Now comes the part of the myth that Socrates cares about most. A spokesman for Lachesis took lots and patterns of lives from her knees, and spoke to the waiting souls. He told them that each would draw a number, and that the one who drew first would choose first. He told them too that the virtue a soul chose would not be assigned to it.

He said that the blame belongs to the one who chooses, and that the god is free from blame.

Then the patterns of lives were spread on the ground before them, far more than the souls who were present. There were lives of every kind, the lives of animals and the lives of men, lives of tyrants and lives of beggars, lives of the strong and the weak, the famous and the unknown.

Here Socrates stops the tale, and speaks to Glaucon directly. This, he says, is the greatest danger for a human being. Each of us should therefore give up other studies, and look for the person who can teach us to tell a good life from a bad one, and always to choose the better one that is open to us.

Er saw what happened next, and it is a strange and humbling sight. The soul that drew the first lot came forward at once and chose the greatest tyranny, greedily and without thinking. Only later, when he looked more closely, did he see that it held terrible things within it, and he wept.

Socrates says this soul had come down from heaven. In his earlier life he had lived in a well-ordered city and had been good by habit, but without philosophy.

So even those who have been rewarded, he suggests, are not safe. Being good by custom alone is not the same as knowing what is good, and a soul without that knowledge may choose badly when the choice is finally its own.

Then Er watched souls choose in their own manner. The soul of Orpheus chose the life of a swan, because he hated women after his death at their hands. Thamyras chose a nightingale. Ajax chose a lion, and Agamemnon an eagle. Atalanta chose the honours of an athlete. And Thersites, the man who mocked kings in the Iliad, chose the body of an ape.

The last soul to come forward was Odysseus. He had been cured of ambition by the memory of his labours, and he went about for a long time looking for the life of an ordinary man who minds his own affairs. He found it lying neglected, and said that he would have chosen it gladly even if he had drawn the first lot.

When all had chosen, the souls were led to the Fates, who made each choice fixed. Then they travelled through the heat to the plain of Forgetfulness, and encamped by the river of Unmindfulness, whose water no vessel can hold. Each soul had to drink a measure of it, and those who were not wise drank more than the measure.

Er alone was forbidden to drink. He did not know how he came back into his body. He only knew that he opened his eyes at dawn and found himself lying on the pile.

And that is how the story ends. Socrates says that the tale has been saved and has not perished, and that if we are persuaded by it, it may save us too, and we shall cross the river of Forgetfulness without defiling the soul.

His counsel is simple. We should hold always to the upward way, and practise justice together with wisdom, believing the soul to be immortal and able to bear every good and every evil. He ends by hoping it will go well with us here, and also in the pilgrimage of a thousand years that he has described.

See what Socrates has done. Earlier in this same book he gave an argument that the soul is not destroyed by its own badness, as the body is destroyed by disease. The argument was meant to prepare the way for the myth, though the myth itself is told as a story, and not offered as proof.

Notice, too, how the Republic began. Its very first words are of a man going down, "I went down yesterday to the Piraeus." The book that began with a descent to the harbour ends with a soul coming back from the other world, and with a choice that is still open to anyone who is listening.

Earlier, speaking of the city built in words, Socrates said that a pattern of it is laid up in heaven for anyone who wishes to look at it, and that such a person might set his own house in order by it. It does not matter whether the city exists anywhere on earth.

As for Plato himself, the old biographer Diogenes Laertius says he lived to the age of eighty-one, and died at a wedding feast. He was buried in the Academy, the garden school where he had spent so much of his life.

The same biographer says that tablets were found after his death, with the opening of the Republic written out again and again in different arrangements. If that is true, then even at the end he was still working at the very first sentence.

Let all of that go now. The cave, the sun, the harbour, the argument, the long road, they have been told, and they will be there in the morning if you want them.

For now there is only the plain, and the evening light on the river, and the quiet. The souls are resting. The spindle turns slowly, and the sirens sing one steady note.

You have nowhere to go. Your breathing is slow, and your body is heavy, and the day has been put down. Whatever you chose today, it is already behind you, and whatever waits tomorrow has not yet been drawn.

Let the river be quiet. Let the light go down. Let the story close around you like warm water, and let yourself sleep.
