Cold open

A man named Balinus stands in a dark vault with a lamp in his hand. He has come down a stair beneath an old statue, and he has been searching for this place for a long time. In the middle of the room sits the body of an old man, and in the dead man's hands there is a tablet of green stone.

That is the story. It is told in an Arabic book called the Book of the Secret of Creation, which scholars usually place in the eighth or ninth century. The old man, the book says, was Hermes. The tablet was made of emerald. And written on it, in a few short sentences, was the secret of how the world holds together.

Settle into that picture for a moment, because it is a good one. But now the first thing to learn. No such tablet has ever been found. No emerald, no vault, no body, and no ancient slab of any kind carrying these words. The text exists only in manuscripts, copied by hand, first in Arabic and later in Latin.

So what do the words say? They are very short, and the best known part is a single idea. What is below is like what is above, and what is above is like what is below, and together they work the wonder of one thing. In later centuries that idea was squeezed into five words, as above, so below. You will find them printed on book covers, carved into jewellery, and written on the skin of people who have never read a page of the old texts.

The tablet was taken seriously by serious people. Among the papers of Isaac Newton there is an English translation of it, in his own hand, which he made while studying alchemy. Alchemists copied it for centuries, and each of them read it a little differently.

Now a second thing, and it is important to keep the two apart. The Emerald Tablet is not the main body of Hermetic writing. That body is a set of Greek treatises, philosophical and religious, written in the first centuries of the Common Era. Those are the Hermetica proper, and the tablet is a much later, much smaller relative. In the year sixteen fourteen, a scholar named Isaac Casaubon showed that these Greek writings were not as ancient as their admirers believed. We will come to that argument in its own time.

For now, hold on to three plain facts. The legend is beautiful. The object never existed. And the words still travel, because a few lines about how the small mirrors the great can mean almost anything a listener brings to them.

What this programme will do is slow down. It will go back before the Arabic book, before the Latin, before Newton, to the Greek and Egyptian world where the name on that tablet was first spoken. It will ask what the old writings really teach, who wrote them, and what they never claimed.

And it begins with the name itself, a strange and rather grand one, which no one in the legend explains. Where did the name Hermes Trismegistus come from?

Two Gods Become One

The harbour at Alexandria is going quiet at dusk, in the days when Egypt was ruled by Greek kings. A ship has come in from the islands, and a sailor steps onto the stone quay, glad to be on land. Before he does anything else, he touches a small stone pillar standing at the edge of the road. It is a square stone, topped with a head, and below the head there is little more than a carved shape. The sailor is a Greek, and he knows this pillar well. It is a herm, a marker of the god Hermes, who watches over roads, travellers and thresholds.

Tonight you are going to follow that god a long way. This is the first part of the story of Hermes Trismegistus, and it begins with a puzzle. How did a Greek god of roads become the author of books of Egyptian wisdom? To see it, we start with the god the sailor knew.

In the oldest Greek poetry, Hermes is a young and clever god. The Homeric Hymn to Hermes, a poem of the archaic period, tells how he was born in a cave on a mountain in Arcadia, to the nymph Maia and to Zeus. On the very day he was born, the hymn says, he climbed out of his cradle, found a tortoise, and made from its shell the first lyre. That same day he stole cattle belonging to his brother Apollo, and then lied about it with a straight face.

So the Greek Hermes begins as a trickster and a maker. But he grew into much more. In the Iliad, Hermes guides the old king Priam through the night to the tent of Achilles. In the Odyssey, he is the messenger who carries the commands of Zeus. He also leads the souls of the dead down to the underworld. A god who crosses boundaries, who carries words from one side to another, who guides the lost: that is the Hermes the sailor was touching on the quay.

Now turn from the harbour and walk inland, up the Nile, to a very different god. His name, in Egyptian, is written in a way modern scholars render as Djehuty, and the Greeks said it as Thoth. He is shown with the head of an ibis, a long-billed wading bird, or sometimes as a baboon. And Thoth is the god of writing. In Egyptian belief, he is the scribe of the gods, the one who measures time, keeps the records, and speaks words that have power. Where the Greeks said that a clever god invented the lyre, the Egyptians said that Thoth gave humanity the written word.

You might think the two gods have little in common, and in some ways that is true. One is a young, quick, laughing thief. The other is a patient, ibis-headed scribe. But look at what they do. Both carry messages. Both are linked with speech and cleverness. Both have a role in the passage of souls after death. In Egyptian funerary scenes, Thoth stands beside the scales on which the heart of the dead person is weighed, and writes down the result. Hermes, in Greek poetry, leads the dead on their way. A Greek who looked at Thoth saw a god who seemed to do much of what Hermes did.

This is exactly what the Greeks of that age tended to do. The historian Herodotus, who visited Egypt in the fifth century before the common era, wrote that the Egyptians had gods whom he named by Greek names. It was an old habit of mind. To a Greek, a foreign god was often his own god under another name. Scholars call this practice interpretation, and the clearest later example is the Greek name for the town of Thoth's cult in Middle Egypt: the Greeks called it Hermopolis, the city of Hermes. Notice that they did not name it after a new god. They simply gave the city the name of the Greek god they felt was the same one.

The turning point came in the year three hundred and thirty-two before the common era, when Alexander of Macedon entered Egypt and founded Alexandria. After his death, his general Ptolemy took Egypt, and his family ruled it for almost three hundred years. Think about what that meant. For the first time, large numbers of Greeks were living in Egypt, in cities, in government offices, in the great library and its scholars' quarter. They lived beside Egyptians who kept their own temples, their own priests, and their own very old writings.

In such a place, two peoples did not merely coexist. They mixed. Greeks learned Egyptian customs, and some Egyptians learned to read and write in Greek. The result was a culture that was neither purely one nor the other. And in this culture, the two gods slowly became one. Writers of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods speak of Hermes Thoth as a single figure. In the Greek-speaking world of Egypt, the ibis-headed scribe and the road-walking messenger were two faces of a single divine mind, a god of words, of learning, and of the road between worlds.

Here a deeper idea begins to appear. If this god is the god of writing, then books belong to him. And if he is a god of wisdom, then books of wisdom might be his own books. Egyptian tradition already held that Thoth was the author of sacred writings. Later Greek writers took the thought further and spoke of whole libraries of Hermes. The Christian writer Clement of Alexandria, around the year two hundred, described an Egyptian religious procession in which a singer carried one of the books of Hermes, and he said there were forty-two such books in all. Whatever lay behind that number, the point is plain. By Clement's day, people in Alexandria believed that Hermes had written books, and they believed those books were very old.

Now we reach the strange title that gives this series its name. Where does Trismegistus come from? It is Greek, and it means thrice-greatest, or three times great. It appears as a title attached to Hermes in Greek writing of the Roman period. It was not a name that anyone was born with. It was a title of honour, piled up like a crown.

Why thrice? Here we must be honest about what is known and what is not. The Egyptian background is quite clear. Egyptian texts often call Thoth great, and they repeat the word for emphasis, calling him great, great, great. A Greek ear hearing that triple repetition could easily turn it into one word, thrice-greatest. This is the explanation most scholars find the most natural. Later writers, though, offered their own stories. Some said Hermes was greatest as a priest, as a king, and as a philosopher. Others said he was greatest in knowledge, in speech, and in action. These explanations are pleasant, but they are guesses made long after the title already existed. The Egyptian repetition of the word great is the firmer ground.

So here is where we stand. In the Greek-speaking world of Egypt, in the centuries around the beginning of the common era, there was a god who was Hermes and Thoth together. He was called Thrice-Greatest. He was thought to be the author of ancient books of wisdom. And people who loved wisdom and searched for it wanted to read those books.

Now picture the same harbour again, some generations later. A man walks up from the quay to a small room in the city, where a friend keeps a roll of papyrus. The friend unrolls it by lamplight. At the top is the name of Hermes, the Thrice-Greatest, and below it, in careful Greek, a discourse on the mind and the world. The man leans in. He has heard the god's name all his life, from sailors and from priests. But here, at last, are words said to be the god's own.

What are they, then? What do these texts actually say, who wrote them, and how did they survive the long centuries? That is the question we turn to next.

The Library That Was Not Hidden

In the year fourteen hundred and sixty, in a monastery in the hills of Macedonia, a monk is unpacking a bundle of old Greek pages. A monk is walking through the cold library, carrying a bundle of old pages, and he is looking for a buyer. He does not know it, but what he carries will soon be read by some of the most famous scholars in Europe. This is the library that was not hidden, and the story of how the Hermetic writings travelled from a Greek manuscript to a Florentine desk.

First, a word about what the collection is. The name Corpus Hermeticum is a modern convenience. It means, roughly, the body of Hermetic writings. The manuscripts themselves do not call it that.

What the manuscripts contain is a set of Greek treatises, short teaching texts, many of them in the form of conversations. Hermes, or Hermes Trismegistus, speaks with a pupil. A father instructs a son. A teacher answers a disciple's questions about God, the mind, the world and the soul.

The English reader meets them most easily in the translation of George Robert Stow Mead, whose three volumes called Thrice-Greatest Hermes appeared in London in nineteen hundred and six. Mead gathered the treatises, the fragments and the testimony of other writers into one large work.

Now, the manuscripts. All the Greek copies of the main treatises that survive today come down through a single line of Byzantine tradition. The oldest are written many centuries after the texts were first composed. This is normal for ancient works. The original papyri are gone. What remains is a chain of copies, each made by a patient hand from the one before.

One of the best known of these manuscripts is kept today in Florence, and the Greek scholar Michael Psellos, who lived in the eleventh century in Constantinople, is the earliest Byzantine writer known to have read the Hermetic texts. So the writings were never entirely lost. They were quietly read in the Greek-speaking East for a thousand years.

Hold that thought, because it matters. The Hermetic books were not buried in a tomb and then dug up. They were copied, carried and read, in monasteries and in the studies of learned men. That is why this part is called the library that was not hidden.

Now we come back to the monk. His name is not certain in the sources, but the story is well told by those who record it. In the middle of the fifteenth century, a manuscript containing a number of Hermetic treatises reached Florence. It was brought, according to the usual account, by a monk who had gathered manuscripts in Macedonia for Cosimo de' Medici.

Cosimo de' Medici was the banker who ruled Florence without holding any crown. He loved books, and he paid scholars to read them. In his villa at Careggi, in the hills above the city, he kept a young man named Marsilio Ficino.

Ficino was the son of a physician, born in fourteen thirty three. Cosimo had marked him out as a translator of Plato, and Ficino had spent years learning Greek for just that purpose. He had already begun the great work of putting the dialogues of Plato into Latin.

Then the Hermetic manuscript arrived. And Cosimo, we are told, did something that surprises us. He told Ficino to put Plato aside. The Hermetic books, he said, should be translated first.

Why the hurry? Because Cosimo was an old man, and he wanted to read them before he died. And because he, like nearly everyone in Florence, believed they were very old indeed.

This belief rested on what other writers had said. Church fathers such as Lactantius and Augustine had spoken of Hermes Trismegistus as an ancient sage, and Lactantius quoted him freely. To a fifteenth century reader, Hermes seemed to be a teacher older than Moses, perhaps older than Plato, a kind of first voice of wisdom. We will test that belief in a later part. For now, simply notice that Ficino was working in good faith, and with great reverence.

So in fourteen sixty three, Ficino set to work. He finished the translation in a matter of months. He gave it a Latin title, Pimander, after the first treatise, and he dedicated it to Cosimo. It contained fourteen of the treatises, in Latin that Ficino made smooth and graceful.

Ficino tells us that Cosimo died in fourteen sixty four, the next year, and that he had been able to hear at least part of the work. Ficino was at Careggi when the old man passed away.

Let us pause here, in the quiet of that villa, and remember where we are in this story. We are in Florence, in the second half of the fifteenth century, with a young scholar at his desk and a dying banker waiting for his book. Behind them stands a long line of copyists, back through Byzantium and into the late ancient world.

The translation spread fast. The printing press had only recently reached Italy. Ficino's Pimander was printed for the first time at Treviso in fourteen seventy one, and it went through many editions in the decades that followed.

Mark what this did. Within a single generation, a text that had lived quietly in Greek manuscripts reached readers all over Europe, in Latin, which every educated person could read. Hermes Trismegistus became, almost overnight, a famous name.

Later, the books crossed into English. In sixteen fifty, a translation of the first treatise, under the title The Divine Pymander of Hermes Mercurius Trismegistus, was published in London by John Everard. It was made not from the Greek, but from a Latin and Dutch route, and it carried the rich, old-fashioned English of its time.

So far, everything in our story comes from the long Greek and Latin tradition. But the twentieth century added a remarkable chapter, and it came from the sands of Egypt.

In December of nineteen forty five, a farmer was digging near the town of Nag Hammadi, in Upper Egypt. His name was Muhammad Ali al-Samman. He was looking for fertilising soil near a cliff, and his mattock struck a large sealed jar.

Inside the jar were thirteen books, bound in leather, written on papyrus. They were codices, the early form of a book with pages rather than a scroll. They had been buried, it seems, in the fourth century, perhaps by monks from a nearby monastery.

The books were written in Coptic, the last form of the Egyptian language. They held more than fifty separate works, most of them what we now call Gnostic writings. These are texts about secret knowledge, about the divine spark within the human being, and about how the world came to be.

And among them, in the sixth codex, were three Hermetic texts. One was a dialogue on the eighth and the ninth, a teaching about an ascent of the soul through the heavenly spheres. One was a prayer of thanksgiving. And one was a long passage from the Asclepius, a work we have so far known only in Latin.

This find mattered for a clear reason. Until then, we had the Hermetic books only in Greek and Latin copies, written down far from Egypt and long after the texts began. Now, for the first time, there were Hermetic writings in an Egyptian tongue, in a book made in Egypt not long after the period when such texts flourished.

It did not prove that the teaching was pharaonic. But it did show that Hermetic ideas were being read and copied by Egyptian readers in the fourth century, side by side with other religious writings. The library, in other words, was not only in Florence and Constantinople. It was also, quietly, in the Egyptian desert.

Let us gather what we have. A set of Greek treatises, copied for centuries in the Byzantine world. A monk who carried a manuscript to Florence. A banker who asked for it to be read before he died, and a young scholar who gave it Latin words in fourteen sixty three. A press that sent it across Europe. And a jar in the Egyptian sand, opened in nineteen forty five, with Hermetic pages inside.

Everything in that chain assumes one thing, that these books are very old. Cosimo thought so. Ficino thought so. They believed they held the voice of an ancient Egyptian priest. But is that so? How old are the texts, really?

Casaubon's Discovery

In the spring of sixteen fourteen, in a house in London, an old scholar named Isaac Casaubon sits at a table covered with open books. He is fifty five years old, he is not well, and he is reading the same Greek sentences again, slowly, with a pen in his hand.

He was born in Geneva, in fifteen fifty nine, the son of a French Protestant family that had fled religious war. He had spent his life among Greek texts, editing and explaining them, and he had become one of the most respected readers of Greek in Europe. When King Henry the Fourth of France was murdered in sixteen ten, Casaubon lost his protector in Paris, and he crossed the sea to England, where King James the First welcomed him and gave him a pension.

Tonight the book he is working on is an answer to another book. It is a huge history of the Church, written by a Catholic cardinal named Caesar Baronius, and its first volume had appeared in fifteen eighty eight. Casaubon is a Protestant, and the king has asked him to examine it. He is going through it page by page, checking the cardinal's sources. And one of those sources, again and again, is a name you now know well. Hermes Trismegistus.

This is the third part of our story. We have met the Greek god and the Egyptian god who became one figure, and we have followed the writings in his name from Egypt to Florence. Now we come to the man who asked a plain question of those writings. How old are they, really?

To see why the question mattered, you need to know what the cardinal was doing with Hermes. Baronius wanted to show that even pagan wisdom had pointed toward Christianity. So he quoted Hermes as a witness from the ancient world, a voice from Egypt speaking of one God and of a divine Son long before Bethlehem.

He was not inventing this use. Some of the earliest Christian writers had done the same. Around the year three hundred, the Latin writer Lactantius quoted Hermes Trismegistus several times, once on the one God who has no name, and once on a Son of God. A century later Saint Augustine also discussed Hermes. He said that this Egyptian was very ancient, and he also criticised a passage in which Hermes seemed to approve of making gods by ritual.

And in Florence, a hundred and fifty years before Casaubon, Marsilio Ficino had taken the same view. Ficino believed that Hermes stood at the head of a chain of ancient sages that led down to Plato. So by Casaubon's day, a very old Hermes was a settled idea. He was older than Moses, perhaps, and older than Greek philosophy. Everything rested on that age.

Casaubon did not begin with a theory. He began, as he always did, with the words.

Think of what a Greek scholar notices when he reads, the way a musician notices a wrong note. Casaubon read the Greek of the Hermetic treatises and found it was not the language of a priest of the early Egyptian kings. It was a Greek of a later age, with the vocabulary of the philosophers. He pointed out that the writings had not been composed in Egyptian at all, though Hermes was supposed to belong to Egypt's remote past.

Then he went further. He traced the ideas. The talk of mind, of the soul's ascent, of the maker of the world, sounded to him very much like Plato and his followers. And in the treatise called Pymander, he heard echoes of the Greek Bible, especially the opening of Genesis, and of the language of the Gospels.

You can feel how the argument goes. If a book speaks in the words of Plato and the words of the Christian Scriptures, then it cannot be older than both. It must come after them.

So Casaubon drew his conclusion. The Hermetic writings, he said, were not the work of an ancient Egyptian sage. They were composed after the time of Christ, under a borrowed name. He suspected that some Christians, or people close to Christianity, had written them. And what of Lactantius and Augustine? They had been honest men who were fooled, he said, by writings that were already in circulation in their own time.

This was the heart of the matter. He was not calling the texts worthless. He was saying something narrower and more dangerous. The name on the cover was false, and the age claimed for them was false. And a witness who is not ancient cannot testify to what the ancient world knew.

You should notice where this appeared. It was not a book about Hermes. It was a long series of essays against Baronius, and the Hermetic discussion sits inside the very first of them, in a section only a few pages long. Casaubon was not trying to start a revolution in the study of Hermes. He was trying to take one prop out from under one cardinal's argument. The result went much further than he knew.

The book came out in sixteen fourteen. Casaubon himself died in London that same year, in the summer, and he was buried in Westminster Abbey. He never saw the long argument that followed.

Let us pause here and place ourselves. We are in the early seventeenth century. Casaubon has just argued that the Hermetic writings are late, Greek and borrowed. Next we must ask whether he was right.

In part, he was. Modern scholars agree that the philosophical treatises of the Corpus Hermeticum were written in Greek, in Roman Egypt, and not by a single author. Mead, writing in nineteen oh six, placed them roughly between the years one hundred and three hundred of the common era. That is later than Ficino imagined, and it is in the same age that Casaubon pointed to.

But in part, he was wrong, or at least too quick. Casaubon had assumed that a late Greek text must be a Christian forgery. That does not follow. By the time of the Roman Empire, Egypt was a place where Greek was the language of learning, and Egyptian priests and Greek speaking teachers lived side by side. Mead argued that the treatises were not frauds in the harsh sense Casaubon meant, but the product of a real religious and philosophical circle.

Then, in the twentieth century, the debate swung back and forth. In nineteen oh four, a German scholar named Richard Reitzenstein argued that the first treatise, Poimandres, carried deep Egyptian roots. Some years later, the French scholar André Jean Festugière took the opposite side. He argued that the philosophical treatises were Greek in thought, drawing on Plato and the Stoics, with little that was truly Egyptian beneath. His edition of the Greek text, made with Arthur Darby Nock, appeared between nineteen forty five and nineteen fifty four.

And in nineteen eighty six, Garth Fowden offered a middle path. He suggested that the writings grew in Egypt, in a Greek speaking world, and that the Egyptian setting was part of their identity even where the words and the arguments were Greek.

What does that leave us with? It leaves a calm picture, and you can hold it lightly as you listen. Casaubon was right about the date, near enough. He was right that the texts were Greek in their language and in much of their thought. He was wrong to call them simply forgeries, and wrong to think a late date made them empty. The Hermes of the legends, the priest king who lived before Moses and taught the pyramid builders, had gone. In his place stood something quieter and, in its own way, more interesting. A circle of writers in Roman Egypt who wished to speak in an old voice.

There is one more thing worth saying about Casaubon's reputation. For a long time it was said that his argument ended the Renaissance love of Hermes at a stroke. Frances Yates, in her famous book on Giordano Bruno, put it that way, though other historians think the change was slower and less complete.

Whichever is true, the question Casaubon asked has never gone away. It is the question we will carry with us from now on, whenever we read a line from these texts. Not only what does it say, but who is speaking, and when.

So let us do what he did, and read. Not with a pen in the margin, but with open ears, and with the whole of the dark quiet around us. We have the date, roughly. We have the language. We know that the voice is late, and Greek, and Egyptian in its setting. Now we should hear what that voice actually says, in the very first of the treatises, the one that begins with a man alone, thinking, and something rising up before him.

What does the first treatise say.

Poimandres

Somewhere in the Egyptian countryside a man is resting, in the years when Greek was the language of every educated household along the Nile. He has been thinking hard about the nature of things. Then his body grows heavy, his senses grow quiet, and he seems to drift, as a person does after a heavy meal or a long day's work. This is how the first treatise of the Corpus Hermeticum begins, and Mead's translation renders the moment plainly: the speaker says that once, when he had begun to think about the things that are, his thoughts were raised on high while his bodily senses were held back, as in sleep.

Then a presence appears. It is a being of immense size, and it calls him by name and asks what he wishes to hear and see.

The speaker answers with a question of his own: who are you? The being replies in a sentence that gives the treatise its title. It says that it is Poimandres, the Mind of the Sovereignty.

Let us settle where we are. This is part four of nine. We have met the two gods who became one, the library and its manuscripts, and the scholar who dated the texts to a later age. Now we leave the history of the books and enter the first of them. For many readers, this single treatise is the heart of the whole collection.

The speaker asks to learn the things that are, to understand their nature and to know God. The being tells him to hold in his mind whatever he wishes to learn, and says that it will teach him.

The scene then changes. In Mead's version, everything is suddenly opened to the speaker in a moment, and he sees a boundless view. All has become light, serene and joyous, and he is filled with love at the sight.

Then, a little while later, a darkness comes down in part of the vision. It is fearful and gloomy, and it winds in spirals like a snake. The speaker says that the darkness changed into a kind of moist nature, tossed about beyond description, and sending out smoke as from a fire.

From this watery darkness there rises a sound that cannot be put into words, a kind of wailing. And out of the light there comes a holy Word, which settles upon that nature. Fire leaps up out of the moist depth and climbs. Air, which is light, follows the fire upward, while earth and water stay mingled below, so that the earth could not be told apart from the water.

See how the teaching works here. The treatise does not state a doctrine and then defend it. It lets the listener watch a universe sort itself, light from dark, high from low, the heavy from the light, with a Word moving over the waters. Many readers have heard an echo of the opening of Genesis in this, and scholars have discussed that echo at length, though how the two texts relate is a matter of debate and not a settled fact.

Poimandres explains the vision. The light, it says, is Mind, and Mind is the speaker's own God, who existed before the watery nature that appeared out of the darkness. The Word that came forth from the light is the Son of God.

Then comes a line that has been quoted for centuries. The being tells the speaker that what in him sees and hears is the Lord's Word, and that his mind is God the Father, and that the two are not divided from one another, for their union is life.

Next the treatise tells of how the ordered world was made. The Mind, we are told, being male and female and existing as life and light, brought forth by a Word a second Mind, a craftsman, who was god of the fire and the breath. This craftsman made seven governors, who enclose the world of the senses in their circles, and their government is called Fate.

Here the ancient reader would have recognised something familiar. The seven governors are the seven wandering stars known to the ancients, which moved against the fixed sky, and their rule was thought to shape the course of earthly lives. The treatise accepts this old picture of the heavens and then gives it a particular moral colouring.

And now the human being enters the story. According to Poimandres, the Mind, who is Father of all, brought forth a Man like himself, and loved him as his own child. The Man was very beautiful, bearing the image of his Father.

The Man wished to make something himself. He looked down through the framework of the spheres, and the governors loved him, and each gave him a share of its own order. Then he leaned out over the harmony of the heavens, and showed down to lower nature the beautiful form of God.

Nature saw that form, and she smiled with love, because she saw in the water the reflection of his shape, and on the earth its shadow. He saw this likeness in her, and loved it, and wished to dwell there. The treatise says that the wish and the act came together, and he entered into the unreasoning form. Nature took hold of her beloved and wrapped herself wholly around him, and they were joined.

This is the turning point of the whole treatise. Poimandres draws the conclusion directly: because of this, alone among all creatures upon earth, the human being is twofold. He is mortal in his body, and immortal in the essential Man. Though he is above all things, deathless and lord of everything, he suffers the lot of the mortal, being subject to Fate.

Pause on that for a moment, because it is the idea the whole collection keeps returning to. The human being is not simply a creature of the earth, and not simply a spirit imprisoned in flesh. He is a joining of the two, and his situation is a kind of sleep that he can wake from.

The treatise goes on to describe how the first humans came to be. From the union of the Man with nature, it says, seven men were born, corresponding to the natures of the seven governors, both male and female and lifted up in the air. Then the story of life on earth continues, until a single command is given: let the one who has mind recognise himself as immortal, and know that the cause of death is love of the body.

That is the pivot from story to teaching. Poimandres tells the speaker that the one who knows himself has come into the good that is above all being, and that the one who has loved the body, born of the error of desire, wanders in darkness and suffers the things of death.

The speaker asks how one may return, and the answer describes a journey. After death, the material body is given over to change, and the person rises through the seven spheres, giving back at each one a part of what that governor had lent. At the last he arrives in the eighth sphere with his own proper power, and sings hymns to the Father together with those who are there.

The vision ends with the speaker commissioned to teach. He says that he began to proclaim to men the beauty of devotion and knowledge, and he calls out to them to stop being drunk with the sleep of ignorance and to become sober. The treatise closes with a prayer of thanks, asking that he may be kept in the knowledge he has received.

So the first treatise of the collection is a story told in the form of a dream. It holds together a creation, a fall, a human nature in two parts, and a road home, all within a few pages. It also tells us what the later writings are for. They are not a list of facts about the world. They are meant to lead a reader, step by step, toward the waking that Poimandres describes.

And if this one vision is the foundation, a question follows naturally. What does the rest of the corpus teach?

The Mind and the World

In a quiet room in Roman Egypt, perhaps in the second or third century of our era, a teacher and a student sit down together. The teacher is called Hermes. The student is his son, Tat. The room is probably an invention of the writer, as is the teacher, but the lesson is real enough, and it has survived. It is the tenth treatise of the Corpus Hermeticum, and it begins with the teacher recalling something he said the day before.

The story so far, if you are just settling in, is this. We have met the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian Thoth, and seen how they became one name. We have followed the manuscripts to Florence, and Casaubon's sixteen fourteen argument that they were not as old as people hoped. Last time we listened to the vision of Poimandres. Now we turn to what the teachers in these texts actually say about the mind, and about the world it lives in.

Begin with the word Nous. It is an ordinary Greek word, usually rendered mind, or intellect, and in these texts it carries great weight. In the first treatise, the being who appears to the seer says plainly: "That Light am I, thy God, Mind". Mind is not only a faculty a person has. It is something shared, something that flows from a source, and the texts keep returning to that thought.

In the twelfth treatise, called in Mead's translation "About the Common Mind," the teacher tells Tat that mind is from God's own essence. The idea is that the mind in you is not a separate invention of yours. It is a kind of kinship. Because of it, a human being is not merely an animal. The texts say more than once that the human being is a twofold creature, mortal in the body, but in the mind, something that touches the divine.

This is a gentle sort of teaching, and also a demanding one. The teacher in the thirteenth treatise says the knowledge of God is not simply handed over. It has to be received by a kind of rebirth, a change in the one who learns. The student asks for the plain answer, and the teacher answers that the answer cannot be told, only seen. So the lesson is built into the form. The student must be quiet. The student must wait.

Now consider how the Hermetic writers saw the world around them. A modern listener might expect them to treat the sky, the earth, the sea as dead matter, a stage on which souls pass through. That is not what the texts say. In the eighth treatise the cosmos is called a living thing. In Mead's rendering the world is "the second god", a god in the sense that it has life and order. It is made by the first God, and it is not eternal in the way God is, but it is alive, and it is good.

The fourth treatise, "The Cup or Monad," goes further. It says God made the cosmos, and that the cosmos is beautiful, and that the person who looks upon it with understanding can find the maker through the work. A student might never see God directly. But the order of the heavens, the turning of seasons, the way living things are born and grow, all of it points beyond itself.

The same treatise tells a curious story about this. It says God set a great mixing bowl in the world, filled with mind, and sent a herald to proclaim to human hearts that they might dip into it and be baptized, and so rise toward knowing. Some did, and received mind, and became perfect. Others did not, and remained in ignorance. The image is a cup, not a commandment. Nobody is forced to drink.

So the idea of the world as alive has a moral side. If the cosmos is a living, ordered whole, and if mind is a spark of what orders it, then to understand the world is also to become more like its source. The texts do not separate knowing from becoming. That, more than any single doctrine, is the quiet centre of Hermetic teaching.

Hold that thought, because the best known place where it is spelled out is a different work altogether, and it comes to us by a stranger road. It is called the Asclepius, or in Greek the Perfect Discourse, and it is set as a conversation.

The speakers are Hermes, named as the teacher, and Asclepius, who is the Greek form of the healing god's name, along with Tat and a figure called Ammon. They meet, in the story, in a temple, and the talk is private. The writer has them clear the room of listeners, because the teaching, they say, is only for those ready for it.

The Asclepius did not come down to us in Greek as a whole. Most of it survives in a Latin translation, long attributed, wrongly, to the Roman writer Apuleius. This is part of why it was known in the Middle Ages, when the Greek treatises of the Corpus were not. Medieval readers knew the Asclepius, and it kept the Hermetic name alive long before Ficino.

What does the Asclepius say? Among the first things is a striking remark about the human being. Hermes tells Asclepius that a human is a great wonder, because the human alone is double, with a part that is divine and a part that is of the world. We have heard this before, in Poimandres, but here it is said with great warmth. The human, in this text, is the one creature who can tend both heaven and earth.

Then comes a passage which many readers remember more than any other. It speaks of the old Egyptian religion, and of its images, the statues of the gods. The text says that humans, knowing how to make gods, made them, and that these statues were filled with a kind of living presence, drawn down by art. Elsewhere the same work grieves for a future in which Egypt will lose its religion, and the land will be empty of the old rites.

Here we must be careful, and honest. The Asclepius really does speak of statues animated by the art of the priests. It is one of the very few Hermetic texts to do so. The Greek treatises of the Corpus, for the most part, do not talk about this. They speak of mind, and light, and rebirth. So when a modern reader says the Hermetic writers practised magic, the Asclepius is one place where there is some support, and the Corpus Hermeticum is a place where there is much less.

What the Asclepius also says, and this is the part that matters for our story, is that the world is a single living whole, held together by a kind of sympathy between its parts. God, it says, is in all things, and all things are linked. It also says that the world is an image of God, and that the human being, who looks upon the world, is an image of the world in small. Hold on to that word image. We shall return to it when we come to the line about above and below.

A word about the dating, since we left Casaubon behind us. Scholars today generally place the Greek Hermetic treatises somewhere in the early centuries of the Common Era, with the Asclepius falling in the same broad period, though exact dates are not known. The Latin Asclepius is usually thought to come from the fourth century. None of this makes the teaching less interesting. It only tells us who the writers probably were: educated people in a mixed world of Greek philosophy and Egyptian tradition.

That mixture shows. The idea that the world is alive, ordered, and ensouled owes a great deal to Greek thinking, to Plato's account of a living cosmos and to the Stoics who spoke of a divine reason running through everything. The Hermetic writers took such ideas and wrapped them in a voice of ancient Egyptian authority. Whether they believed that voice, or used it as a literary frame, we cannot say for certain.

So let us gather the threads. Mind, Nous, is a gift shared between God and the human being. The cosmos is a living thing, made by God, and beautiful. The one who studies it can come to know its maker. And the human being stands between, double, able to reach either way.

It is a hopeful picture, and a quiet one. And yet a picture like this is easy to bend. If the world is a living web, bound together by sympathy, if statues can be filled with presence, if the mind can rise by knowing, then a curious reader might ask whether the web can be pulled. Whether, by knowing the links, one might move things. That question, and the strange journey it takes, belong to the next part of our story: what happened to the idea of magic.

As Above, So Below

Balinus walked down into the dark beneath the great statue at Tyana, with a lamp in his hand and the old city quiet above him.

That is how the Arabic story begins. Balinus is the name Arabic writers gave to Apollonius of Tyana, the wandering sage of the Greek world, and here he is a seeker after the hidden causes of things. He had searched for a long time. At last, so the tale goes, he found a vault under the figure of Hermes. Inside sat the body of an old man, and in the old man's hands lay a tablet of green stone.

You have heard this tablet named many times, and you have been told it is the root of everything Hermetic. This part of the story goes looking for where it actually came from. The answer is a little different from the legend, and in some ways more interesting.

Here is where we are. The earlier parts took you through the Greek Hermetic treatises, the vision of Poimandres, and the teaching of Mind and of the living cosmos. Now we turn to the one famous text that is not among them.

Start with a plain fact. The Greek collection called the Corpus Hermeticum, in the edition Mead translated, does not contain the Emerald Tablet. No Greek original of it has ever been found. The earliest versions we can read are in Arabic.

The best known of them stands in a book called the Book of the Secret of Creation, which is attributed to Balinus, the same sage who descends into the vault. The scholar Ursula Weisser, who edited that book in nineteen seventy nine, placed it around the eighth or ninth century, though the date is an estimate and not a certainty. So the tablet is not older than the Greek treatises. It comes centuries after them, in the world of Baghdad and the Arabic translators.

It was a German scholar, Julius Ruska, who set this out in a study published in nineteen twenty six. He argued that the Latin Emerald Tablet descended from Arabic sources, and later work has largely held to that.

The tablet turns up elsewhere too. A version appears in the Secret of Secrets, an Arabic work presented as the advice of Aristotle to his pupil Alexander. That book was translated into Latin in the thirteenth century, and it travelled widely. Through books like these, the little text slipped from Arabic into Latin.

Now the thing itself. It is very short. You could read it aloud in about a minute. In the English version that Isaac Newton made for himself, it opens with an insistence on truth: "Tis true without lying, certain and most true."

Then comes the line that has travelled furthest. In Latin it runs: "Quod est superius est sicut quod est inferius." In Newton's English: "That which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below, to do the miracles of one only thing."

Hear the grammar, because it matters. The text says like, or similar to. It does not say that above and below are the same, and it does not say that one causes the other. It says they correspond. And it gives a purpose: the likeness is there so that the miracles of one thing may be done.

The famous short form, As above, so below, is a later compression. It is not the wording of the Latin, and it is a slogan made out of the sentence, not the sentence itself. There is nothing wrong with a slogan. But it is good to know that the words most people repeat are a shortening of something more particular.

What is that one thing? The tablet does not say plainly. It goes on to describe it in riddles. The sun is its father and the moon its mother. The wind carried it in its belly, and the earth is its nurse. And the text tells the reader to separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross, "sweetly with great industry."

Listen to that last instruction. It is not about gazing at the stars. It is about work, patient and careful work, done with the hands. That is the first clue to how the tablet was first used. It was read by people who worked with furnaces, flasks and metals, and who believed that the processes of the vessel echoed the processes of nature. This was the world of alchemy.

Near the end the speaker names himself. He says he is called Hermes Trismegistus, because he holds the three parts of the philosophy of the whole world. So the tablet claims the old name of Thrice-Greatest, and that claim is part of what gave it such authority.

Why would the line about above and below have made sense to its first readers? Because the idea was already old and familiar. The Stoic philosophers of the Greek world taught that the cosmos is a single living being, whose parts feel and answer one another. They called this sympathy. Greek astrology rested on a related belief, that the movements of the heavens are linked to events on earth.

The Hermetic treatises you have already met share the mood. The Latin Asclepius, as we saw, speaks of the human being as a great wonder, a creature that joins the divine and the mortal in one. The human being is a little world set inside the great one. That is the same thought as the tablet's, put in the language of philosophy and not of the furnace.

So here is what the line can reasonably be said to mean. The order of the heavens and the order of earthly things are patterned alike. What you learn by watching one can teach you something about the other. And a person who understands the pattern may work in step with nature, and not against it. In the old chemistry, that meant the changes in the vessel were not random, but echoed the changes of the whole world.

Now what it does not mean. It does not say that your thoughts create your circumstances. It does not promise that wishing at one level moves events at another. Those are modern readings, and they are often attached to the line by people who have never seen its context. It also does not appear in the Greek Hermetic teaching as a stated doctrine, and the Corpus never uses it.

You may also meet a phrase that goes As within, so without, offered as part of the tablet. That is not in the early texts either, and it is best set aside as a later addition.

There is one more thing to say about how the tablet was read. Newton, the great natural philosopher, took it seriously enough to make his own English translation by hand, in a manuscript usually dated to around sixteen eighty. He did not read it as an ancient revelation about stars. He read it as a guide to nature's hidden workings. That is an important reminder. For most of its life, the tablet was a text for people who worked, who experimented and who hoped to understand matter.

And it remained, always, a little mysterious. Its words are compact and its images are dreamlike. Every age that read it found in it what it was looking for, a chemical recipe, a spiritual path, a law of the universe. The text itself is patient with all of them, because it never says exactly what the one thing is.

So we have a small green stone that was never found, a tale of a vault beneath a statue, and a single line about likeness between heaven and earth. It came from Arabic books of the early Middle Ages, and not from a Greek scroll or an Egyptian temple.

What remains is the next question. A text like this, once it reached Latin, had to find readers in a very different world, with its own universities, its own churches and its own hopes. How did the West receive it.

Renaissance and Alchemy

In the spring of fourteen sixty three, an old man in Florence is called to a villa at Careggi, outside the city walls. His name is Cosimo de' Medici, and he is the richest banker in Europe. He wants a book read to him before he dies. The book is Greek, and it has been sitting on his shelves, and he has told a young scholar to stop everything else and translate it first.

The young scholar is Marsilio Ficino. He is the son of Cosimo's physician, he is about thirty years old, and he has been working on the dialogues of Plato. Cosimo tells him to put Plato aside. The Hermetic writings, he says, must come first.

You may remember from earlier in the story that the manuscript had come from Macedonia, brought to Florence by a monk who was collecting old Greek books for the Medici. Ficino now sits down with it and works fast. In a matter of months he has the first fourteen treatises in Latin, and he calls the work the Pimander, after the first treatise.

It is worth pausing on what Ficino believed he was holding. He did not think of it as a late book. He had read the Latin church fathers, and Lactantius and Augustine both spoke of Hermes Trismegistus as a very ancient sage. Ficino took this at face value. In his preface, he placed Hermes at the head of a line of wise men, before Moses was thought to have been born, and leading toward Plato.

That belief did a great deal of work. If Hermes was older than Plato, and if his teaching sounded something like Plato and something like the gospel, then ancient wisdom was one river with many streams. Pagan philosophy and Christian faith did not have to quarrel. Ficino called this the ancient theology, and it suited a city that loved both its saints and its Greeks.

Cosimo died in August of fourteen sixty four, and Ficino's translation spread. It was printed for the first time in Treviso in fourteen seventy one, and it was reprinted again and again over the next century and a half. By the time the century ended, there were dozens of editions.

Ficino himself kept working. In fourteen eighty nine he published a book on life, in which he gave advice to scholars on keeping the body and spirit in health, including advice about music and the stars. Some of it leans on a Hermetic text called the Asclepius, which speaks of statues that seem to be alive. The church took notice, and Ficino was careful about how far he went.

Now the story moves to a younger man, a count from a small town near Ferrara, who is clever, handsome and rather too sure of himself. His name is Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, and in fourteen eighty six, when he is twenty three, he arrives in Rome with nine hundred theses. He offers to defend them against any scholar in Europe, and he will pay the travel costs of anyone who comes.

The nine hundred theses draw on Greek, Latin, Arabic, Hebrew and Aramaic sources. Among them, Pico set out to show that all these traditions pointed the same way. Hermes Trismegistus was one of the voices he called upon.

The debate never took place. A papal commission looked over the theses and condemned thirteen of them. Pico wrote a defence, and in the end Pope Innocent the Eighth condemned the whole collection. Pico fled to France and was briefly arrested there before Lorenzo de' Medici helped bring him home.

The speech he had meant to open the debate with survived, and it became famous after his death. It is often called the Oration on the Dignity of Man, though that title was added later. In it Pico says that God made the human being without a fixed place, so that he could shape himself, downward toward the beasts or upward toward the divine.

Pico opens that speech by recalling a line from the Asclepius, where Hermes calls man a great miracle. The idea of a being poised between animal and angel, free to choose, runs through the Hermetic writings, and Pico built it into something grander. Pico died young, in fourteen ninety four, at thirty one.

The third figure is a very different man. Giordano Bruno is born near Naples in fifteen forty eight, enters the Dominican order, and leaves it under suspicion of heresy. He spends years on the road through Geneva, Paris, London and Germany, teaching, writing and quarrelling.

In London in fifteen eighty three and fifteen eighty four he lived near the French embassy and published his Italian dialogues. He argued that the universe is infinite, that there are many worlds, and that the earth moves, as Copernicus had said. He was fond of the Hermetic idea that the cosmos is alive and full of divinity.

Here is a question that scholars have argued about for a long time. Was Bruno a Hermetic magician who wanted to restore an old Egyptian religion, or a philosopher who borrowed Hermetic language for his own purposes? The historian Frances Yates made the first claim famous in her nineteen sixty four book on Bruno. Later scholars have been more cautious, and many think she pressed the case too far.

Bruno returned to Italy in fifteen ninety one, was denounced to the Venetian Inquisition, and was sent to Rome. He was held for years. On the seventeenth of February sixteen hundred, he was burned at the stake in the Campo de' Fiori.

His trial papers do not survive complete, and the exact charges are known only in part. What we can say is that his views on the plurality of worlds, on the nature of Christ, and on the soul were all in question. It would be too neat to say that he died simply for being a Hermeticist.

Now we turn to a second group of people who used the name of Hermes, and they were not interested in philosophy so much as in matter. These are the alchemists. The word Hermetic, as in a hermetic seal, comes from them, because they said their vessels were closed in the manner of Hermes.

Here the story needs a careful note. The Hermetica we have been following are treatises about the mind, the cosmos and the soul. The alchemical writings that carry the name of Hermes are a different body of work. They were mostly composed in Arabic, then Latin, centuries after the Greek treatises, and they deal with metals, furnaces, and the making of a mysterious substance.

The two bodies of writing share a name and little else. Only a few passages in the Greek Hermetica touch on practical arts, and the great philosophical treatises say nothing of turning lead to gold. The alchemists wanted the authority of an ancient sage, and Hermes had it.

The most famous alchemical text under the name is the Emerald Tablet, which you will remember from the opening of this programme and from the part before this one. In the late Middle Ages it was copied and glossed in Latin again and again, and the commentators read it as a short recipe for the Stone. In the sixteen hundreds, men such as Isaac Newton filled notebooks with alchemical reading, and some of those notebooks include an English version of the Tablet.

The alchemical picture of Hermes grew very large. He appeared in woodcuts, in poems, and in the title pages of printed books, always older than anything around him. Then, in sixteen fourteen, Isaac Casaubon dated the Greek treatises to the early centuries of the Christian era, and the old picture began to wear thin. But that comes later, in its place, and it has already been told.

What remains is something worth keeping in mind as we go on. Ficino, Pico, Bruno and the alchemists all took a name from the past and gave it a new meaning that suited their own time. Each of them heard in Hermes what he wanted to hear. And that raises a question that the story must now turn toward: what is the Hermetic claim to be careful about?

What the Texts Do Not Say

On a quiet evening in the spring of sixteen fourteen, in a study in London, the scholar Isaac Casaubon sat at his desk with a stack of old Greek writings and a sharp, patient pen. He was not hunting for a scandal. He was reading, and what he read did not match what everyone had assumed. This is part eight of nine, and the story so far has carried you from Egypt to Florence and through the Arabic tablet. Now it turns to a gentler task, which is to set down, calmly, what the texts do not say.

Begin with the oldest myth, the one that gives the writings their glamour. People have long said that Hermes Trismegistus was a real sage of deep antiquity, who lived before Moses and taught the Egyptians everything. The Renaissance believed it. Marsilio Ficino, translating in Florence, took the author to be a priest of great age.

Casaubon undid that belief with a close reading of the language. He argued that the Greek of these books was not the Greek of a writer from the dawn of Egypt, and that the thoughts in them echoed Plato and the Christian writers, not pharaonic scribes. He published this in sixteen fourteen, in a long attack on a church history.

Later scholars refined his instinct and put real dates on the matter. The Greek treatises of the Corpus Hermeticum are generally placed in the early centuries of the Common Era, roughly the second and third, though the exact dates are argued over. They were written in Greek, in the Roman world, and Egypt was the setting the authors chose for their teacher more than the place where the ideas began.

Here a careful listener should hold two thoughts together. The texts are not ancient in the way legend claimed. Yet some of them may still carry older Egyptian echoes. The scholar Garth Fowden argued that the Greek Hermetic writings grew within the real religious life of Roman Egypt, shaped by Greek philosophy and also by a native setting. How much is truly Egyptian remains debated, and honest scholars say so.

The second myth is the pyramid. You will hear that Hermes designed the great pyramids, or that the Hermetic books describe them, or that the teaching was hidden inside their chambers. The texts say nothing of the kind. The treatises that survive are mostly dialogues, and they speak of mind, of God, of the soul and of the cosmos. They contain no building plans and no account of pyramid construction. The great pyramid at Giza was built in the age of the pharaoh Khufu, in the third millennium before the Common Era, roughly two thousand years before the earliest Hermetic writings could have been composed.

So the pyramid story has the order of events backwards. The books came long after the monuments, written by people who lived among ruins already ancient to them.

The third myth is the lost super-science. In this telling, the Hermetic books preserve a vanished technology, perhaps from a drowned civilisation, perhaps from visitors out of the sky. Open the Corpus Hermeticum looking for it and you will not find it. What you find is a teaching about knowing God through the mind, and a moral way of life. There are no machines in it, no energy systems, no formulas for power. The famous first treatise, the Poimandres, is a vision, and its subject is the origin of the human being and the way back to the divine.

There is one place where the Hermetic name does touch practical craft, and it is worth being fair about it. Writings on alchemy, astrology and magic were later credited to Hermes, and some of these are called technical Hermetica. These are a separate family from the philosophical treatises, and they belong to the study of nature as the ancient world understood it. They are not a lost engineering manual. The word hermetic, in the sense of sealed against air, comes from the later legend of Hermes as the founder of alchemy.

The fourth myth concerns the Emerald Tablet, with which this story began. People say it was found carved in a cave, or in the hands of a mummified Hermes, or in a tomb beneath the earth. The oldest known forms of the text are in Arabic, in a book often called the Secret of Creation, usually dated to about the eighth or ninth century of the Common Era. The tale that Apollonius of Tyana found the tablet in a vault appears in that Arabic setting. No emerald tablet has ever been recovered. What survives is a short text, copied and translated, whose own original is not known.

Its best known line, as above, so below, is brief, and it has been quoted far beyond what it can bear. The Latin version says that what is below is like that which is above, and what is above is like that which is below, for the working of the miracle of one thing. In its own setting, this belongs to the lore of the alchemists and the old idea that heaven and earth answer to one another. It is not a promise that thoughts create reality, and it is not a law of physics.

A related myth speaks of seven hermetic principles, a tidy list sometimes sold as the ancient teaching of Hermes. That list comes from a book called The Kybalion, published in nineteen oh eight by authors who signed themselves the Three Initiates. It is a modern work, written in the early twentieth century. The principles in it are not found in this form in the ancient Hermetic treatises. Many readers find value in it, and that is their right, but it should be known for what it is.

Now consider what scholars can and cannot tell. They can tell that the Greek treatises exist, that manuscripts of them survive, and that Byzantine copyists passed them down. They can tell that a number of Hermetic texts turned up among the Nag Hammadi books, found in Egypt in nineteen forty five, which shows these ideas were circulating among real readers in late antiquity. They can read the arguments, trace the borrowings and compare the manuscripts.

What they cannot tell is who wrote any of the treatises. The authors are unnamed. They cannot say whether a formal Hermetic community, a kind of church or school, ever met to study them. Some scholars suspect small study circles, while others suspect only readers and copyists who never gathered at all. And they cannot say how much the Egyptian priests of the age would have recognised in these pages. These are open questions, and leaving them open is part of being truthful.

You may find that gentler than the legends. A hidden pyramid sage and a vanished science make a fine tale, but the real history holds its own quiet interest. A few unknown writers in the Roman world, thinking about mind and God and the human soul, gave their words to an imagined teacher, and those words travelled for sixteen centuries.

The lamp in Casaubon's study burned down, and he went on reading, and his careful doubt did not destroy the Hermetic books. It set them in their proper place, where they could be read for what they are. So as this part comes to rest, one question is left to carry into the last chapter. When the legends are set gently aside, what can a listener keep?

The Quiet Core

In the autumn of fourteen sixty three, an old man named Marsilio Ficino sat in a villa outside Florence, a short ride from the city, with a lamp at his elbow and a Greek manuscript open before him. His patron, Cosimo de' Medici, had told him to set aside Plato for a while and translate this other book first, because Cosimo was old and wished to read it before he died. The book was the first part of what we now call the Corpus Hermeticum. Ficino worked through it quickly, and when he was done he gave it a Latin title and sent it to his patron.

We come back to that lamplit room now, at the end of this long story, because it is a good place to ask a simple question. After all the doubts, the dates, the myths and the corrections, what is actually left in these writings? What do the scholars, who agree on so little, agree on?

Start with the plainest fact. The texts are Greek, written in Egypt, most likely in the early centuries of the Common Era. Isaac Casaubon had argued as much in sixteen fourteen, and the later work of scholars has mostly confirmed the outline, though they have refined it in many places. So the quiet core is not a secret from the age of the pyramids. It is something humbler and, in its way, more interesting: a set of thoughtful voices, in a mixed and crowded city culture, trying to say what a human being is, and what a human being should do about it.

Here is the teaching, in the smallest form we can give it. In the first treatise, the one called Poemandres, a mind appears to the narrator and tells him what the world is made of, and then what he himself is made of. In G. R. S. Mead's English, the human being is described as two things at once, mortal because of the body, and immortal because of the essential man. The texts keep returning to this doubleness. A person is a creature of earth, and a person is also something that can look up from the earth and understand.

And understanding is the heart of it. The word that carries the whole project is gnosis, the Greek word for knowing. In these texts, to know is not to collect facts. It is to recognise something: that the mind in you is related to the mind that orders the whole world. The writers say this in many ways. The tenth treatise says that the knowledge of God is itself a kind of salvation. The reader is meant to come away changed, not merely informed.

So what does a changed person do? This is the part people forget, because the famous lines about heaven and earth steal the attention. But the ethic of the texts is quiet and fairly clear. The writers ask for a life of restraint. They warn against the pull of the body's appetites, and they speak of the passions as a kind of weight that holds the soul down. In one treatise, the soul is said to be freed by climbing through the spheres, shedding at each stage a vice it has carried. The picture is of a person gradually becoming lighter.

There is also a gentle moral thread about speech and worship. The Asclepius, which survives in a Latin form, says that the best offering to God is not incense or blood, but praise and thanksgiving. The thirteenth treatise ends with a hymn, and the speaker declares that he sings it quietly, from the heart. One sentence in the Corpus says that the only worship that is truly pleasing is simply to be good. That is a short line, and a large one.

You might expect that texts so taken up with the mind and the heavens would ask the reader to turn away from other people. They do not, or not entirely. The Asclepius insists that humans are bound to one another by kinship, and that the wise person cares for the world as a gardener cares for a garden. The human being, in this view, has a task: to tend what is below, because he belongs both to it and to what is above. The line that later became so famous, as above so below, belongs to another tradition and another century, as we saw earlier. But its mood is not far from this one.

It is worth being honest about what the texts leave unsettled. Scholars still disagree about whether the treatises describe a real religious community, or whether they are the work of readers and writers who enjoyed thinking in this way without belonging to any group. The evidence is thin. Some of the writings contradict one another, and they do not fit tidily into one philosophy. A careful reader today holds the collection loosely, as a library of voices rather than a single book.

And still the voices rhyme. A teacher speaks to a pupil. A father speaks to a son. The pupil asks, and the teacher answers, and the answers are patient.
