Marcus Aurelius and Meditations: A Calm Lesson in Stoic Philosophy — full script

Cold open

Long after dark, in a room in a winter camp on the Danube, a lamp is still burning. The river is somewhere out beyond the wall, wide and black, and past it lies country the Romans do not hold. A lamp burns low. A man in his fifties sits close to it with a pen, and he is very tired.

His name is Marcus Aurelius, and he is the emperor of Rome. He was born in the spring of the year one hundred and twenty one, and he has ruled since the year one hundred and sixty one. For much of that time he has not been in Rome at all. He has been here, on the northern frontier, with the legions, in the long wars against the tribes across the river.

Tonight there are no ceremonies, and no one is waiting for a speech. He is writing a few lines, and the lines are for himself.

He writes in Greek. That is a little strange, because he rules a Latin empire, and Latin is the language of its law and its armies. But Greek is the language in which he learned philosophy, and it is the language he reaches for when he wants to think clearly about how to live.

Some of these pages carry small notes about where they were written. At the end of the second book, in George Long's English, there are just four words: "This in Carnuntum." Carnuntum was a Roman base on the Danube, in the province of Pannonia, not far east of modern Vienna. At the end of the first book, the note says he was among the Quadi, one of the peoples beyond the river. So we know, quite simply, that this notebook was written in tents and camps, in the middle of a war.

And what does he write? Not battle plans, and not a history of his reign. He writes reminders. He tells himself to get up in the morning and do his work, and he calls that work "the work of a human being." He argues with his own bad temper. He tells himself that he will not live long, and that he should not waste the time.

Here is the part that is easy to forget. He did not call it the Meditations. That name came much later. The old manuscripts head it with a plain Greek phrase that means something like "to himself." Nothing in it suggests he ever meant a reader to open it. It was a private place, where the most powerful man in the world could be honest with one person.

Tonight, though, you are going to read over his shoulder, quietly, the way one listens to someone thinking aloud in the next room. Eighteen centuries on, the lamp is still burning on those pages.

So settle in. Let the camp go quiet, and let the river run on in the dark. Before we hear what he wrote, there is a simpler question, and it is the one we begin with. Who was he?

A Boy Called Verissimus

In the spring of the year one hundred and twenty-one, in a house on the Caelian hill in Rome, a boy was born who would one day be emperor, though nobody in that house could have said so. His name at birth was Marcus Annius Verus. His family was wealthy and old, with roots in Spain, and his grandfather, also named Marcus Annius Verus, had been consul three times. The boy's father died while he was still very young, and so he was raised first in his mother's household and then in his grandfather's.

Picture him there, a quiet child among tall rooms, and you are already close to the truth of him. The Historia Augusta, a Latin collection of imperial lives written long after his death and not always reliable, says he was serious from an early age. It adds a small detail that the emperor Hadrian noticed this earnestness and teasingly called him Verissimus, which means something like "most truthful." That is the nickname that gives this part its title. Whether Hadrian said it exactly so, we cannot be sure, but the name has stuck to him ever since.

Now the story widens. The emperor at that time was Hadrian, a restless, clever man who travelled the empire for years and had no son of his own. Hadrian's chosen heir, Lucius Aelius Caesar, died suddenly at the very end of the year one hundred and thirty-seven. So Hadrian looked again, and he made a careful plan. He adopted a respected senator named Antoninus, who was later known as Antoninus Pius, and he asked Antoninus in turn to adopt two boys. One of them was Marcus, then about sixteen. The other was the young son of the dead Aelius, named Lucius Ceionius Commodus.

Think about what this meant for a teenager. He had been a boy in his grandfather's house. Now, by a legal act, he was the adopted son of the man who would rule the world. Hadrian died in the summer of the year one hundred and thirty-eight, and Antoninus Pius became emperor. Marcus was drawn into the centre of power, and he took a new name, Marcus Aelius Aurelius Verus. Later, when he himself became emperor, the name we know him by, Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, would come to him.

You may be wondering what a young heir actually did all day. The answer is that he studied, a great deal. The Historia Augusta tells us he had many teachers, and we should hold that list with some care, because the author was fond of lists. But Marcus himself confirms the broad picture. In the first book of his private notebook, which this series will visit properly in a later part, he thanks the teachers and family who shaped him one by one. So what we have is the grown man looking back and naming his debts.

Let us meet a few of them now. In the notebook he thanks his great-grandfather for the fact that he did not go to public schools but had good teachers at home, and he adds that a man should spend freely on such things. In the translation by George Long, published in eighteen sixty-two, the sense is that he learned to know that on such things a man should spend liberally. It is a gentle remark, and it tells us the boy was taught at home, among private tutors, rather than in a crowded schoolroom.

He also tells us about a man named Diognetus, who taught him not to waste time on trivial things and not to be taken in by wonder-workers and charm-sellers. Diognetus was a painter, and he is also said to have introduced the boy to the Greek way of life. Marcus recalls that this teacher encouraged him to write dialogues while still a child and to lie on the simple bed covered with a skin, as the Greek discipline required. Imagine that: a rich Roman boy, choosing a hard, plain bed on purpose, because a teacher had planted an idea in him.

That idea was philosophy. The Historia Augusta says that at about the age of twelve he took up the philosopher's dress and the austere habits that went with it, sleeping on the ground and wearing a rough cloak. The same source says his mother had to persuade him to take a softer couch. We should be honest here. This is the kind of charming story that later writers loved to tell, and we cannot check it. But it fits what Marcus says about Diognetus, and so the broad picture is probably sound.

Alongside philosophy came words, and especially rhetoric, the art of speaking well, which every Roman leader needed. His chief teacher of rhetoric was Marcus Cornelius Fronto, the most admired orator of his day. Fronto's letters to Marcus survive, and they show a warm and affectionate bond between the tutor and the young prince. Yet something fascinating happens in those years. Marcus loved his teacher, and still he began to drift away from rhetoric toward something he found more urgent, the question of how to live.

This is the point where the story reaches its first great turning. In the notebook, among the thanks to his teachers, Marcus mentions a man named Rusticus, Quintus Junius Rusticus, who was a Stoic philosopher and one of the great influences on his life. Rusticus taught him that his character needed straightening and training. He also taught him not to be led astray into writing for show, and not to deliver fine little speeches about philosophy to impress others. Remember that, because it matters later. The notebook we read today was never written for show.

And then comes the gift that gives this part its last great scene. Marcus tells us that Rusticus lent him his own copy of the discourses of Epictetus. Epictetus had been born a slave, in Phrygia, and had become one of the most respected teachers of Stoicism. He wrote nothing himself. His student Arrian, who sat in the lecture hall and took notes, preserved his words in the Discourses and in the shorter Enchiridion, the handbook.

Picture the scene. A young man who will one day command legions sits with a borrowed book, the lecture notes of a former slave, and reads. In the Enchiridion, in the translation by George Long, the very first lesson is that some things are in our power and others are not. Our opinions, our desires, our aversions belong to us. Our body, our property, our reputation do not. Here was an idea that a prince could use. He could not command what the world would do to him. He could only command how he met it.

Marcus himself says the book came from Rusticus's own shelf, a generous thing for a teacher to do. We do not know exactly how old he was when he read it, and we should not claim to. What we know is that the lesson stayed, because it echoes through everything he later wrote to himself.

So let us pause where the story stands. A boy named Verus, nicknamed most truthful, has been lifted out of an old family house into the household of an emperor. He has been surrounded by tutors, trained in speech, drawn toward hard beds and plain cloaks, and handed a slave's lecture notes by a man who loved him. The world gave him a throne to inherit. His teachers gave him something to hold when the throne grew heavy.

What exactly did that training teach him? That is the question we carry forward, into the quiet that follows.

What Stoicism Is

Some sixty years before Marcus Aurelius was born, a lecture is under way in a small room in Nicopolis, a town on the western coast of Greece. A lame man sits on a stool, speaking to a few students in the plain Greek of ordinary people. His name is Epictetus, and he was once a slave. A student sits close by, taking notes, and later those notes will be written up as the Discourses.

It is worth beginning here, because the philosophy that shaped an emperor was taught by a man who owned almost nothing. Later sources say that Epictetus was born a slave and that his master allowed him to study with a Stoic teacher. He was freed, and then he taught. This part of our story is about what he taught, and what the school behind him believed. You have met the boy, Marcus, in the last part. Now we turn to the ideas he would spend his life trying to practise.

Stoicism takes its name from a painted porch in Athens, the Stoa Poikile, where its founder Zeno of Citium taught around three hundred years before the common era. The name stuck to the school, and the school lasted for centuries. By the time of Epictetus it had become less a matter of clever arguments and more a way of living, a training for ordinary days.

The ancient Stoics divided their teaching into three parts: logic, physics, and ethics. Logic taught how to think clearly. Physics, the study of nature, taught how the world is built. And ethics taught how a person should live. Marcus would draw on all three, but the part that touched his daily life most was ethics, and it rests on one idea that the Stoics put very simply.

Here is how Epictetus opens his short handbook, the Enchiridion, in the translation of George Long. He begins: "Of things some are in our power, and others are not." That is the whole foundation. Everything else is built on that one sentence.

So what is in our power? Epictetus lists it. In our power, he says, are opinion, movement toward a thing, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own acts. Put in plainer terms, this means how we judge things, what we choose to pursue, what we choose to avoid, and what we do.

And what is not in our power? He lists that too: the body, property, reputation, and offices. Our health, our money, what people think of us, and the positions we hold are, in his words, not our own acts. Mark how much he places on that second list. Almost everything people spend their lives chasing sits there.

Modern writers often call this the dichotomy of control, though that phrase is a modern label, not an ancient one. The Stoics did not use those words. They simply taught the distinction, again and again. It is a division of the world into two piles. What is up to us, and what is not.

Now, Epictetus does not stop at the list. He tells you what happens if you get the piles confused. If you think the things that are by nature slavish are free, and that what belongs to others is your own, he says, you will be hindered, you will lament, and you will be disturbed. In short, the trouble comes from reaching for what you cannot hold.

The other side follows. If you think only that which is your own to be your own, he says, no person will ever compel you. No one will hinder you, you will blame no one, and you will do nothing against your will. A person who has sorted the piles correctly cannot be pushed around by the world, because the world can only reach the things that were never theirs.

A thought like this can sound cold at first, so it helps to see how Epictetus brings it down to a single afternoon. He asks you to think of a jug, or a cup, that you love. When you grow fond of it, he says, remind yourself what it is, and then if it breaks, you will not be disturbed. He gives the same advice about a child or a wife. The point is not to love less. The point is to remember that these things are loaned, and not held by us as our own.

He also says that people are troubled not by things themselves, but by their opinions about those things. In Long's wording, it is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things. Death, he says, is nothing terrible, otherwise it would have seemed so to Socrates. The terror lies in the opinion that death is terrible. This is the heart of the method. Between the event and your reaction there is a small space, and in that space you can ask: is this really harmful, or is it only that I have judged it so?

Marcus would put the same idea in his own notebook, in his own way. In the Long translation of the Meditations he writes: "Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, I have been harmed." And he adds that if you take away the complaint, the harm is taken away. This is the same thought as Epictetus, coming from a man who held the highest office in the world.

Now we reach the image that gives this part its name. In the Meditations Marcus writes of the mind as a kind of fortress. In Long's translation he says that the mind free from passions is a citadel, for a man has nothing more secure to which he can fly for refuge and for the future be inexpugnable. The word inexpugnable simply means that it cannot be taken by force.

Marcus is careful about what he means. He is not saying that the walls of a citadel keep out bad news. He says the opposite: troubles will arrive, because they are part of life. What he means is that there is a part of you, your judgment and your choices, that no outside power can storm. Men, he notes elsewhere, seek retreats for themselves in the country, by the sea, in the hills. But it is in your power, he says, whenever you choose, to retire into yourself.

Casaubon, the earlier translator, gave the Meditations to English readers in sixteen thirty four, and his wording has an older, rougher ring. Yet the thought survives both versions, one from the age of King Charles and one from the age of Queen Victoria: that there is a place within where a person may go, and be quiet, and be free.

Alongside the sorting of piles, the Stoics taught a second large idea. They held that virtue is the only true good. By virtue they meant things like wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control. Health and wealth might be preferred, they said, and sickness and poverty avoided where possible, but none of these could make you a good person or a bad one. Only your character could do that.

It follows that a Stoic is not asked to give up the world. Marcus was an emperor, and he governed, judged cases, and led armies. The school asked him to do these things well, while keeping his peace of mind separate from their outcomes. He acts, but he does not make his happiness depend on the result.

One more piece completes the picture. The Stoics believed the universe is ordered and rational, and that human beings are meant to live in agreement with nature. Marcus returns to this often. Each person is part of a larger whole, and he thought it right to do one's share of the common work, calmly, for the sake of others.

So here is the school in plain terms. Sort what is yours from what is not. Hold the first firmly, and loosen your grip on the second. Remember that your judgments, not events, trouble you. Keep an inner citadel. And try, each day, to act well.

It all sounds simple when it is said slowly in the dark. It is not easy, and Marcus never claimed it was. His notebook is full of reminders to himself, as if he had to learn it afresh each morning. That raises a question. Where did a young prince, raised in a palace, ever come to hear a freed slave's lessons? Where did he learn it?

The Debts of Book One

On a night in a military camp, somewhere along the northern frontier, a man sits by a small lamp with a stylus and a stack of writing tablets, or perhaps a roll of papyrus. We do not know exactly which. What we know is that he is the emperor of Rome, and that before he writes anything about himself, he writes about other people.

The book he is writing has no title in the manuscripts that survive. Later readers called it the Meditations, and it has been known by that name ever since. And the first book of it is unlike anything that follows. It is a list of thanks.

You are in the story of Marcus Aurelius, and you have already met the boy called Verissimus, and heard what the Stoic school teaches. Now the story slows down to look at the notebook's opening pages, where an emperor counts what he owes.

The first book is made of short paragraphs, each one about a single person. Each begins with a name, or with a relation, and then gives a list of what Marcus learned from that person, or what he saw in them. In the old translation by George Long, the first entry reads, from his grandfather Verus, "good morals and the government of my temper." See what is being thanked. It is not wealth or rank. It is a temper held steady.

Some scholars think this book was written last, or added later, and a note at the end of the Greek text in the manuscripts places the writing of the first book among the Quadi, beside the river Gran. The river Gran runs through what is now Slovakia, and the Quadi were a people beyond the Danube. So the list of thanks may have been composed in a camp at war, far from the people it describes. That is worth holding in mind as we go. Some of the people he thanks were long dead by then.

His father, he says, he knew only by reputation, and by his memory. Long renders it that from his father's reputation and remembrance he learned "modesty and a manly character." Marcus's father had died when the boy was very young. So the thanks here are for a man he barely knew, and they are gentle, and brief.

Then comes his mother, Domitia Lucilla. From her he learned, in Long's words, "piety and beneficence, and abstinence, not only from evil deeds, but even from evil thoughts." He adds that she taught him simplicity in his way of living, far removed from the habits of the rich. It is a striking thing to read, because the household he grew up in was one of the richest in Rome. The lesson was to live plainly inside great wealth.

Next he thanks his great grandfather, who, he says, kept him from attending public schools and made sure he had good teachers at home. And Marcus adds a sentence worth pausing on, that a man should spend liberally on such things. He means that good teachers are worth their price.

Then the teachers themselves arrive, one after another, and here the list becomes a portrait of a mind being built.

From his governor, the man who looked after him as a boy, he learned not to take a side in the chariot races, nor to favour one kind of gladiator over another. This is a small matter, but it tells you something. Rome was mad for the games, and the boy was taught to stand a little apart from the crowd.

From a man called Diognetus he learned not to waste time on trifles, and not to be carried away by the tricks of wonder workers and charmers, who spoke of spells and of driving away spirits. It was Diognetus who also, Marcus says, encouraged him to write dialogues while he was still a boy, and to take up the rough bed and skin cover of the Greek philosophers. That last detail is vivid. The boy of a great house was persuaded to sleep on a hard bed, as the Greek philosophers did.

Then comes Rusticus, Quintus Junius Rusticus, and here the tone of the book changes, because the thanks grow longer and warmer. From Rusticus, Marcus says, he gained the idea that his character needed correction and training. He learned not to be led astray into zeal for rhetoric, not to write about speculative matters, and not to make speeches to persuade people to be good. Marcus the emperor, remember, was a man whom everyone expected to give speeches.

And then comes the gift that matters most for the story we are telling. Marcus writes that from Rusticus he received a copy of the Discourses of Epictetus, which Rusticus lent him out of his own library. This is the moment, as Marcus himself tells it, that the boy first met the Stoic teacher whose words would shape his life. We spoke of this reading in the first part. Here we see it from Marcus's own pen. It is not a grand scene. It is one man lending another man a book.

Rusticus also taught him to read carefully, and not to be satisfied with a superficial understanding of a book, nor to agree too quickly with those who talk a great deal. That is advice many readers could use tonight.

Now the list moves to Apollonius, a Stoic from Chalcedon, and the tone shifts again. Marcus says that from Apollonius he learned freedom of will, and undeviating steadiness of purpose, and to look to nothing else, even for a moment, except to reason. He saw in Apollonius a man who was, in Long's phrase, always the same, in sharp pains, on the occasion of the loss of a child, and in long illness. The same, in good days and in bad. That, for a Stoic, is the whole aim.

He adds a gentle observation, that Apollonius was a living example of a man who was both most earnest and most easy in his manner, and who did not seem to value his own skill in teaching as anything remarkable. And he thanks Apollonius for showing him how to receive favours from friends without losing his self respect, and without passing them by without notice.

The thanks go on. There is Sextus, from whom he learned kindness, and the pattern of a household governed in a fatherly way. There is Alexander the grammarian, who taught him to correct a person's error without insulting them. There is Fronto, the great orator, the man who tutored him in Latin eloquence, and from Fronto Marcus says he learned to notice the envy and the duplicity that come with power, and that those we call patricians are, for the most part, somewhat wanting in natural affection. It is an honest remark, and a little sharp. Even in a list of thanks, he does not flatter.

Marcus also thanks his brother, the gods, and then the emperor who adopted him, Antoninus Pius. Of Antoninus he writes at great length, and with plain love. He recalls his gentleness, his firmness in decisions once made after due thought, his freedom from vanity, and his habit of working steadily, never dropping a matter before it was fully examined. He remembers that Antoninus did not rush to dinner parties, and that he was easy to please. This is a portrait of the kind of ruler Marcus hoped to be.

Now stay with the shape of what he has done. Marcus has not written an account of battles. He has not listed the provinces he governs, or the honours he holds. At the opening of his private notebook he writes the names of those who made him, and beside each name he puts a quality, as if he were pressing leaves between pages.

There is a reason this fits the Stoic way. The school teaches that we are shaped by many hands, and that to see this clearly is to be less proud and more grateful. Marcus, who could have thought himself the author of his own greatness, begins instead by tracing it to others.

Notice too that the book never says who these thanks are for. Some readers have felt the list is a kind of preparation, a way of gathering himself before the harder pages that follow. It begins with gratitude, and only then turns to the hard business of living well.

The first book also ends with a short note of thanks to the gods, who gave him good grandparents, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good attendants, good kinsmen and friends, nearly everything good. It is a long breath of thanks, and then the book moves on.

So here is where the story stands. The emperor has paid his debts, at least on paper. He has named the people who gave him a steady temper, a love of reading, a plain way of living, and a teacher in the form of a borrowed book. The man at the lamp, in the cold camp, has remembered where he came from. What remains to ask is what he did with all of it, once the power was in his hands. How did he rule.

The Plague and the Frontier

At night, on the northern edge of the empire, somewhere along the Danube, a tent glows faintly, in the years when the emperor Marcus Aurelius lived more among soldiers than among senators. A lamp burns low. Outside, sentries change over, and the river moves in the dark. Inside, a man in his fifties sits with a tablet and writes, in Greek, a few lines for no one but himself. That is where this part of the story begins, and we will come to the notebook in a moment. First we have to understand what brought him there.

The trouble started in the east. In the year one hundred and sixty-one, Marcus became emperor, and he took his adoptive brother Lucius Verus as a joint ruler, which was a new thing in Roman history. Almost at once the Parthians attacked in the east, and Lucius was sent to command the war. The Roman armies did win. But the ancient sources tell us that when the troops came home, they brought something with them.

That something was a plague. The ancient writers place its spread through the empire in the later sixties of the second century, and it is named for the family of Marcus, the Antonines. The Roman historian Cassius Dio, writing a generation or so later, says that a pestilence in his own time was the greatest he knew of, and that two thousand people were dying in Rome in a single day. He is writing from memory and from report, so we should treat the number as a mark of how it felt, not as a count.

We do not know for certain what the disease was. The famous physician Galen lived through it and described a fever, a rash, and a long illness. Many modern scholars suspect smallpox, though that is an inference from his description, and the question is still argued. What we can say is that it was widespread, it was deadly, and it lasted for years. How many died is truly uncertain. Some estimates run very high, and others are far more cautious, and honest historians do not agree.

Think about what it meant to rule under such a shadow. The emperor could not cure anyone. He could not even keep the pestilence from the army. What he could do was keep the city functioning, and the sources give a few glimpses of him doing so. The later and less reliable Historia Augusta, a collection of imperial biographies written long after, says that Marcus gave great attention to the burial of the dead, and that he allowed even poorer people to be buried at public expense. That book mixes truth with invention, so we hold it a little loosely. Still, the picture fits the man who appears in his own notebook.

Then the second blow fell. Even while the plague was still moving, trouble came from the north. Peoples beyond the Danube, among them the Marcomanni and the Quadi, pushed across the frontier, and the war that followed is called the Marcomannic wars. Lucius Verus died in the year one hundred and sixty-nine, and Marcus was left to rule alone. From that time he spent long years on the northern frontier, with the army, far from Rome.

So the man we meet in the tent is not a philosopher in a quiet garden. He is a commander, and a tired one. The Historia Augusta says that the wars were so pressing that he sold imperial treasures at an auction in the forum to raise money, rather than lay a new burden on the provinces. Again, that source is late, and we should say so. But it is a story that Romans told about him, and it tells us what kind of ruler they remembered.

Now we can look at the tent again. The notebook that we call the Meditations carries two small notes that tell us where parts of it were written. At the end of the first book, the text says, in the old translation of George Long, that it was written among the Quadi, on the river Gran. At the end of the second book it says, in Long's English, that it was written at Carnuntum. Carnuntum was a Roman military town on the Danube, in what is now Austria, and it served as a headquarters in these wars. Those two lines are among the few facts we have about where Marcus wrote. They suggest that the notebook grew in the field, not in a study.

We should be careful here, though. The notes do not tell us when each passage was written, and they do not prove the whole book came from the camps. Scholars treat the notes as reasonable evidence for some of the writing, and they are cautious about the rest. A little while ago I said much of the notebook was written here. That is the usual view, but it rests on a very small amount of text.

What did the days look like? We can only guess at the detail, and we should say that we are guessing. There would have been councils, and reports from scouts, and letters from the capital. There would have been the dull, hard business of grain, and pay, and winter quarters. Cassius Dio describes the war as long and costly, with Romans at times in real danger. A famous scene on the great column in Rome, set up in his honour after his death, shows the campaigns in carved stone, and the figures there are soldiers and prisoners and officers, not philosophers.

Yet the notebook is not about the war. It rarely names a battle. That strikes many readers as strange, and it is one of the most interesting things about it. A man at the head of an army, in a plague, writes almost nothing about the army or the plague. He writes instead about how to behave. He writes about getting up in the morning, about people who annoy him, about how short life is. The war is the weather in which he thinks, and not the subject of his thinking.

There are a few places where the frontier does seep in. In one passage, in Long's translation, he writes of how a spider is proud when it has caught a fly, and one man when he has caught a hare, another when he has taken a boar, another when he has taken Sarmatians. It is a small, dry joke, and it is aimed at the very thing he is doing. The Sarmatians were a people living beyond the Danube, and Marcus fought them. He sets the conquest of a people beside a spider and a fly, to keep it in proportion.

Another passage is blunter still. He reminds himself, in Long's wording, that Alexander and Caesar and Pompeius, so often masters of cities and armies, are gone, and that the same fate waits for him. That is a thought one might have at night, in a tent, after a day of command, when the noise has stopped and the lamp is low.

There is also the matter of illness. The plague killed many, and Marcus's own household was not spared. The sources tell us that he buried many of his children, and that only a few of them outlived him. It is hard to read the notebook without that fact in mind, though he rarely speaks of it directly. In one place, in Long's English, he gives himself a quiet instruction about a child, that he should not pray to lose a child, but pray rather not to fear to lose him. The idea is not cold. It is a way of loving a thing without clenching around it.

We need to be honest about how much we really know of his inner life. We have his notebook, and we have what others wrote about him, mostly in later years. We do not have a diary that says what he felt on a given night. The notebook is not a record of events. It is a record of thoughts, set down without dates, and arranged in no clear order. That is part of why readers can use it so freely, and part of why historians must be careful not to read too much into any one line.

Still, one thing stands out. The Stoic teachers he admired had told him that the mind is a place one can go back to, even when the outer world is a mess. He had read Epictetus, as we have heard, and Epictetus had said that some things are up to us and some are not. A man in the middle of a plague and a war has plenty of evidence for the second list. The notebook is, in a way, his daily effort to stay inside the first.

So we leave him there, in the tent on the Danube, with the cold coming in under the canvas and the river going by in the dark. The wars are not over, and the plague has not finished. He sets down his tablet for the night. And we are left with a plain, practical question, the one we will take up next: what does this notebook actually sound like?

Notes to Himself

Late in the evening on the Danube frontier, the camp has gone quiet, and one lamp is still burning low. A man in middle age sits on a camp stool with a wax tablet or a roll of papyrus on his knee, and he writes a few lines in Greek. Then he stops. He does not polish the lines or arrange them for a reader. He simply puts the pen down, and tomorrow he will write something else.

This is the picture that sits behind the book we call the Meditations. In this part of the story, we look at how that book is built, and why it is better understood as a practice than as a book at all.

Begin with the name. The title Meditations is not the author's. The oldest surviving manuscripts give no such title, and the earliest reference to the work, from a Byzantine scholar named Arethas, calls it something like "the writings to himself." The Greek phrase used in the later manuscript tradition is "ta eis heauton," which means "the things to himself." That is a plain, almost shy, description. It tells you the audience was one person.

Now notice something odd about the book as we have it. It comes to us in twelve books, but these are not chapters in the way a modern author would write them. They are collections of entries, some a single sentence, some a few paragraphs, set down one after another with little visible plan. Scholars have long noted that the first book is different from the rest, since it is a list of thanks to those who shaped him, which the last part touched on. After that, the entries mostly drift from theme to theme, return to old themes, and repeat themselves.

The repetition is the first clue to how the thing works. An author writing for readers avoids saying the same thing twice. A man writing to himself does the opposite. He says it again, because he has forgotten it again, or because he did not quite believe it the first time. Marcus returns again and again to a handful of ideas: that death is natural, that other people's faults are not his harm, that fame is brief, that the matter in front of him is the only matter he has.

Consider how he begins a day. In the second book, he gives himself an instruction. In George Long's translation, it reads: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." This is a rehearsal. He is standing at the door of his day and picturing, in advance, the people he will have to deal with. He does not do it to feel gloomy. He does it so that no one will surprise him.

And he gives his reason in the same passage. These people are like this, he says, because they do not know what is good and what is evil. So he cannot be harmed by them, and he cannot be angry with them, for they are his kin, made for the same common work. Notice the shape of the thing. It is a small drill, run in the morning, to set the mind before the day can disturb it.

If you lie still and listen, you can hear that this is not how most of us think of philosophy. We imagine a thinker building an argument, step by step, to prove a conclusion. Marcus does little of that. He has already accepted the Stoic conclusions, which you met in the second part. What he does is remind himself of them, in short and usable forms, so that they are in his hand when he needs them.

The forms are worth naming. One is the aphorism, a sentence small enough to carry. Another is the reminder, an instruction addressed to himself, often in the plain command form. A third is the image, a picture meant to loosen a hold. And a fourth is the question he puts to his own mind, such as asking what a thing is, in itself, stripped of the story he tells about it.

Take the aphorism. In the fourth book he writes, in Long's rendering: "Thou hast power over thy opinions." That is a short line, but it contains a whole programme. Events are not in your power, yet your judgment of events is. Elsewhere he puts it in the form of a test for any trouble that arrives: if a thing outside him distresses him, he says, it is not the thing itself but his judgment about it that troubles him, and that judgment he has the power to wipe out at once.

Now take the reminder. He tells himself, in the fifth book, to rise with a clear sense of purpose. The passage is famous. When you are unwilling to get out of bed in the morning, he says, let this thought be present: "I am rising to do the work of a human being." He then asks himself whether he was made to lie under blankets and keep warm. It is a gentle scolding, the kind a man gives himself when no one is listening, and it has the quality that makes the whole notebook feel alive. Here is an emperor of Rome, master of an army and an empire, arguing with his own reluctance to leave a warm bed.

You may have noticed how rarely he writes about public events. He mentions a war and a plague only in passing, if at all. The notebook is not a diary in our sense. There is no account of battles won, no record of decisions taken. Where an event does appear, it is usually as an example, a thing to be seen rightly, and not as a story to be told. This is part of why the book can be read by anyone, anywhere. It strips away the particular so that the lesson can stand.

So where did he learn to write this way? Here we return to a teacher we met earlier. Epictetus, the lame former slave whose lessons Marcus first read in his youth, had taught that a philosopher must practise. In the Enchiridion, the short handbook assembled from his lessons, he tells his student to have the principles ready at hand, and to meet each impression with a prepared response. In Long's translation of the Discourses, Epictetus insists that a man must exercise himself daily, and not merely talk about the doctrine. Marcus took this literally. His notebook is the exercise.

There is a useful way to see this. Think of an athlete who does not read about running but runs, day after day, until the movement is easy. The Stoics thought of virtue in a similar way, as a skill that needed training. Marcus writes as if his own mind were a muscle that slackened if he did not work it. And the writing itself was the training. Putting a thought into words, he seems to say, makes it firmer.

Notice, too, the odd honesty of the book. Because he wrote for himself, he did not pretend to be wise. He writes often as one who has fallen short. He scolds himself, urges himself on, and reminds himself that his time is running out. In the second book, he tells himself that the time allotted is limited, and that if he does not use it to clear the clouds from his mind, it will be gone, and he will be gone with it. A man who thought himself finished would not write so.

We should be careful here, and honest about what we do not know. We do not know exactly when each entry was written, or in what order. Two notes within the text itself attach places to the work. At the end of the first book, a note in the manuscripts says it was written among the Quadi, on the river Gran, and at the end of the second book comes a note saying it was written at Carnuntum. Those are real places on the northern frontier, which fits the picture of a campaigning emperor. But the notes may have been added by a later hand, and scholars treat them with some caution. So we picture the tent, but we hold the picture loosely.

We also do not know whether he meant the notebook to survive. The more likely view is that he did not. Nothing in the text addresses a reader, and its whole manner is private. That it came down to us at all is a small accident of history, one the final part of this programme will return to.

What we can say is that the later writers who mention Marcus describe him in ways that fit. The Roman historian Cassius Dio, writing a generation or so after his death, praises him as a man who tried to live by the philosophy he had learned and who was devoted to the work of ruling. The late Roman collection known as the Historia Augusta, which must be used with care because it is uneven and sometimes unreliable, also says that he gave himself to philosophy from a young age. Neither source mentions the notebook as we know it. The Meditations speaks for itself.

So here is how to hold the book in mind. It is not a treatise, and it is not a memoir. It is a set of exercises, written by a busy man in the margins of a hard life, each one a small act of returning. A thought arrives, he tests it, he sets it down, and he comes back to it tomorrow. If you read a page and find it plain, even repetitive, that is a sign you are reading it as he wrote it, as a thing to be used and not admired.

And that raises a question that this part has only circled. We have seen the form of the exercises, the morning rehearsal, the aphorism, the reminder, the image. We have not yet said what they are for, or what the whole of the daily work adds up to, or how an ordinary person might take it up. What is the practice.

The View From Above

It is evening in a camp on the Danube, and a lamp is burning low in a tent. The emperor has finished the business of the day. He has heard petitions, read dispatches, and listened to officers describe the state of a river crossing. Now he sits alone, and he does what he has done on many such nights. He takes up his writing and begins to look at the world from very far above.

This is the sixth part of our story of Marcus Aurelius and his notebook. We have followed the boy, the school he learned from, the thanks he owed his teachers, the plague, and the long wars on the frontier. Tonight we turn to one of the most striking habits in the notebook, a trick of the imagination that he returned to again and again.

The trick is this. He asks himself to rise. He asks his mind to lift off the ground, higher than the camp, higher than the river, until the whole scene of human life lies small beneath it.

He writes the instruction plainly. In the translation of George Long, he tells himself to look down from above on the countless herds of men, their countless solemnities, their voyages in storms and calms, and the differences among those who are born, live together, and die. He asks himself to consider too the life lived by others in times long before him, and the life that will be lived after him, among barbarous nations.

Mark what he is doing. The emperor of Rome, the man at the centre of the largest state in the western world, is deliberately making himself small. He is not saying that Rome does not matter. He is practising a kind of calm, by placing his worries beside the sheer size of time and space.

The Greek behind this idea has a long history, and Marcus knew it. The Stoics before him had taught that a person should try to see the world as a whole, as one great city shared by gods and men. Epictetus, the lame former slave whose lessons shaped Marcus so deeply, speaks of the world as a single community. Marcus himself writes that the city he belongs to, as Antoninus, is Rome, but as a man it is the world. The view from above is a way of feeling that larger belonging.

Now listen to what it does to his sense of time. Elsewhere he writes that the whole of the present is a tiny point. He says that a man lives only in the present moment, and that all the rest is either already lived or uncertain. Then he adds that the time any man lives is a little thing, and little too is the corner of the earth where he lives.

So the height gives him two measures at once. Space shrinks, and time shrinks. And once both have shrunk, something strange happens to the thing most men and emperors crave, which is fame.

Marcus has a great deal to say about fame, and none of it is kind. He knew how it worked. He had grown up in a city of statues and inscriptions, where men paid to be remembered. His own adoptive family had its faces on coins across the empire. And yet he writes, with a cool eye, that the time before oblivion takes us all is very short.

He goes further. In Long's translation he says that he who has a vehement desire for posthumous fame does not consider that every one of those who remember him will himself die very soon, and so in turn will he who has succeeded the first. The chain of remembering is a chain of mortals, each one passing the memory on to someone who will also vanish. Fame, in his picture, is a torch handed along a line of people who are all about to go out.

There is a gentle humour in how he says it. He asks what, after all, is everlasting remembrance, and answers that it is altogether vanity. Then he asks what it is, then, about which we ought to employ our serious pains, and gives his answer in a single line. It is this, that the thoughts should be just, the acts social, the speech such as can be trusted, and the disposition ready to welcome whatever happens.

That small list is the point of the whole exercise. The view from above does not leave him with nothing. It strips away what is loud and leaves what is quiet and firm. Be fair in thought, useful in action, truthful in speech, and calm toward events.

Now we should be honest about where this comes from, because it matters for how we read him. The idea of the view from above is not unique to Marcus. A version of it appears in earlier writers, and scholars have traced the habit of looking down on the earth from the sky through several schools of ancient thought. What is distinctive about Marcus is how he uses it, as a daily tool and not as a grand theory. He is not writing a treatise on the heavens. He is talking himself down from irritation.

And he gives us some wonderfully specific examples of where he points his gaze. He tells himself to run over in his mind the great figures of the past. He names the age of Vespasian, and sees men marrying, bringing up children, falling ill, dying, making war, keeping festivals, trading, farming, flattering, and suspecting. He names the age of Trajan, and finds just the same. Then he says that those whole lives are gone, and that nothing remains of them.

Here we come to the second great theme of this part, which is change. Having shown himself how small and brief everything is, Marcus turns to the way everything moves. In the notebook he says that the universe is change, and that life is opinion. It is one of the shortest and most compressed sentences he ever wrote, and it has been quoted and argued over ever since.

He is not mourning this. For a Stoic, change is not a defect in the world. It is the world's own working. Marcus compares the universe to a thing that is always remaking itself. He writes that the nature of the universe loves to change the things that are, and to make new things like them. Everything that exists is, in a manner, the seed of what will come after it.

He puts the same thought in an image of a river. He speaks of time as a river made up of events, a violent stream, in which each thing is no sooner seen than it is carried past, and another thing takes its place, and that too will be swept away. If you stand on the bank and watch it for a moment, you may feel dizzy. Marcus asks you to feel instead a kind of steadiness, because the river was always going to flow.

Think what this must have meant to a man writing in a plague year. Around him the old were dying, and the young, and soldiers, and slaves, and friends. The very scale of loss in his empire was a lesson in change that he could not avoid. He lost many of his own children, and the sources tell us that only a few of them outlived him. When he writes that things pass away, he is not being clever. He is a father and an emperor trying to hold his mind steady in the middle of grief.

And so he gives himself a rule for how to think of any beloved thing. In the pages that echo Epictetus, the lesson is to remember, when you kiss your child, that it is mortal. It sounds hard, almost cold. But the intention is not to love less. It is to love with open eyes, so that nothing can ever feel like a theft.

There is a last use he makes of the height, and it is perhaps the most practical. He uses it on his own anger and his own vanity. If a man is puffed up by some small honour, the view from above shows him how small the honour is. If a man is stung by an insult, the view shows him how short the insult will live. Marcus writes of the whole earth as a point, and of the greatest part of that point as inhabited by a few. From that distance, the quarrel of an afternoon in a tent hardly registers.

Yet he does not leave us floating in the sky. This is the quiet wisdom of the exercise. He rises so that he can come back down. After the great survey of the ages, he returns to the present task. He tells himself, as he does so often, to do the work that lies in front of him, now, with seriousness and kindness.

The lamp in the tent is smaller now. Outside, the river goes on, and the frontier fires burn along its bank. He has looked at all the herds of men, and the long dead, and the long unborn, and he has seen how briefly any of it holds. Tomorrow will bring another council, and another report, and certainly some difficult person to be borne with patience.

So the story turns toward that next problem, because a calm mind is tested not by the stars but by the people who stand in front of us. How does a man who has seen the whole of time still find the patience to face one hard person across a table? That is where Marcus goes next.

Dealing With Difficult People

In the dark before dawn, on the Danube, a man sits up on his bed with a lamp burning low beside him. He is the emperor of Rome, and the camp around him is waking in the cold. Soon the officers will come, and the petitioners, and the men who must be dealt with. Before any of them arrive, he takes up his stylus and writes a few lines to himself in Greek.

We are in the seventh part of our story. We have walked with Marcus from his boyhood through the school of the Stoics, and we have stood with him on the frontier. Tonight we look at one of the plainest and most useful habits in his notebook: how he prepared himself, each morning, for the people he would meet.

The passage is among the best known in the whole book. In the translation of George Long, it begins: "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial." See how little hope there is in it, and how little bitterness. He is not complaining. He is making a list, the way a traveller checks the weather before setting out.

Then comes the reason, which is the heart of the matter. Long renders it this way: "All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil." Marcus believed, as the Stoics did, that people do wrong because they are mistaken about what is truly good. A man who lies, or sneers, or cheats, has judged badly about what matters. He is more to be corrected than hated.

He goes further. He says he has looked at the nature of the one who does wrong, and found that it is akin to his own. Long's version says that such a person is "of the same blood and birth, not the same seed, but the same mind, and possessing a portion of the divine." The man who offends you is a relation. He is a fellow creature made for the same work.

And so the passage ends on a thought that sounds almost like a promise. Long gives it: "I can neither be harmed by any of them, for no man can involve me in anything inappropriate." Here the lesson from the earlier parts returns. What other people do is outside Marcus's control. His judgment about it is inside it.

The old translator Meric Casaubon, working in sixteen thirty-four, gives the same teaching in his own seventeenth century English. His sentences are longer and a little stiff, but the sense is the same. The morning list is not a bad mood. It is a rehearsal.

Why would an emperor need such a rehearsal? Think of how his days were spent. A Roman emperor sat in judgment, received embassies, and heard complaints from every corner of an empire. The later Roman historian Cassius Dio, writing a generation afterwards, admired Marcus for how he bore the burden of rule. Dio says that Marcus did not grow harsh with the pressure of it, and he praises the emperor for the gentleness of his manner. The notebook gives us a glimpse of the labour behind that gentleness.

There was also a teacher behind the habit. Marcus had read Epictetus, the former slave who taught that people are disturbed not by things but by the judgments they form about things. In the little handbook called the Enchiridion, in Long's translation, Epictetus advises a person who is about to bathe to remind himself beforehand what happens at the baths, that some splash, some shove, some steal. Then, he says, you will go about the thing with a steadier mind. Marcus took this very counsel and widened it from the bathhouse to the whole of public life.

Now we come to anger, which Marcus treats with real attention. He does not pretend that he feels none. He writes as a man who knows the heat of it, and who is trying to cool it. One of his arguments is practical, and it is a favourite of the sleepless listener. He observes that anger and grief do more harm to us than the acts that cause them. Long's version reads: "How much more grievous are the consequences of anger than the causes of it." A rude word lasts a moment. A grudge can last a year.

He also reminds himself how short everything is. In another place he tells himself that soon both he and the man who wronged him will be dead, and that in a little while even their names will be gone. Hold that thought for a moment, because it softens a quarrel. Two men shouting in a tent, and both of them already walking toward the same quiet.

Then there is a line about gentleness that Marcus plainly wants to remember. He writes that kindness is invincible, if it is genuine and not a smirk or an act. The question he puts to himself is simple. What can the most insolent man do to you, if you keep on being kind to him? Nothing, Marcus says, and he adds that if the occasion offers, you may gently set him right, calmly and without ill will. There is no surrender in that. He is allowed to correct people. He is only forbidden to burn.

Now the other half of the subject, which is duty. A man who expected rudeness and forgave it could still sink into idleness, and Marcus knew the danger. He writes about the struggle to rise from bed on a cold morning. Long translates it: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being." The picture is of a man who would rather stay under the blankets. He talks himself up and out by remembering his purpose.

Part of that purpose is cooperation. He compares people to the parts of a single body, to feet and hands, to rows of upper and lower teeth. We were born to work together, he says, and to act against one another is contrary to nature. When a colleague is tiresome, then, the Stoic does not withdraw in disgust. He keeps doing his share, because the work was never done for the colleague's approval.

Marcus also knows how easily an ordinary person, even a good one, can start to judge others from a height. So he turns the lens round. He asks himself whether he has not done something of the same kind. He writes that when you are offended by another's fault, you should turn at once to your own, and consider whether you have the same fault, or one very like it. It is a humbling little habit, and a useful one.

There is a plain kindness in this, and it reaches the ones nearest to him. In the first book, which we visited earlier, he thanks his teachers and family for patience and example. Among the things he credits his adoptive father Antoninus with was a calm, even temper, a man who could hear a matter through without losing his composure. Marcus does not claim to have inherited it. He claims to be working at it, day by day, on the pages of this notebook.

We should be honest, too, about what the notebook can and cannot tell us. It was written for himself, and it does not record each quarrel or name each difficult man. We do not know which senator or general lay behind any given line. We know only the method he set himself, and the tone in which he set it down. The later biography called the Historia Augusta describes him as patient with those around him, and as slow to anger. But that work is late, and it mixes sound report with invention, so we should hold its praise lightly. The notebook is the better witness, because it shows a man still trying.

So the morning ritual is, in the end, a small exercise in realism and in mercy together. Expect the world as it is. Do not be surprised by the rude, the vain, or the ungrateful. Remember that they err from ignorance, and that they are your kin. Guard your own judgment, keep your own work, and let your kindness go on unbroken through the long day.

Now the lamp is nearly out, and the grey light is coming over the river. The first footsteps sound outside the tent, and the emperor sets down his stylus and gets up to meet whoever has come. He has made his list and he is ready for it. But there is another visitor that no list can turn away, one who comes to emperor and servant alike, and of whom Marcus thinks more often than of any rude man. What about death.

Death and the Last Campaign

In the winter of the year one hundred and eighty, in a military camp on the Danube, an old emperor lay ill and tired, and the men around him watched his face for signs. The camp stood near a place called Vindobona, which is the city we now call Vienna. The emperor was Marcus Aurelius. He was fifty-eight years old, and he had spent much of the last decade away from Rome, fighting the tribes beyond the river.

You have followed him a long way by now, from the boy called Verissimus, through the schools and the plague and the frontier camps. Tonight the story reaches its end, and with it the question that runs through his whole notebook: what does a man do with the fact that he will die?

He had been thinking about it for years. The Meditations returns to death again and again, and it never raises its voice. In one passage he asks what death is, and answers that it is a thing of nature, no different from growth or old age. In the translation of George Long, he writes that death is such as generation is, a mystery of nature, a composition out of the same elements and a decomposition into the same. There is nothing dramatic in the tone. He talks about it the way a gardener talks about the turn of a season.

He also tries to take the fear apart, piece by piece. He tells himself that a man should not despise death, but be content with it, since it is one of the things which nature wills. Then he does something quietly practical. He notes that the people who seem to cling to life most fiercely have often buried many others first. He writes that even those who were once at the bedside of the dying have themselves been carried out.

Hold that picture for a moment. The emperor, in his tent, writing down the names of the dead. Elsewhere in the notebook he runs through famous men of the past and shows how all of them are gone. The great doctors who frowned over so many sickbeds are dead, and the astrologers who foretold the deaths of others are dead too.

You might expect such thoughts to make a man cold. They did not seem to. He writes that death should be met cheerfully, and he offers a small comfort drawn from his own school: that a person should wait for the end with a calm mind, as one waits for a ripe olive to fall from the tree. In Long's wording, the fruit falls blessing nature that produced it and thanking the tree that gave it life.

Now come back to the camp near Vindobona. The wars were not finished. The northern peoples, the Marcomanni and the Quadi, had been a trouble for years, and Marcus had planned to push further. The ancient writers say that he meant to carry the campaign on and make new provinces beyond the river. Whether that was truly his plan, or a story told afterward, is not something the sources let us settle with certainty. Cassius Dio, who was writing a generation later, believed it.

Then the illness came. We do not know what it was. Some later writers have guessed that it was the plague that had swept the empire, but the ancient accounts do not name it, and a guess is only a guess. The Historia Augusta says that he fell sick and called his friends to him. That same source adds a detail that has often been repeated, that he refused food and drink for some days, as if to hasten the end. Historians treat the Historia Augusta with care, because it is a late and sometimes unreliable work, so this detail should be held loosely.

What the sources agree on is the weight of the next question: who would follow him? His son Commodus was about eighteen. The boy was with him in the camp. Marcus had made him a junior partner in rule some years before, so there was no struggle over the succession, and no sword was needed.

There is a sadness in this that the ancient writers feel too. Marcus had lived a long time under the idea that emperors should choose the best man, and that was how he himself had come to power, through adoption by those who came before. But he had a son of his own, and he did not choose another. Later readers have wondered whether he saw what kind of ruler the young man would be. Cassius Dio, looking back, called the change from his reign to the next one a fall from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust. That is the judgment of a historian who knew how it ended, and it should be read that way.

Still, the old emperor had left him advice, if we trust the accounts. The Historia Augusta says that he urged his friends to look after the boy. And one famous story says that when the tribune came to ask for the watchword that night, Marcus gave this answer: go to the rising sun, for I am already setting. The tale comes down to us from a source we cannot fully rely on, but it is a fitting thing to hear, and it may be only a legend.

He died on the seventeenth of March, in the year one hundred and eighty. The ancient sources give his death at Vindobona or at a place called Sirmium, and they do not agree. The matter is not settled, and it hardly changes the shape of the story. What is certain is that the empire mourned him, and that he was honored as a god by the senate, as emperors were.

Think now of the man himself, in those last days. There is no scene in the notebook of his dying. The Meditations breaks off without a farewell, because it was never meant as a book at all. It was a set of reminders written to himself. But one note near the end seems to speak for the whole. He writes that a man should leave life with a quiet mind, and he compares it to an actor leaving the stage when the play is over. In Long's phrase, you have lived as a citizen in a great city, and it makes no difference whether for five years or a hundred, since the law of the city is equal for all.

And so he went, the way he had hoped to go, without complaint. What he left behind was a small, plain notebook in Greek, written in camps and tents in the dark, never shown to anyone. The man who wrote it was gone, but the pages were not.

What did that little notebook become, once the emperor's hand was gone from it?

A Book That Survived

Somewhere in the lands of the Eastern Roman Empire, perhaps a thousand years after the emperor's death, a scribe sat with a lamp and a sheet of parchment and copied out a short Greek work whose author had never given it a name. The scribe did not know it as a famous book. He knew it as a small collection of sayings, and he wrote it out, line by line, in the quiet that comes after dark.

That is how this story ends, with copying, and with the long chain of hands that carried a private notebook out of a Roman army camp and into the present. You have followed the emperor Marcus Aurelius from a boy called Verissimus to a sick man near Vindobona. Now the notebook itself takes over the story.

Begin with the oddest fact about it. The emperor did not call it Meditations. The Greek title it carries today means something like "to himself," and even that seems to have come later, from readers and copyists. He wrote in Greek, the language he used for philosophy, though he was a Roman and ruled in Latin.

There is no sign that he ever planned to publish it. The pages read like a man talking quietly to himself, reminding himself of things he already knew and kept forgetting. Yet it survived, and by a surprisingly thin thread.

For some centuries after his death, we hear of the book only faintly. A rhetorician named Themistius, writing in the fourth century, mentions that the emperor had left behind writings addressed to himself. A little later, a Greek historian called Aristophanes of Byzantium has no part in this, so set that name aside. What matters is that a few late writers knew the work existed, and quoted from it now and then, which tells us copies were passing quietly from reader to reader.

The real hinge is the tenth century. A Byzantine scholar named Arethas, who became bishop of Caesarea, owned a manuscript of the book and wrote in the margin of one of his own works that he had had a copy made of the emperor's writings, which had become old and worn. That small remark, made by a man tidying his library, is among the earliest solid evidence of the book's survival.

Think about what that means. The text had by then lasted around eight centuries as a private object, handed on without any grand plan. Nobody ordered it preserved. People simply found it worth the cost of parchment and ink.

Then the long silence again. No single complete manuscript from the Byzantine period survives today in the form Arethas would have known. The book reaches the modern world through a few later Greek manuscripts, and scholars have worked out how they relate to one another. One of them, which was lost, is known only because earlier scholars described and printed from it.

So the thread was thin, and it nearly snapped more than once. If the right monk had been less careful, or a library had burned a decade earlier, we might know the emperor only from his enemies and his biographers.

Now the book steps into print. In the year fifteen fifty nine, a Swiss scholar named Wilhelm Xylander published the first printed edition of the Greek text, in Zurich, along with a Latin translation. That is the moment the private notebook becomes a public book. The reader in a study in Switzerland and the soldier in a tent on the Danube were now, strangely, holding the same sentences.

The first English translation followed in the early sixteen hundreds, and then the version by Meric Casaubon, published in sixteen thirty four. Casaubon was a scholar and a clergyman, and his English has the weight and slow music of his age. Many readers of that century met the emperor through him.

A later and more famous English rendering came from George Long, whose translation appeared in eighteen sixty two. Long wrote plainly, and his version became the standard for generations of readers, partly because it was clear and partly because it was freely available. It is one of the two translations this programme has drawn on.

And so the book travelled through the centuries, picking up readers who could hardly have been more different from one another. Kings and soldiers were among them. Frederick the Great, king of Prussia, is said to have carried a copy of the emperor's work, and the tradition is old, though the details are less sure than the legend suggests. In the nineteenth century the poet and critic Matthew Arnold wrote an essay on Marcus Aurelius, praising him as a man who sought moral strength in a world of uncertain belief. In more recent times, soldiers, politicians and people in hospital waiting rooms have all kept it close.

Why would such different people want it? Perhaps because it asks for nothing grand. It does not demand that you believe in a creed. It asks only that you look again at your own mind, and do the next honest thing.

Here the story should be fair to the emperor, and to you. The book has limits, and he would not have minded hearing them named.

First, it is not a system laid out for students. It is a set of notes, repeated and reworked, and the same few ideas return many times. Readers who expect a tidy argument sometimes find it frustrating, and they are right that it is not one.

Second, the Stoic claim is narrower than people often think. It does not say that pain is an illusion, or that you should feel nothing. Epictetus, the teacher whose lessons shaped Marcus, taught that some things are up to us and some are not, and that our judgments about events are what we can govern. That is a claim about where to place your effort. It is not a promise that life will be gentle.

Third, the emperor himself did not always live up to it. The notebook is full of reminders precisely because he needed them. A man who has mastered a thing does not write himself the same note again and again. The book is a record of effort, not of arrival.

Fourth, there are things Stoicism cannot do for you. Marcus watched a plague move through his empire, and he buried children. Philosophy did not undo these losses. At best it gave him a way to stand inside them without being swept away. That is a real thing, but it is not a cure.

And fifth, his reign was not a model of gentleness. Cassius Dio, who lived through the years just after, records that Marcus was a conscientious ruler who nonetheless fought long and hard wars on the frontier. The same hand that wrote about kindness signed orders for armies. The honest reader holds both facts and does not tidy either away.

Dio, a senator and historian writing in Greek, also tells us how the world felt on losing him. He marks the death of Marcus as the point where Rome passed from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust. That is a historian's judgment, a little sad and a little dramatic, and it shows how much his contemporaries thought had changed.

The Historia Augusta, a later and less reliable collection of imperial lives, adds its own warmth. It describes his seriousness, his habit of study, and his simple dress. We should handle it with care, since parts of that work are known to be unreliable, but its portrait of a plain, dutiful man matches what the notebook itself suggests.

So here is what survives. A man wrote to himself on cold nights, in a language not his first, with no thought of being read. Copyists kept the pages alive. A bishop remarked on it in a margin. A scholar in Zurich put it in print. Translators in England gave it a voice. And ordinary people, century after century, have opened it when they were tired, or frightened, or simply trying to be a little better than the day before.

There is something quietly hopeful in that. The things that last are not always the loudest. A set of private reminders, written by someone who doubted himself, carried more weight than many monuments.
